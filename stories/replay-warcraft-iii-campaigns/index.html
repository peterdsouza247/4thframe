Before Forsaken Kingdom, Replay Warcraft III's Original Campaigns

Azeroth's history is easy to summarise. Warcraft III makes you live through the decisions that created it.

By Peter D’Souza
WARCRAFT III
Fourth Frame
Games / Campaigns / 04

You can learn the broad outline of Warcraft III in a few minutes: Lordaeron falls, Arthas becomes the thing he set out to destroy, old enemies unite against a larger threat, and the survivors build a different future. That summary gives you the facts. It cannot give you the feeling of starting a mission with too few troops, watching a familiar kingdom turn hostile, or having to keep a fragile alliance alive long enough to reach the end.

With Warcraft III Reforged: Forsaken Kingdom returning to the ruins of Lordaeron, the original campaigns are worth more than a lore recap. They are still a rewarding way to experience how this world was made. I have not played the new campaign, and this is not a review of it. It is a case for going back through Reign of Chaos and The Frozen Throne before moving forward.

You watch a world change from several sides

The campaigns do something that a timeline cannot. They move you between peoples who understand the same crisis differently. Lordaeron's fall is a human catastrophe, an undead conquest, and the beginning of another faction's struggle to survive. By the time the story carries you across the sea, the orcs and night elves are not simply new armies to unlock. They have their own histories, fears and reasons to mistrust the others.

Playing each side matters. A change of faction alters what you can build, what you can afford to lose and how you solve a battle. Human workers build and repair a settlement; undead structures spread their own territory; night elf buildings are living things that can uproot and move. These mechanics do not explain every belief a culture holds. They give its identity a physical presence on the map.

That is one of Warcraft III's great strengths as worldbuilding. Azeroth grows through encounters rather than an encyclopedia entry. Places become meaningful because you have defended them, invaded them or arrived too late to save them. Returning to Lordaeron in a new campaign has a different weight when you remember how it felt before it became a ruin.

The missions ask more than “destroy the enemy base”

The old campaigns are not a continuous run of conventional RTS matches. They change the pressure on you. At times you are developing an army and deciding when to attack. Elsewhere you are escorting, surviving, exploring or racing against an enemy's progress. The objective changes the story's pace and, with it, your relationship to the character you control.

The culling of Stratholme is the obvious example. Its horror is not confined to the dialogue around Arthas's decision. The mission makes his decision the work you must perform to advance. It is an uncomfortable use of player agency: you do not choose whether he crosses that line, but you carry out the objective as the person he has become. You can understand why he thinks he has no alternative and still recoil from what he does.

Other missions find their tension in holding out rather than conquering. That variety keeps the campaigns from feeling like story scenes attached to a skirmish mode. The rules of each encounter help tell you what kind of moment this is: a desperate defence, a pursuit, an expedition, or a victory that no longer feels like one.

Some of those missions show their age. Pathfinding can turn a clean plan into a muddle, and a long base-building stretch can interrupt the urgency of the plot. Replaying them is not an exercise in pretending those frustrations vanished. It is a chance to see how often the campaigns' ideas still come through despite them.

Characters earn their place in the world

I love a good fantasy setting, but an enormous history is not enough to make me care about it. Warcraft III gives its conflicts faces: Arthas, Jaina, Thrall, Tyrande, Illidan, Sylvanas and others whose choices send consequences far beyond a single mission. They disagree, misjudge one another and sometimes discover too late what their victories cost.

The series that followed has added years of lore and revised how some of those figures are remembered. Replaying the campaigns lets you encounter them at the point when their futures are still uncertain. Arthas is not yet a completed legend. Sylvanas is not yet a collection of later arguments about her character. Their stories unfold in sequence, with space to feel the changes rather than starting from the outcome.

The Frozen Throne is particularly useful here. It does not simply tie a bow on Reign of Chaos. It follows the damage left behind and the people trying to claim a future inside it. That makes it valuable context for a new campaign about the Forsaken. According to Blizzard's overview of Forsaken Kingdom, the new story begins in Lordaeron's ruins and follows the Forsaken as they break free of the Scourge and establish a foothold in the Undercity. The original campaigns let you see why that ground matters before a new story asks what can grow there.

You do not need to play like a competitive RTS player

There is a temptation to look at a classic strategy game and assume that enjoying it requires quick hands, perfect build orders and an appetite for online matches. The campaigns ask something different. They give you time to learn four factions, pay attention to a small group of important units and adapt when the mission changes. You will still have to make decisions under pressure. You do not have to master a competitive ladder to appreciate why a defence holds or why an attack falls apart.

Play for the story and the texture of the world. Notice how a faction gathers resources, which units it relies on, and how a new objective changes the value of the army you brought. Let the game surprise you when it shifts away from the standard base-building pattern. If you know where the story ends, pay attention to how much uncertainty it creates on the way there.

Blizzard says Forsaken Kingdom mixes a traditional four-mission prologue with a more open, RPG-shaped main campaign inspired by The Frozen Throne's Rexxar adventure. That description is an invitation to revisit the range already present in Warcraft III, not proof that the new campaign delivers on it. Blizzard's design overview makes the connection explicit.

Return for the journey, not just the answers

The old campaigns will tell you where the Forsaken came from. More importantly, they will remind you how Warcraft III made its history feel consequential. A kingdom did not fall because a paragraph said it did. You watched the threat approach, tried to hold a line, changed sides and saw what remained afterward.

That is why I would replay Reign of Chaos and The Frozen Throne before stepping into Forsaken Kingdom. Not to complete homework for a new release, but to spend time in a world whose future once felt undecided. The best reason to return to Warcraft III is that its history still feels like something you helped make.
