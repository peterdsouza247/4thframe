10 NES and Famicom Shooters Worth Taking Flight For

By Peter D’Souza
CLEAR THE SKIES
Fourth Frame
List / 8 min

The plane sits ahead of you, the ocean stretches to the horizon, and something dangerous is approaching from the distance. Sky Destroyer makes an unusually strong first impression for such a small game. You can see the machine you are flying and the sea beneath it. That simple arrangement gives its battles a sense of place.

The NES and its Japanese counterpart, the Famicom, offered far more than one kind of flying shooter. Some built everything around a formation breaking apart. Others gave you a scrolling landscape, an upgrade decision or another player whose survival suddenly mattered as much as yours.

This is an editorial ranking of ten games worth revisiting, balancing distinctive ideas, handling and variety. It includes real aircraft and fictional spacecraft, so Galaga belongs alongside 1943. Japan-only Famicom releases are labelled. These recommendations concern the home versions, which can differ considerably from the arcade games bearing the same names. Familiar games sit beside less obvious picks; obscurity is not a requirement.

10. Sky Destroyer

Version: Famicom, Japan-only. Style: behind-the-plane aerial combat.

Most games in this list show the action from above or the side. Taito's Sky Destroyer places the view behind your aircraft, with machine-gun fire for the skies and torpedoes for targets below. Having to think about both air and sea gives the scene more character than a simple procession of incoming planes. Gameplay and version reference.

The repetition is noticeable, and the early presentation does not offer the variety of the higher entries. That is why it starts this ranking rather than leading it. Yet the horizon, approaching enemies and changing light remain a compelling little spectacle. If this is the plane game you half remember from childhood, there is a good reason its viewpoint stayed with you. Its original Famicom release was in 1985.

9. Galaga: Demons of Death

Version: NES; Galaga on Famicom. Style: fixed-screen space shooting.

Galaga gets a great deal from restricting your movement to the bottom of the screen. Enemies assemble overhead, then break formation and dive. The important space is often the gap a movement creates, rather than the enemy you are currently shooting. A good run depends on recognising when an attack is becoming dangerous.

Its most memorable gamble is letting a fighter be captured, then rescuing it to form a dual fighter with greater firepower. That possibility makes a threatening enemy ability something you can exploit. The fixed screen limits the scenery, but the changing formations provide the drama. It is the clearest starting point here if scrolling terrain and elaborate upgrades feel like too much to learn at once. The NES manual explains the capture and rescue mechanic.

8. Xevious

Version: NES/Famicom. Style: vertically scrolling air-and-ground combat.

The forward gun handles airborne enemies. The targeting reticle tells you where a bomb will hit the ground. That distinction makes Xevious immediately interesting: a position that lines up a ground target may also put you in the path of incoming fire. You have to read two layers of the battlefield at once.

Its restrained presentation is a long way from the busy spectacle of later shooters, but the division of your attention still works. Rather than chasing a more powerful weapon, you are trying to make better use of the two attacks already available. It is a useful next step after Galaga: more movement, a scrolling landscape and another job to do, without a complicated upgrade system. NES mechanics and manual excerpt.

7. Stinger

Version: NES; Moero TwinBee on Famicom. Style: colourful scrolling shooting.

Clouds contain bells. Shooting a bell changes its colour, and collecting the colour you want gives you a particular benefit. That turns the act of powering up into its own small juggling problem, because the enemies do not stop attacking while you arrange a pickup.

The cartoon aircraft and odd enemies make Stinger a welcome change from military sorties and alien machinery. Its alternating scrolling directions also keep the journey from settling into one routine. Two-player play is part of the NES version's appeal, but do not confuse its limits with the Japanese release: Moero TwinBee supports three players, while the NES game supports two. The friendliness of the art should not be mistaken for an easy ride. Version differences and bell system.

6. 1943: The Battle of Midway

Version: NES/Famicom. Style: military aircraft, vertical scrolling.

For someone who specifically wants planes rather than spaceships, this would be my first recommendation. Air formations and large naval targets make the combat feel like a sortie with an objective. The NES adaptation also lets you improve your aircraft's attributes, giving later attempts a sense of preparation as well as practice.

The energy meter adds pressure even when you are avoiding direct hits. Survival includes managing depletion and taking the right pickups, not simply keeping a small collection of lives intact. It can be a demanding game, and its longer engagements will not suit everyone. Still, the mixture of aircraft development, aerial threats and ships gives it a stronger return appeal than 1942 for this list. The console progression system is a reason to seek out this version specifically. NES adaptation and energy system.

5. Gradius

Version: NES/Famicom. Style: horizontal space shooting with selectable upgrades.

The power meter is the decision that makes Gradius distinctive. Collecting a capsule advances the selection; pressing the power-up button takes the highlighted upgrade. You can improve something useful now or keep collecting toward a different weapon or protection. A pickup has value because of what it lets you choose.

Building the Vic Viper into a capable ship feels excellent. Losing those upgrades makes the return to a weaker craft feel severe, particularly in a stage you had been handling comfortably. That harsh recovery is worth knowing about before you begin. If you enjoy preparing a machine and then protecting the investment, however, the game has a strong pull. Konami's NES manual explains the power-boosting system.

4. Crisis Force

Version: Famicom, Japan-only. Style: vertical shooting with transforming craft.

Konami's late Famicom shooter makes a persuasive argument for looking beyond the NES releases you already know. The craft can switch configurations, changing its attack coverage. Deciding how to address enemies around you gives the transformation an immediate purpose.

Two-player play has an additional spectacle: the ships can merge into a powerful temporary form, with one player taking control. It is an unusual way to make cooperation change the machinery on screen rather than merely double the number of shots. The ambitious backgrounds and brisk action help give the game the feel of a bigger production. It is worth seeking out if your impression of this hardware's shooters stops at the earliest arcade adaptations. Transformations and cooperative play.

3. Zanac

Version: NES. Style: vertical shooting with responsive difficulty.

Zanac makes selecting and using weapons part of a conversation with the game's opposition. Its difficulty-control system reacts to aspects of play and weapon use, varying the pressure instead of relying entirely on an identical enemy procession every time. The original manual describes this as automatic difficulty control.

That does not mean a modern learning system or an opponent that understands your intentions. It means rules that alter the attack patterns and difficulty as you play. The result is an energetic shooter where recovery and adaptation matter alongside memorising the route. Its clean visual style also helps you pick out threats quickly. Start here if you like the idea of surviving a situation that can change, rather than perfecting only one fixed sequence.

2. Gun-Nac

Version: NES/Famicom. Style: vertical shooting with weapons, bombs and a shop.

Compile pairs a generous arsenal with enemies that can look decidedly ridiculous, including hostile rabbits and everyday objects. The humour makes the setting distinctive without preventing the shooting from becoming serious. Upgrading a weapon gives you the gratifying sense that the small ship has become a much larger problem for everything ahead of it.

Collecting money adds another reason to consider a risky pickup. Between stages, the supply shop lets you spend on equipment, connecting decisions made during the fighting with your preparation for the next area. There is enough to learn that it rewards another attempt, while the weapon variety offers room to find an approach you enjoy. This is the less obvious recommendation I would put beside the console's established classics. The supply shop and arsenal.

1. Life Force

Version: NES. Style: alternating horizontal and vertical space shooting.

Flying into an enormous alien gives Life Force a setting that is difficult to forget. Organic passages make the environment feel hostile before the enemies arrive. Alternating horizontal and vertical stages changes how you read the screen and where you look for the next danger. Weapons and power-ups connect it to Gradius, but the journey has a character of its own.

Two-player cooperative play is the deciding advantage for this ranking. Learning a difficult section together, then surviving it with both ships intact, gives the achievement another layer. It remains demanding, and its upgrade losses can hurt. For variety, memorable scenery and a good reason to bring someone else along, though, this is my first overall pick. This entry refers to the NES Life Force, not every arcade or home release called Salamander. Publisher description of the NES version.

Pick the kind of flight you want

Start with Galaga for a simple control scheme and formation reading. Pick 1943 if the attraction is aircraft and naval battles. Choose Gun-Nac for upgrades and odd personality, or Life Force for a shared campaign. Sky Destroyer is the distinctive nostalgia stop, especially if you remember watching the ocean from behind a green plane.

A good shooter makes you notice something before it happens: the gap closing, the formation turning, the pickup drifting into danger. These games use a small screen to make those decisions immediate. That is enough to keep a short flight interesting, even decades later.
