Who Is a Football Club For? What Ted Lasso and Manchester City Reveal About Ownership

By Peter D’Souza
THE CLUB
Fourth Frame
Long read / 5 min

In Ted Lasso's third season, Edwin Akufo gathers club owners to sell them a lucrative new league. Rebecca Welton looks around the room and asks them to remember the people who first made them love football. Her argument works. The other owners abandon the scheme, and the game is saved from their worst instincts by one of their own.

It is an appealing scene, especially because Rebecca once treated AFC Richmond as a tool for hurting her ex-husband. She has learned to care about the club and the people around it. Still, the decision happens in a room full of owners. Supporters are central to Rebecca's argument, but none of them gets a seat at the table. The club is theirs in feeling and hers in law.

That gap between belonging and control is where Ted Lasso has something to say about real football. Manchester City's newly published financial ruling makes the limits of the show's answer easier to see. Richmond and City are not equivalent clubs, and Edwin Akufo's fictional league has nothing to do with the charges against City. The connection is the power that ownership gives someone over a competition other people have built their lives around.

Spoilers for Ted Lasso through season three follow.

Rebecca's change is the point

In the pilot, Rebecca hires Ted because she expects him to fail. Richmond's staff, players and fans face the consequences of a plan they know nothing about. Her ownership gives her the freedom to try it. As the series continues, Ted helps create a culture in which players support each other and staff members are heard. Rebecca begins to value that culture herself.

The season-three episode "International Break" takes her change beyond Richmond. Akufo's proposal promises wealth and influence to a select group of owners. Rebecca recognizes that a competition built for them could leave the people who fill the stands with much less to believe in. She persuades the room by reminding her peers that they were once fans too.

It is a fine character moment. It is also a very Ted Lasso solution: a person with power looks at other people more generously, then uses that power well. By the end of the third season, Rebecca even offers Richmond supporters a share of the club. Her change of heart matters, but she still chooses the terms on which they can participate.

A real club has no writer to make the owner listen

Manchester City has a different history and vastly different resources. City Football Group says it is majority owned through a company wholly owned by Sheikh Mansour, with Silver Lake holding a substantial minority stake. City's investment has helped build a club with global reach and success on the pitch. It has also made questions about financial reporting consequential for competitors who have to play under the same rules.

On 29 September 2026, the Premier League published the findings of an independent commission. It upheld all substantive charges concerning breaches of financial rules from 2009/10 through 2017/18, as well as three of four charges concerning failure to cooperate with the league's investigation. The commission found that sham arrangements overstated revenue and understated costs by more than £900 million, giving the appearance that City complied with spending limits when it did not. City maintains its innocence and says it will appeal. A sanction has not yet been imposed.

This is a question about accounts, oversight and competitive rules. It cannot tell us how much a City supporter loves the club, or dismiss the work of players and staff. It also cannot be resolved by asking whether the owner feels an emotional connection to football. The league has to establish what happened and enforce its rules through a process that can withstand challenge.

Football's invisible decisions

Ted Lasso makes club administration visible through Rebecca and Higgins. They have names and faces, so we can see when a decision hurts someone and when another decision begins to repair it. Real administration often consists of contracts, accounts and regulatory proceedings. It shapes what clubs can spend, who can compete and whether other clubs trust the result.

The City process shows how difficult accountability can be after the fact. The league says its investigation began in December 2018; the commission hearing ended in December 2024, and its core findings were published in September 2026. City has the right to appeal and the question of sanction remains open. Whatever follows, those seasons cannot be played again. That delay matters to City's supporters as well as its rivals, all of whom have had to live with the unresolved case.

Richmond's pub regulars remind us why any of this has stakes. A club is part of how people spend their weeks and remember their lives. City's fans have that relationship with their team too. Ownership can fund a stadium, recruit talent and take a club across the world, but those decisions do not give owners a greater claim to its meaning than the people who have stayed with it.

The part television cannot settle

Rebecca's speech is moving because it imagines someone powerful remembering what it felt like to have no power in the game. More owners could stand to remember that. But the scene ends when the owners agree. It has little reason to ask what happens when they do not, or when a decision is hidden in financial paperwork for years.

That is why the City ruling belongs in this conversation without turning City into Ted Lasso's villain. The commission's findings, City's appeal and the eventual sanction need to be judged on their own evidence. Richmond supplies a question worth bringing to that process: who pays for the choices made at the top of a club? The real answer has to be written into rules and enforced in time for the people outside the owners' room to trust them.
