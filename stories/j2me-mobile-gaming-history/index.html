5 Surprising Facts About Mobile Gaming's Forgotten J2ME Era

By Peter D’Souza
MOBILE ERA
Fourth Frame
List / 04

Before app stores, people played games on phones with physical buttons and screens smaller than a postage stamp. Downloading one might involve a carrier menu, an unfamiliar file format and a lot of patience. Here are five details from that period that are easy to forget.

1. Snake wasn't the first game on a phone

A version of Tetris shipped on the Hagenuk MT-2000 in 1994, three years before Nokia put Snake on the 6110. Snake had the advantage of being there when you switched the phone on. You didn't have to find it or pay for a download, which helps explain why so many people played it.

2. Sonic helped demonstrate Java on a phone

At the 2001 JavaOne conference, Digital Bridges and Sega demonstrated a Sonic sprite moving on prototype Motorola hardware. It was a small demonstration, but seeing a recognisable game character move on a phone made the possibilities easier to picture.

3. The first commercial Java phones couldn't even display colour

The Nokia 3410 and Siemens M50 brought Java games to monochrome screens in 2002. At resolutions as low as 96 by 64 pixels, developers had to make every sprite and menu choice count.

4. The N-Gage made changing games a chore

Changing games on the original 2003 N-Gage meant powering it down, removing the back and battery, and reaching the cartridge slot underneath. To make a call, you held the side of the phone to your face. The internet called it 'side-talking.' The later N-Gage QD moved the cartridge slot, but the first model's awkward design had already become the story.

5. The App Store changed what happened to old Java games

The App Store changed how players found and bought phone games. Java releases were built for many different handsets and storefronts, which makes them difficult to collect and run now. Emulation and preservation groups have kept many of them accessible when the original download routes disappeared.
