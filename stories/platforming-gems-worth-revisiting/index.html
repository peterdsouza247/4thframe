9 Overlooked Platforming Gems Worth Revisiting

By Peter D’Souza
ONE MORE JUMP
Fourth Frame
List / 8 min

A cardboard box is a surprisingly good weapon when you are a chipmunk. Pick it up, throw it at a mechanical dog, then duck beneath another while your partner decides whether to help or hurl you across the screen. Chip 'n Dale: Rescue Rangers understood that a small set of good actions could carry an entire adventure.

That is the appeal of this corner of platforming history. Mario and Sonic are obvious return visits, but the cartridges and early PC games around them held cartoon adaptations, odd mascots and action games with ideas worth borrowing. Some of these sold well. Aladdin and The Lion King are familiar names, hardly secret discoveries. The case here is for playing them again, alongside games that receive much less attention.

These are nine picks across consoles and DOS, not a strict ranking. The list stretches from straightforward platformers to action games where jumping shares the controller with fighting. Versions matter: a familiar title on a different console can be a different game altogether.

1. Chip 'n Dale: Rescue Rangers

Version: NES. Best for: two-player mischief.

Capcom makes being small the whole point. Boxes become ammunition and hiding places, while oversized furniture turns an ordinary room into a landscape. You can understand the basic moves almost immediately, then spend the rest of a stage discovering what else those moves permit. The original manual even explains how steel boxes can be stacked to reach higher platforms.

Two-player play adds a useful amount of chaos. Carrying and throwing a partner can solve a problem or create one, depending on the mood of the person beside you. It gives cooperation a physical, slightly mischievous quality. The game is also perfectly enjoyable alone; having someone to blame for a bad landing is a bonus.

2. Mighty Morphin Power Rangers

Version: the 1994 SNES game. Best for: a short burst of Saturday-morning action.

This is the genre-borderline pick. Natsume's game is primarily a side-scrolling brawler, with climbing, jumping and platforming sections along its single-plane stages. What earns it a place is how neatly it stages the television fantasy. The early levels begin with your chosen Ranger in civilian clothes, then let you morph before the boss encounter. Later, the scale changes again for Megazord battles.

The fighting is simple and the enemy designs repeat, so this is a modest recommendation rather than a lost masterpiece. Still, the transformation gives a familiar stage structure a little anticipation. If your memory is of a different Power Rangers cartridge, check the platform: the Genesis original is a fighting game, not this SNES adventure. SNES version details.

3. The Lion King

Versions: SNES and Mega Drive/Genesis. Best for: animation and a challenge you may still resent.

The opening has a confidence few film adaptations manage. Young Simba bounds through bright scenery, and his movement sells the character before the game needs much explanation. Growing into adult Simba changes the emphasis from a cub's pouncing and roaring toward more direct combat. Even in a short platformer, that change gives the journey a sense of development.

Then there is the obstacle course accompanying I Just Can't Wait to Be King. Its monkeys, giraffes and timing demands make a cheerful scene feel considerably less cheerful. The difficulty is uneven, and attractive animation cannot excuse every awkward jump. Revisit it for the craft and the changing character, with realistic expectations about the frustration. This entry concerns the 16-bit games; the NES and handheld releases should not be treated as identical versions. SNES gameplay reference.

4. Aladdin

Versions: Capcom's SNES game and Virgin's Mega Drive/Genesis game. Best for: comparing two interpretations of one film.

There are two recommendations under this heading. Capcom's Aladdin puts its emphasis on movement, jumps and acrobatics. Apples can stun enemies before you bounce on them; the pleasure is in keeping the journey moving. Virgin's version gives Aladdin a sword as well as throwable apples, with animation that makes even a brief exchange with a guard entertaining to watch.

Choose the SNES game if platforming is the main attraction. Choose the Genesis game if expressive characters and swordplay are what you remember. Better still, try both and notice how the same source material produces different rhythms. They are separate games, not a single port with altered colours. The Disney Classic Games Collection includes both 16-bit interpretations; it is an expanded package compared with the original 2019 collection. Capcom version background; Genesis mechanics.

5. Shadow of the Ninja

Version: NES, also known as Blue Shadow in Europe. Best for: a ninja adventure you can share.

A second ninja changes the appeal immediately. Hayate and Kaede can fight through Natsume's action platformer together, with enemies and gaps demanding attention even when there are two of you. Hanging from platforms and choosing when to commit to an attack make movement part of the fighting, rather than a pause between encounters.

The futuristic streets and machinery give the adventure a harsher atmosphere than the cartoon games here. Its difficulty asks both players to learn the route, and company does not make every jump forgiving. That is part of the satisfaction: clearing a troublesome section together feels earned. This entry is for the original NES game, called Kage in Japan and Blue Shadow in Europe; the later Shadow of the Ninja Reborn is a separate remake. Original game and cooperative play.

6. Earthworm Jim

Versions: the original Mega Drive/Genesis and SNES games. Best for: strange sights and physical comedy.

Jim's suit gives him a gun, but his own body is the better joke. He can use his worm head as a whip to strike enemies and catch hooks, turning a ridiculous character design into a useful platforming move. The animation carries that absurdity into the way he stands, moves and reacts. Even the opening's cow-launching gag makes the world feel happily unhinged.

Shiny keeps interrupting the usual run-and-jump routine with different situations, including rocket races and the job of protecting Peter Puppy. Those detours give the game personality, although some are more frustrating than funny when you have to repeat them. Revisit it for its expressive action and willingness to be strange, rather than expecting every experiment to land. The 16-bit releases, later Special Edition and handheld adaptations are not all interchangeable. Original versions and gameplay.

7. Darkwing Duck

Version: NES. Best for: careful jumps and a hero with more confidence than health.

The gas gun is the obvious tool, but Darkwing's cape and ability to hang from ledges make the game more interesting than a simple march to the right. You can block certain incoming attacks, wait for an opening, then climb back into the action. It asks you to notice enemy placement and make room for a clean shot.

Special gas adds further possibilities, including arrows that can help with traversal. It is a compact example of how a character's equipment can shape a platformer. Expect to learn troublesome sections rather than sail through on cartoon charm. If DuckTales is already on your replay list, this is a worthwhile companion with a sharper action emphasis. Controls and special weapons.

8. Jazz Jackrabbit

Version: the original 1994 DOS game. Best for: fast movement with a gun in your hands.

Jazz brings mascot-platformer energy to the PC: a green rabbit, a red bandana and enough speed to make you watch the next stretch of ground carefully. His gun changes the rhythm. You can clear threats while moving, but running too eagerly still puts you into trouble. The pleasure is in finding a route you can take at pace without losing control of the situation.

Collecting ammunition lets you switch away from the basic blaster, with weapons such as bouncing projectiles and missiles that split into a V-shaped shot. The original on-disk manual explains the arsenal. This recommendation is for the first Jazz Jackrabbit, not its sequel. The Jazz Jackrabbit Collection groups the original episodes, additional CD episodes and Holiday Hare outings, making the scope of the release worth checking.

9. Dangerous Dave

Version: the 1990 DOS game. Best for: a small challenge with no room for careless jumps.

Find the trophy, then reach the exit. Dangerous Dave gives you a goal that fits in a sentence, but the route can make you think twice. Fire, awkward ledges and moving enemies turn a simple screen into a sequence you need to understand. A gun or jetpack can change your options, making the journey to a useful pickup part of the problem.

It is plainer and less fluid than the big 16-bit productions in this list, and its unforgiving hazards can make a short attempt end abruptly. The appeal is how quickly it gets down to business. There is very little between starting the game and having a jump worth concentrating on. Here, “Dave” means the original DOS Dangerous Dave, rather than Dangerous Dave in the Haunted Mansion. John Romero's series history identifies the 1990 PC release; his level notes describe the trophies, weapons and jetpack routes.

Where to start

For an easy first decision, choose Rescue Rangers with a friend or Capcom's Aladdin for platforming. Try Earthworm Jim for strange comedy, Jazz Jackrabbit for speed, or Dangerous Dave for a compact DOS challenge. Pick Shadow of the Ninja when you want to learn a demanding route with someone beside you.

The strongest reason to revisit these games is a specific action that still feels good: throwing the box, catching the ledge, landing the head whip. Get that right and an old game needs very little help to become interesting again.
