10 Overlooked Platforming Gems Worth Revisiting

By Peter D’Souza
ONE MORE JUMP
Fourth Frame
List / 8 min

A cardboard box is a surprisingly good weapon when you are a chipmunk. Pick it up, throw it at a mechanical dog, then duck beneath another while your partner decides whether to help or hurl you across the screen. Chip 'n Dale: Rescue Rangers understood that a small set of good actions could carry an entire adventure.

That is the appeal of this corner of platforming history. Mario and Sonic are obvious return visits, but the shelves around them held cartoon adaptations, odd mascots and action games with ideas worth borrowing. Some of these sold well. Aladdin and The Lion King are familiar names, hardly secret discoveries. The case here is for playing them again, alongside games that receive much less attention.

These are ten picks, not a strict ranking. The list stretches from straightforward platformers to action games where jumping shares the controller with fighting. Versions matter: a familiar title on a different console can be a different game altogether.

1. Chip 'n Dale: Rescue Rangers

Version: NES. Best for: two-player mischief.

Capcom makes being small the whole point. Boxes become ammunition and hiding places, while oversized furniture turns an ordinary room into a landscape. You can understand the basic moves almost immediately, then spend the rest of a stage discovering what else those moves permit. The original manual even explains how steel boxes can be stacked to reach higher platforms.

Two-player play adds a useful amount of chaos. Carrying and throwing a partner can solve a problem or create one, depending on the mood of the person beside you. It gives cooperation a physical, slightly mischievous quality. The game is also perfectly enjoyable alone; having someone to blame for a bad landing is a bonus.

2. Mighty Morphin Power Rangers

Version: the 1994 SNES game. Best for: a short burst of Saturday-morning action.

This is the genre-borderline pick. Natsume's game is primarily a side-scrolling brawler, with climbing, jumping and platforming sections along its single-plane stages. What earns it a place is how neatly it stages the television fantasy. The early levels begin with your chosen Ranger in civilian clothes, then let you morph before the boss encounter. Later, the scale changes again for Megazord battles.

The fighting is simple and the enemy designs repeat, so this is a modest recommendation rather than a lost masterpiece. Still, the transformation gives a familiar stage structure a little anticipation. If your memory is of a different Power Rangers cartridge, check the platform: the Genesis original is a fighting game, not this SNES adventure. SNES version details.

3. The Lion King

Versions: SNES and Mega Drive/Genesis. Best for: animation and a challenge you may still resent.

The opening has a confidence few film adaptations manage. Young Simba bounds through bright scenery, and his movement sells the character before the game needs much explanation. Growing into adult Simba changes the emphasis from a cub's pouncing and roaring toward more direct combat. Even in a short platformer, that change gives the journey a sense of development.

Then there is the obstacle course accompanying I Just Can't Wait to Be King. Its monkeys, giraffes and timing demands make a cheerful scene feel considerably less cheerful. The difficulty is uneven, and attractive animation cannot excuse every awkward jump. Revisit it for the craft and the changing character, with realistic expectations about the frustration. This entry concerns the 16-bit games; the NES and handheld releases should not be treated as identical versions. SNES gameplay reference.

4. Aladdin

Versions: Capcom's SNES game and Virgin's Mega Drive/Genesis game. Best for: comparing two interpretations of one film.

There are two recommendations under this heading. Capcom's Aladdin puts its emphasis on movement, jumps and acrobatics. Apples can stun enemies before you bounce on them; the pleasure is in keeping the journey moving. Virgin's version gives Aladdin a sword as well as throwable apples, with animation that makes even a brief exchange with a guard entertaining to watch.

Choose the SNES game if platforming is the main attraction. Choose the Genesis game if expressive characters and swordplay are what you remember. Better still, try both and notice how the same source material produces different rhythms. They are separate games, not a single port with altered colours. The Disney Classic Games Collection includes both 16-bit interpretations; it is an expanded package compared with the original 2019 collection. Capcom version background; Genesis mechanics.

5. Shadow Dancer: The Secret of Shinobi

Version: Mega Drive/Genesis. Best for: deliberate action with an unusually useful dog.

The Shinobi name covers several different kinds of ninja game. For a less obvious return visit, Shadow Dancer deserves a turn. Its dog can pin an enemy down while you make the next move, adding another way to manage an encounter beyond throwing a shuriken or closing the distance yourself.

One hit is enough to kill you. That makes the distance to an enemy, the order of your attacks and the moment you commit to a jump matter. It is satisfying when a section that initially looks dangerous becomes a sequence you can execute cleanly. The Genesis game differs substantially from the arcade Shadow Dancer, so check the subtitle. The Revenge of Shinobi and Shinobi III are other strong directions to explore; neither should be confused with Tengen's much rougher NES adaptation of the original Shinobi. Version and gameplay comparison.

6. Little Nemo: The Dream Master

Version: NES. Best for: exploring a strange world through changing abilities.

Nemo enters Slumberland with a bag of candy, which is a wonderfully peculiar starting loadout. Feed certain creatures and he can ride them or take on their abilities. A new animal can change how you read the terrain: something that looked unreachable becomes a route worth investigating. Gathering keys encourages you to search rather than race straight to the exit.

There is a vulnerability to controlling a small child in this world, particularly when candy will only stun an enemy. The animals make the dream inviting, but the game can be demanding. Its appeal is the mixture of curiosity and danger, with each new capability making familiar space feel different. Capcom's manual is a useful introduction to how the animal companions work.

7. Darkwing Duck

Version: NES. Best for: careful jumps and a hero with more confidence than health.

The gas gun is the obvious tool, but Darkwing's cape and ability to hang from ledges make the game more interesting than a simple march to the right. You can block certain incoming attacks, wait for an opening, then climb back into the action. It asks you to notice enemy placement and make room for a clean shot.

Special gas adds further possibilities, including arrows that can help with traversal. It is a compact example of how a character's equipment can shape a platformer. Expect to learn troublesome sections rather than sail through on cartoon charm. If DuckTales is already on your replay list, this is a worthwhile companion with a sharper action emphasis. Controls and special weapons.

8. Ristar

Version: Mega Drive/Genesis. Best for: a mascot who actually moves differently.

Ristar reaches out with stretchy arms, grabs an enemy and pulls himself into a headbutt. The same emphasis on reaching and holding shapes the platforming. Where a conventional mascot game asks whether you can clear a gap, this one also asks what you can grab around it.

The movement takes a little adjustment if you arrive expecting Sonic's momentum. Give it that time. Reaching in different directions makes the character feel unusually tactile, and it gives the designers more to work with than a change of costume. There is a strong idea here that would still support a good game without the bright scenery or appealing star-shaped hero. This recommendation is for the Genesis release, not the separate Game Gear adaptation. How the grab works.

9. Rocket Knight Adventures

Version: Mega Drive/Genesis. Best for: a platformer that keeps changing the situation.

An armoured opossum with a rocket pack sounds like a mascot assembled from spare parts. Sparkster makes the combination work. Charging the pack and choosing a direction gives him a burst of movement that can serve traversal or attack. A good launch feels committed, with a destination you have to think about before letting go.

Konami surrounds that central move with changing scenery and encounters, so the game has room to surprise beyond its first clever idea. Its personality comes through in the action as much as the character design. The original Rocket Knight Adventures is included in Rocket Knight Adventures: Re-Sparked, alongside the two 16-bit Sparkster games. Those are distinct follow-ups, not alternate names for this adventure.

10. Kid Chameleon

Version: Mega Drive/Genesis. Best for: secrets, transformations and a longer commitment.

The sunglasses suggest another attempt to make a mascot look cool. The masks are the reason to stay. A knight can climb walls, a samurai carries a sword, and other forms change what you can break, reach or survive. Finding a transformation can alter the route through a stage as well as your next fight.

This is the untidy, ambitious pick. Branching paths and a long journey make it rewarding to investigate, but also harder to recommend for a quick evening than Rescue Rangers. Expect some friction and a little wandering. If you enjoy platformers where a power-up changes the possibilities of a level, there is plenty to discover beneath the attitude. Masks and route design.

Where to start

For an easy first decision, choose Rescue Rangers with a friend, Capcom's Aladdin for platforming, or Ristar for an unfamiliar way to move. Save Shadow Dancer for an evening when you want to learn a precise sequence, and Kid Chameleon for when you have time to get lost.

The strongest reason to revisit these games is a specific action that still feels good: throwing the box, catching the ledge, launching the rocket. Get that right and an old cartridge needs very little help to become interesting again.
