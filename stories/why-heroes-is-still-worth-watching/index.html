Why Heroes Is Still Worth Watching 20 Years Later

By Peter D’Souza
HEROES
Fourth Frame
Long read / 4 min

The first episode of Heroes asks what you would do if you discovered you could do something impossible. Its answer depends on who you are. Claire Bennet tests the limits of a body that keeps healing, then has to go back to high school. Hiro Nakamura can bend time and space, and is delighted to find himself inside the sort of story he loves. Peter Petrelli thinks he might be able to fly, though he is much more certain that he wants to help people. The abilities are exciting. The lives they interrupt are what make you stay.

Heroes premiered in September 2006, twenty years ago this month. Watching Lanterns has put superhero television back on my mind, and this feels like a good moment to revisit a show built before a shared cinematic universe was the default model for the genre. Its first season remains an unusually satisfying recommendation. The later seasons are a more complicated proposition, but they do not erase what that opening run gets right.

A power is also a problem

Claire's regeneration does not give her a ready-made place in the world. She still has to decide who can know about it and whether her family is protecting her or keeping secrets from her. The show's early scenes of her recording injuries are memorable because she is trying to understand herself with the tools available to a teenager. Her ability makes her vulnerable in ways a conventional origin story might hurry past.

Peter's story has a different pull. He wants to believe that a meaningful life involves saving someone, long before he understands the scale of what he can do. His brother Nathan worries about reputation and a political future. Their conflict gives both characters something tangible to lose. Hiro, meanwhile, brings a welcome sense of joy to the premise, but the show tests whether enthusiasm can survive the consequences of changing events. These people approach heroism from different starting points, which makes their eventual connections feel earned.

The series is most compelling when it lets an extraordinary event make an ordinary relationship harder. A parent with a secret, a brother who refuses to believe you, a friend asked to trust a story that sounds absurd: those are easier to recognize than a grand fight over the fate of the world. The opening episode's premise is simple enough to hold all of them.

The pleasure of watching the stories meet

The first season moves among people in different cities who have no reason, at first, to think their lives belong to the same story. An image or prediction that means little to one character becomes urgent once we have followed somebody else. Each new connection changes what the audience thinks it knows. It is an ensemble mystery with the momentum of a page-turning comic and the space to spend time with each person's fears.

That structure also gives the show's biggest question some force. The characters learn that a catastrophe may be coming, but none of them has a complete account of how to stop it. They have to judge whom to trust with incomplete information. The famous injunction to save Claire works because one teenager's safety suddenly seems tied to everyone else's. The scale grows without entirely losing the person at its centre.

The early episodes understand the appeal of withheld information. They also know when to give us an answer and let it create a new problem. Creator Tim Kring has discussed the first season's planning, including the difficulty of sustaining so many interlocking stories on a network television schedule. You can feel the ambition in the finished season. Sometimes its effects show their age, but the suspense rarely depends on spectacle alone.

What does not hold up as well

It would be misleading to recommend all four seasons with the same confidence. The later run keeps finding interesting characters and ideas, yet some revelations arrive with less impact once the series has established how readily it can change the rules. Familiar faces remain central even when a fresh set of people might have opened up the premise. The second season was cut short during the 2007 writers' strike, as Kring recounted in the anniversary interview, though the strike alone cannot account for every later creative choice.

The first season has its own weaknesses. Certain plot turns rely on people failing to share information, and the pace can make a character's decision feel convenient. A twenty-episode-plus network season also asks for more patience than many viewers now expect. If you want every mystery resolved quickly, its pleasures may take time to appear.

Those reservations make the recommendation more useful. Start with season one, which tells a substantial story and gives its central characters room to become more than their powers. If their company is enough to bring you back, the remaining seasons have moments worth finding. There is no need to pretend the whole run is equally strong.

Why return to it now?

Heroes catches people at the instant their lives stop making sense and lets them disagree about what comes next. Some want to hide. Some want an explanation. Some see an opportunity to control others. Hiro wants an adventure; Claire wants room to decide who she is. Twenty years of superhero television have produced bigger images and more elaborate worlds, but this show's first season still understands the nervous excitement of discovering you might be different.

Its question has aged well: once you can do something remarkable, what responsibility follows? Heroes works because it makes that question personal before anyone gets to call themselves a hero.
