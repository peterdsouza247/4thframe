10 Best Vampires In Gaming (And The Underrated Ones You're Sleeping On)

With The Blood of Dawnwalker finally out and turning players into card-carrying vampire obsessives all over again, it felt like the right time to look past the obvious picks and dig into gaming's long, bloody relationship with the undead — the icons, the surprises, and a couple of genuinely obscure entries that even seasoned vampire fans might not clock as vampires at all.

10. Total War: Warhammer's Vampire Counts

What makes this one worth including isn't the aesthetic, it's the scale. Most vampire fiction is intimate — one bloodsucker, one victim. Warhammer's Vampire Counts turn vampirism into a political institution: Vlad von Carstein doesn't seduce individuals, he conquers nations and raises their dead as a standing army. It's the rare vampire story told at empire-building scale, and that shift in scope is what earns it a spot most "best vampire" lists never think to look for.

9. Vampire: The Masquerade – Bloodlines

The compelling part isn't the writing (though it's still sharp two decades later) — it's that your clan choice mechanically becomes your vampirism. Play a Malkavian and your dialogue options fracture into prophetic nonsense. Play a Nosferatu and you're locked out of normal social spaces entirely, forced through sewers because your face is a monster's. No other vampire game makes the curse a systems problem instead of a narrative one.

8. Skyrim's Vampire Lords

The Vampire Lord transformation is mechanically the best power fantasy in Skyrim's arsenal — flight, life drain, raising thralls. The compelling twist is that it's also socially catastrophic: NPCs recoil, guards attack on sight, entire questlines close off. It's one of the only games where "becoming more powerful" and "becoming more monstrous" are the literal same button. Serana's own arc, centuries imprisoned by a father who saw her as property rather than daughter, gives that power a genuine cost worth caring about.

7. Vampyr

Here's the thing most vampire games get wrong: feeding is flavour text. Vampyr makes it a spreadsheet with a conscience. Every Londoner you drain has a name, a family, a role in their district's health — and killing them measurably degrades the city around you. It's the only vampire game where restraint is a real, mechanically painful choice rather than a moral pose.

6. The Blood of Dawnwalker

Coen isn't one character, he's two — a human by day, a vampire by night, with genuinely different capabilities, relationships, and reputations attached to each. That dual-identity structure, running against a hard thirty-day clock to save his family, is what makes this feel fresh rather than another open-world RPG with fangs bolted on. The ticking clock forces choices most vampire games let you stall on forever.

5. Elden Ring's Chanting Winged Dames

A slight cheat — FromSoftware never actually calls these bat-winged crones vampires. But what makes them compelling has nothing to do with the label and everything to do with how you earn the story: nobody hands it to you. Translate their Latin dirge yourself and you find women once destined for motherhood, spurned by Queen Marika, left to mourn eternally. It's vampiric tragedy told entirely through environmental patience — the exact opposite of every other entry on this list, which just tells you outright.

4. Bloodborne's Vilebloods

This is the best-kept secret on the whole list, and the reason it's compelling isn't just "hidden vampire lore" — it's that the Vilebloods recontextualize the entire base game. Blood ministration is Bloodborne's central premise, sold to you as medicine. Cainhurst Castle reveals its dark mirror: a vampiric aristocracy built on the same blood, just honest about what it actually is. Swear Queen Annalise's oath and you're not finding a side quest, you're finding out what the Healing Church was always hiding.

3. Legacy of Kain

What earns Kain the near-top spot is the sheer audacity of the question the series keeps asking across multiple games and a rewritten timeline: is this vampire the hero, or is "hero" just a role history hasn't finished assigning yet? Raziel's own damnation and resurrection, Kain's manipulation of causality itself — nothing else on this list operates at that scale of moral and temporal ambition.

2. Astarion — Baldur's Gate 3

The reason Astarion works isn't the fangs, it's that Larian wrote his hunger as addiction and his sire's control as literalized abuse — and then handed you the choice to either help him reclaim autonomy or become his abuser's successor. That's the whole trick: the vampirism is the trauma narrative, not a metaphor sitting next to it. Nothing else in gaming has made "vampire" and "survivor" the same word this convincingly.

1. Castlevania

Everything above owes Castlevania rent. What makes it the definitive entry isn't nostalgia, it's that Alucard fighting his own father is the story every entry on this list is still quietly retelling — vampirism as inherited curse you have to choose to resist. Coen's day/night split, Astarion's fight against his sire, even the Vilebloods' doomed bloodline — trace them all back far enough and you land here.
