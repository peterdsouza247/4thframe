The Games We Never Got to Play

StarCraft: Ghost, Warcraft’s lost Thrall adventure, Prey 2 and the other cancellations that still hurt.

By Peter D’Souza
NEVER PLAYED
Fourth Frame
Long read / 23 min

There are games I would happily have cleared my schedule for that never even reached the point of asking for my money.

StarCraft: Ghost is an obvious one. Put me inside that universe as a covert operative, let me sneak around military installations, and make the things I used to command from above considerably more frightening at eye level. I can see the appeal without needing a cinematic trailer to explain it.

Then there is Blizzard’s Andromeda: a proposed Warcraft action game featuring Thrall, with God of War as a point of reference. That is the sort of project I wish had been allowed to find its audience. Warcraft has enough history, character and atmosphere to support adventures that don’t require understanding the latest expansion’s place in a much larger argument.1

Jason Schreier’s reporting is a good way into these stories because he tends to get past the announcement and ask what people were actually making. Follow that question and the cancellations become more interesting, and sometimes more understandable. You find promising ideas, working demonstrations, disagreements about what the game should be, and teams trying to solve problems that the trailer never needed to mention.

I’m interested in the projects where there was a particular experience worth wanting. A different way to explore a familiar setting. A combat system with an unusual relationship at its centre. A horror game that had already demonstrated how effectively it could make a small space unpleasant.

We should be careful about calling all of them lost masterpieces. An unreleased game has a considerable advantage over a released one: we tend to supply the good parts ourselves. It has no disappointing final act, no tedious crafting menu and no mandatory tower to climb unless we choose to imagine one.

But there is enough here to regret. The reasons become clearer when we look at what each game was trying to do.

StarCraft: Ghost would have made the battlefield feel different

The original StarCraft gives you an excellent view of a war and a fairly comfortable distance from its consequences. Units die, replacements are trained, and the next order goes out. Taking that same setting into a third-person action game offered a useful change in scale.

Announced in 2002 for consoles, StarCraft: Ghost centred on Nova, a Terran ghost operative. Development began at Nihilistic Software, moved to Swingin’ Ape Studios in 2004, and continued after Blizzard acquired that studio in 2005. In March 2006, Blizzard announced that production was being postponed indefinitely while it considered the next generation of consoles.23

That announcement can make the game sound like a straightforward casualty of unfortunate timing. The development history was messier. Patrick Stafford’s interviews with developers, reported by Polygon and summarised contemporaneously by Develop, describe a project struggling to settle the balance between stealth and action. New ideas kept arriving while existing ones still needed to become a coherent game.4

As someone who likes Splinter Cell: Chaos Theory, the stealth side makes immediate sense to me. A convincing infiltration game depends on vulnerability. You need to understand what enemies can notice, what your equipment can do and how much trouble you can survive when your plan fails. StarCraft supplies a world in which being caught could be a very bad idea indeed.

A marine is a different proposition when you are beside the armour rather than dragging a selection box over it. A Zerg encounter could make the player reconsider the wisdom of entering a dark facility alone. The setting already contains strong visual identities; a closer camera could have turned those identities into practical information about threats, routes and survival.

The difficulty is that stealth and battlefield spectacle encourage different habits. If Nova can overpower every patrol, observing them becomes optional. If she is fragile enough for infiltration to matter, the game needs care when it asks her to participate in a larger battle. Plenty of good games negotiate that change, but the transition has to be designed. Recognisable units and an expensive cinematic won’t do it for you.

That is why I regret Ghost without assuming Blizzard had a finished classic sitting in a drawer. The published history gives us reasons to believe the project had serious problems. It also gives us a premise worth solving those problems for.

Nova eventually appeared elsewhere, including StarCraft II. That preserves a character, but the original attraction was the opportunity to inhabit her job.5 I still think StarCraft deserves a game that makes crossing one of its battlefields feel like a dangerous personal undertaking.

Andromeda offered a more personal Warcraft

The Blizzard project involving the fired creative director was codenamed Andromeda. In his September 2024 Reddit AMA, Schreier confirmed the name in response to a question about a God of War-style game featuring Thrall, and said it was cancelled when Alex Afrasiabi was fired in 2020.1

The misconduct needs to be described accurately. Blizzard subsequently confirmed that it had terminated Afrasiabi following an investigation into his treatment of other employees. The company’s statement and the allegations against him are a separate part of the record from Schreier’s account of the game’s cancellation.6

There is very little public material with which to assess Andromeda as a game. We have a reported concept and its connection to a leadership departure. That supports interest in what was being attempted; it doesn’t support claims that an almost finished Warcraft equivalent of God of War was thrown away.

Still, Thrall is a compelling choice for this kind of adventure. Warcraft’s history gives him responsibilities and relationships that could carry a story at a human—or orc—scale. An action game could spend time on what it means to exercise power, live with a decision, or meet someone who remembers an earlier version of you. Those are things a sustained journey with one protagonist can explore well.

The God of War comparison suggests possibilities rather than a feature list. I can imagine the appeal of travelling through Azeroth with a small cast, learning a readable combat system and seeing familiar places built around movement, encounters and discovery. I would be especially interested in a game that gave ordinary stretches of the journey enough attention to make the major confrontations matter.

What I wouldn’t want is a procession of famous Warcraft faces interrupting the adventure to establish that they, too, are available for merchandise. A strong setting should give a smaller story confidence to stand on its own.

The disappointing part of Andromeda is that this avenue closed before we could see what the team might make of it. There were other people working on the project whose contributions deserve to exist in the history too. A studio should be able to remove a leader for misconduct and make a clear decision about the work that remains. The public account gives us too little detail to judge the alternatives Blizzard had here.

I would still be interested in another attempt at the underlying idea. Warcraft has room for an adventure with a clear beginning, a manageable cast and an ending you can actually reach.

Hades asked how far Diablo could change

Blizzard’s cancelled Hades—unrelated to Supergiant’s game—raises a different question: how much can you alter before a sequel becomes something else?

Schreier’s published Play Nice excerpt describes Josh Mosqueira’s proposed Diablo follow-up as an over-the-shoulder game with Arkham-influenced combat and death that granted benefits for subsequent runs. Cooperative play complicated the design, particularly the use of slowed time. After Mosqueira left in 2016, Blizzard evaluated the project and cancelled it.7

I can understand wanting a more physical Diablo. The series has grotesque creatures, ruined places and a world that would support a closer view. Deliberately timed blows could make a small encounter satisfying in a way that differs from clearing a room through the accumulated power of a build.

But the pleasure of Diablo also comes from seeing a build work. You choose abilities and equipment, then discover how effectively their interactions deal with a problem. Move towards a combat system that asks for more attention to each individual opponent and you change the balance between preparation and execution.

Neither approach is inherently better. I like games where the immediate act of fighting demands concentration, and I also enjoy systems where good preparation produces an absurdly effective result. Combining them requires deciding where the player’s attention should go. A monster that is fun to counter individually might become irritating when the game surrounds you with twenty of them.

That makes Hades an interesting experiment rather than an obvious improvement we were denied. I would have liked to see Blizzard attempt a separate action game in that world, with the freedom to find its own pace. Calling it the next mainline Diablo would inevitably have brought a different set of expectations.

Prey 2 had a job I actually wanted to do

Of all the games here, Prey 2 gives me one of the clearest answers to “what would you actually do?”

Human Head’s proposed sequel followed Killian Samuels, an abducted US marshal working as a bounty hunter on an alien world. Contemporary E3 coverage described a demonstration involving parkour, shooting during traversal, a scanner that identified targets, and equipment for bringing them down. Despite the headline of one preview calling it hands-on, its writer explicitly said he had watched the demonstration rather than controlled it.8

That distinction matters, but the design still offers something concrete to discuss. A bounty-hunting game gives exploration a purpose beyond finding the next collectible. You are looking for a person in a place that has other people in it. Identifying the target, considering the surroundings and deciding when to act could all belong to the same activity.

The movement is what makes that premise especially attractive. If the target runs, the pursuit should test your understanding of the city. A route across a roof needs to offer something different from following at street level. A gadget should help you make a decision about the chase, rather than simply play an animation after you have won it.

That is the version I would want to play. The public demonstration could only show part of whether Human Head could sustain it, and contemporary coverage still had questions about the wider mission structure. Even so, the parts described fit together in an unusually persuasive way: observation, traversal, pursuit and capture.8

Then the game disappeared from view. In a 2015 Develop interview, project director Chris Rhinehart said Human Head had stopped working on it near the end of 2011, close to alpha, with the major content represented. Bethesda formally confirmed cancellation in 2014, with Pete Hines saying the project had failed to meet its quality requirements. Human Head publicly disagreed with that assessment.910

I don’t think the available record lets us settle that disagreement by choosing the account we prefer. The developer’s pride in the work is meaningful. So is the difference between having the main content present and having a game ready to release. A strong demonstration can establish potential while leaving the hardest production questions unresolved.

What makes this cancellation sting is the specificity of the opportunity. I love the populated science fiction of Mass Effect, where different species and interests share places you can move through and get to know. Prey 2 offered a more physical occupation within that kind of setting. It made me interested in how I would spend an ordinary hour playing it.

Arkane’s released Prey belongs to its own discussion. A shared title doesn’t make it a substitute for Human Head’s bounty-hunting game. Both ideas could have been worth having.

Star Wars 1313 was an invitation downstairs

Star Wars 1313 had a similarly strong setting, although its announced approach was more cinematic.

LucasArts introduced it in 2012 as a third-person action game about a bounty hunter in Coruscant’s underworld. The official description emphasised gadgets, weapons and physical skills, with contributions from across Lucasfilm’s production disciplines. Its title referred to a level deep beneath the city’s surface.11

There is an excellent Star Wars game in the idea of going downstairs and discovering how the galaxy works for people who don’t have a seat in the Senate or an inheritance of mystical importance. Coruscant is especially well suited to that. Its physical arrangement can express status immediately: who gets daylight, who gets space, and who lives beneath everyone else’s traffic.

I would have wanted time to inhabit that setting. A market, a contact, a place where a job goes wrong and people remember it later. The cinematic action could have been enjoyable, but a spectacular escape means more when you have some understanding of the place you are escaping.

That was a question for the finished game. A tightly directed sequence can demonstrate animation, presentation and momentum without revealing much about how a location will function between the set pieces. 1313 looked promising; the footage couldn’t establish that its underworld would become somewhere worth knowing.

Schreier later reported that the project had shifted towards Boba Fett before cancellation.12 I can see the commercial appeal, though the possibility of meeting an unfamiliar protagonist in an unfamiliar corner of Star Wars was attractive too. The setting was already doing a great deal of the work.

In April 2013, following Disney’s acquisition of Lucasfilm, LucasArts’ internal development operation was closed as the company moved towards licensing. That change ended the development circumstances under which 1313 had been made; contemporary statements left open the possibility of external partners, but the project never emerged as a released game.13

This is where the loss extends beyond one title. Closing a development operation disperses the people who have been learning how to make something together. You can retain a licence and still lose a particular combination of skills, experiments and working relationships.

The prospect I regret is that descent into Coruscant: getting close enough to see the machinery beneath the grand view of the city, and finding a story that could only happen there.

Ragtag understood why Star Wars needs a crew

Amy Hennig’s Ragtag, developed at Visceral Games, is sometimes reduced to the phrase “Uncharted in Star Wars.” That sounds appealing enough, but her explanation of the project was more interesting.

In a 2019 interview with Eurogamer, Hennig described how Star Wars’ ensemble structure would shape the game. It needed multiple protagonists, scenes following the villains and playable sequences happening in parallel. She used the Death Star escape as an example of characters accomplishing different things towards a shared goal.14

That gets at something I value in a good team adventure. A group becomes convincing when its members have things to contribute and reasons to rely on one another. You understand the value of a character’s ability because the situation makes use of it.

It is part of why Mass Effect 2 works so well for me. Spending time with the crew changes how I approach the mission that requires them. There is a practical relationship between getting to know people and trusting them with something important. A Star Wars adventure built around a caper crew could have found a different expression of that pleasure.

Parallel sequences also offer a useful kind of tension. The person opening a route doesn’t necessarily know what is delaying the person meant to use it. Success can depend on timing and trust across several locations. Played well, those changes in viewpoint could make the group feel larger than the character currently under your control.

There would have been difficult questions to solve. How much freedom could each sequence allow? Would switching characters interrupt the player’s momentum? Could the companions contribute convincingly without becoming a series of scripted rescues? Hennig’s account describes a promising direction, while leaving plenty of room for the work required to realise it.

EA closed Visceral in October 2017 and announced that the project’s design would change under new development arrangements. Its public explanation cited feedback, changes in the market and a desire for a broader experience. EA’s CEO subsequently said the decision wasn’t simply a choice between single-player and multiplayer games.1516

Hennig’s own account adds the practical difficulties. She told GamesBeat that Visceral was expensive to operate, that parts of the team had been hired for Battlefield, and that the cinematic traversal the project needed required new functionality in Frostbite. She also described a widening gap between the crafted adventure she was making and the kinds of games EA wanted in its portfolio.17

That makes “EA decided single-player was dead” an inadequate account. Visceral needed suitable tools, a team organised for the task and a publisher committed to the resulting game. Those conditions were entangled. Building a new feature takes time; uncertainty about the game’s commercial direction makes that investment harder to defend. The specific ensemble adventure Hennig described was lost amid those problems.

1313 and Ragtag are separate projects with separate histories. What connects their appeal is the chance to make Star Wars feel occupied by people with work to do and relationships beyond the saga’s central family. I would happily have made room for both.

Scalebound needed the dragon to matter

Scalebound had a premise that was very easy to sell to me: a PlatinumGames action RPG in which you fought alongside a dragon.

Microsoft’s 2015 presentation introduced Drew, transported to Draconis and bonded to Thuban. The advertised design included combining attacks with the dragon and changing its form and abilities. These were development promises, but they established what made the game distinctive.18

The best possibility here was a relationship expressed through combat. A dragon should change the space around you. It should affect which enemies you can approach, what draws their attention and where you can safely stand. If your own attacks create openings for its attacks, and its behaviour creates opportunities for you, the partnership becomes something you learn.

That is a much more interesting goal than having a spectacular creature arrive whenever the game wants a bigger explosion. I would want to finish an encounter with a sense that I had become better at fighting alongside this particular companion. Its size and strength could support the fantasy; its limitations would give that fantasy useful shape.

It also explains why the project’s difficulties deserve attention. A large companion has to work with the environment, respond clearly and remain useful without doing everything for the player. Add the other demands of an RPG and there are a lot of ways for the experience to become awkward.

Microsoft cancelled Scalebound in January 2017. In a later Cutscenes interview, director Hideki Kamiya acknowledged Platinum’s difficulties with an unfamiliar engine and its lack of experience building around online features. He apologised to both players and Microsoft.1920

That account makes it harder to turn the cancellation into a simple story about a publisher refusing to support a developer. There was an ambitious game to build, and the team had struggled with important parts of building it.

I still regret the opportunity. Platinum’s appeal has always included the pleasure of learning how actions fit together. Applying that sensibility to a partnership with a creature of radically different scale could have produced something memorable. I would have been quite happy to tolerate a cocky protagonist if the dragon made good company.

LMNO wanted companionship to change how you played

Steven Spielberg’s involvement makes LMNO an easy project to remember. The design described in Matt Leone’s 2010 investigation for 1UP gives me a better reason to care about it.

The early concept combined first-person movement and escape sequences with an AI companion named Eve. Doug Church led development, with Randy Smith as lead designer. The team wanted Eve’s behaviour to change in response to the player’s treatment of her, with communication relying on more than dialogue.21

That ambition goes directly to one of the things I like most about games: becoming attached to someone because of what happens while we are doing something together. Dialogue can establish personality, but a companion’s decisions during play can establish trust. You start relying on the person who helps at the right moment. You notice when they hesitate.

It is easy to imagine that working beautifully, and just as easy to imagine the frustration. For a relationship expressed through behaviour to mean anything, the player has to understand the behaviour. If Eve fails to help, I need some basis for deciding whether she is frightened, angry, unable to reach me or simply stuck behind a chair.

That problem is part of the attraction. The team was attempting to make the relationship itself affect how an action game unfolded. It deserved experimentation, including the possibility that some of the experiments would fail.

Leone’s reporting also describes an interest within the team in a short, highly replayable structure. The project later changed into a more conventional third-person adventure called The Escape Artist, which was cancelled in 2009; EA publicly confirmed the end of LMNO in 2010.21

I find the shorter proposal especially appealing. A few concentrated hours could make it easier to notice the consequences of treating a companion differently on another attempt. There is room for a game whose replay value comes from understanding an interaction better, rather than clearing the same map again with a new weapon unlocked.

The article doesn’t establish that those ambitions were solved. It records a project that changed substantially while trying to find a workable form. What we lost was an attempt to make companionship carry more of the game, backed by people willing to ask difficult questions about how that could work.

I would still like more games to pursue that question. They wouldn’t need Spielberg’s name to get my attention.

The Crossing tried to put another person inside the encounter

Arkane’s The Crossing offered a very different approach to unpredictable behaviour: let other players supply it.

Blake Hester’s retrospective, based on interviews with the team, describes a campaign whose encounters could include human-controlled opponents. Its setting involved contrasting versions of Paris, with Viktor Antonov helping develop the world. The team had a playable demonstration, and had worked on how campaign progression and multiplayer encounters would fit together.22

A human opponent changes what it means to learn an encounter. The room can be familiar while the decisions inside it remain uncertain. Someone might recognise what worked against them last time, refuse to approach from the obvious direction, or exploit a route you had stopped watching.

That can be exciting. It can also disrupt the reasons someone chose a campaign in the first place. I often want time to look around, understand a place and decide how to proceed. Another player is under no obligation to respect my interest in the architecture. They may quite reasonably prefer to shoot me while I am admiring it.

The interesting design problem is how to preserve the campaign’s purpose while giving the opposing players something worthwhile to do. A human enemy who merely waits to be defeated is wasted. A human enemy who can repeatedly prevent progress may be enjoying a very different game from the person trying to follow the story.

The Crossing’s developers were working through those tensions. The project’s eventual end also involved the conditions under which it could be financed. Colantonio described walking away after prolonged publisher negotiations that combined a lower budget with additional demands, including a PlayStation 3 version. He concluded that the proposed deal would lead the project to failure.22

That is a cancellation story in which the developer made the decision to stop. I find that useful to remember. Continuing under an unworkable arrangement can consume the team needed to make the next game.

There is something worth regretting in The Crossing, especially its attempt to connect a distinctive world to a different encounter structure. There is also room to respect the judgement that the available route to finishing it was a bad one. Wanting to play a game doesn’t give me a better understanding of the contract than the people being asked to sign it.

Silent Hills left us something we can judge

Silent Hills occupies an unusual place in this history because its teaser became a substantial work in its own right.

Released in 2014, P.T. used a repeating domestic space, small changes, disturbing sounds and the threat of a presence the player couldn’t comfortably control. Completing its mystery revealed the proposed collaboration involving Hideo Kojima, Guillermo del Toro and Norman Reedus.23

The constrained setting is central to why it works. Learning a place usually makes it less threatening. You know where the exit is, what the rooms contain and which sounds belong there. P.T. makes that familiarity unstable. Recognising the space gives you more reasons to notice that something has changed.

That is a precise use of the player’s attention. You look for information because you want to understand the rules, then become increasingly uncomfortable with what looking might reveal. Repetition gives small details weight. A sound can make the next few steps difficult without needing to introduce another elaborate monster.

There are reasons to be cautious about extending that achievement to Silent Hills. Konami described the larger project as embryonic when it confirmed cancellation in April 2015. A teaser can organise its effects around a short, concentrated experience. A full game needs a structure that can support discovery, variation and an ending.24

Perhaps the collaboration would have produced something exceptional. Perhaps it would have struggled to maintain the intimacy of the teaser. We don’t have the game that would answer those questions.

What we do have is a persuasive demonstration of creative judgement. P.T. understands how a player’s wish to know more can become part of the fear. That gives the cancellation a firmer basis for regret than an impressive piece of concept art or a list of famous names.

Konami also removed the teaser from the PlayStation Store in April 2015.25 That decision made the loss more frustrating. Preserving an abandoned project’s most meaningful public work would have allowed more people to understand the interest in it for themselves.

I would put Silent Hills among the most painful cancellations here because P.T. established something valuable. I would stop short of awarding the unreleased game the same achievement.

Sometimes the next game needs the cancellation

Blizzard’s Titan complicates any neat argument that an ambitious project should always be allowed to continue.

In his 2016 GameSpot interview, Jeff Kaplan described Titan as a failure and explained how a smaller group regrouped to make Overwatch. His later account on Lex Fridman’s podcast describes an enormous proposed MMO combining secret-agent action, everyday life, businesses and a shared world. He also described fundamental problems with its direction and production.2627

It is easy to hear that concept and construct the version we would have loved. A huge world with a life outside combat sounds attractive. It also sounds like several difficult games whose parts would need to support one another before the whole became worthwhile.

Overwatch is a useful reminder that recognising a failed project can create the conditions for a successful one. That doesn’t make the years spent on Titan painless or automatically worthwhile. It does make it difficult to argue that finishing Titan at any cost would have been the better outcome.

The same restraint belongs in the rest of these stories. Human Head’s confidence in Prey 2 doesn’t settle Bethesda’s concerns. Kamiya’s record doesn’t erase the problems he acknowledged on Scalebound. A promising Warcraft concept doesn’t tell us how far Andromeda had progressed. Each case deserves its own explanation.

There is still a real loss to discuss. People spend years making systems, environments, characters and tools that the public may never see. Some of that work helps a later project. Some remains inaccessible. Even when stopping is the right decision, the work deserves a history more detailed than a place on a list of cancelled titles.

That is why reporting like Schreier’s matters, alongside the interviews, previews and retrospectives that preserve the other accounts here. They let us see the effort and uncertainty behind a name that otherwise becomes a bit of gaming trivia.

The ones I still want to play

If I could put one of these projects back into development with a team and conditions that suited it, I would start with Prey 2. The bounty-hunting activity gives it a clear purpose, and its combination of movement and observation is something I would still like to see explored properly.

StarCraft: Ghost would be close behind. The shift in perspective remains attractive even after accounting for its troubled development. I want to see how that universe feels when getting past a patrol is a problem I have to solve personally.

The prospect behind Andromeda might be the easiest to revisit in spirit. There is enough in Warcraft to support a focused action adventure without trying to recover this particular cancelled production. Give a good team a manageable story, a protagonist worth following and the time to find a satisfying way to play it.

For Ragtag, I keep coming back to the crew. For Scalebound, the partnership. For LMNO and The Crossing, the attempt to make another character’s behaviour change the experience in a meaningful way. P.T. goes further than a proposal: it gives us a small, unsettling piece of work whose larger future was cut short.

I can accept that some of these games needed to end. I can also wish their best ideas had found another chance. What stays with me is the possibility of an hour I would have enjoyed spending in them: following a target across an alien city, learning to fight beside a dragon, or finally standing inside a StarCraft installation and deciding how to get out alive.
