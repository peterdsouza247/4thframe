4 Consoles That Deserved Way Better Than They Got

By Peter D’Souza
LOST SYSTEMS
Fourth Frame
List / 06

Not every commercial failure was actually a bad console. Some of gaming's most interesting hardware got buried by bad timing, bad marketing, or a rival with deeper pockets — not by being bad.

1. Sega Dreamcast

Launched in November 1998, the Dreamcast was the first console with a built-in modem and genuine online console gaming, an entire generation before Xbox Live existed — Phantasy Star Online let players team up and raid dungeons together years before that was a console norm. Its NAOMI arcade board shared near-identical architecture with the home hardware, which is why arcade ports like Crazy Taxi, Soulcalibur, and Marvel vs. Capcom 2 translated almost perfectly to the living room. Sega also bet enormous money on the console's exclusives — Shenmue alone reportedly cost around $70 million to make, one of the most expensive games ever produced at the time. None of it was enough: the Dreamcast sold 9.13 million units total, and Sega discontinued it in March 2001, ending its console business entirely after 18 years in hardware.

2. Neo Geo Pocket Color

SNK's 16-bit handheld launched in the US in 1999 through an unusual exclusive deal with online retailer eToys, priced at $69.95 — cheaper than the Game Boy Color, with a genuine arcade-style microswitched joystick critics loved. Its library leaned on real SNK pedigree: King of Fighters R-2, Metal Slug 1st and 2nd Mission, and SNK vs. Capcom: Card Fighters' Clash. It even had a link cable to connect with the Sega Dreamcast for cross-platform King of Fighters play, and Sega itself published Sonic the Hedgehog: Pocket Adventure for the system — a rare case of one platform holder making an exclusive-feeling game for a rival's hardware. None of it mattered against Game Boy Color's market dominance and the looming Game Boy Advance, and the line died alongside SNK's 2001 bankruptcy.

3. WonderSwan

Designed by Gunpei Yokoi, the same engineer who created the Game Boy, after he left Nintendo following the Virtual Boy's failure. Yokoi died in a car accident in 1997, before WonderSwan ever launched in 1999 — meaning the man who defined handheld gaming never saw his final creation reach players. What he left behind was genuinely impressive: a single AA battery delivered around 40 hours of play, easily beating the Game Boy Color on efficiency, and its dual-orientation design let games run vertically or horizontally off the same cartridge. Squaresoft became its unlikely biggest supporter, porting Final Fantasy I and II and developing Front Mission exclusives — fallout from Square's own very public falling-out with Nintendo during the N64 era. It sold roughly 3.5 million units and carved out close to 10% of the Japanese handheld market, but it never once released outside Japan, and Bandai discontinued the line in 2003 as the Game Boy Advance took over.

4. PlayStation Vita

Sony's 2011 handheld had console-grade hardware — a 5-inch OLED screen, dual analog sticks years before that was standard on portables, a quad-core processor — and a genuinely excellent launch and early library: Uncharted: Golden Abyss, Gravity Rush, and Persona 4 Golden all became system-defining titles. What killed it was largely self-inflicted: Sony locked the Vita to expensive, proprietary memory cards instead of standard SD cards, pricing away exactly the enthusiast audience who wanted it most. Sony quietly wound down its own first-party support by 2015, and the Vita sold somewhere between 10 and 16 million units over its lifetime — a steep fall from the PSP's roughly 76 to 80 million before it. Production ended entirely in 2019.
