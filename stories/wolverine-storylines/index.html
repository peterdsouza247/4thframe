8 Wolverine Comic Storylines That Would Make Incredible Video Games

By Peter D’Souza
WOLVERINE
Fourth Frame
List / 02

Marvel's Wolverine has finally given Logan the game treatment he's deserved for decades, and it's opened the door for more. But the comics have sixty-plus years of material to draw from — and some of the best Wolverine stories would make for genuinely different kinds of games, not just reskins of the same combat-heavy formula. Here's what that could actually look like.

8. Wolverine: Origin (Jenkins/Kubert)

The pitch: a slow-burn narrative game, closer to Red Dead Redemption 2's opening hours than anything superhero. You'd play sickly, aristocratic James Howlett for most of it — walking, talking, small choices — with a single mechanic underneath: a hidden rage meter that occasionally strips control from the player entirely, cutting to black and back with the aftermath already done. The horror isn't fighting monsters, it's not knowing what you just did.

7. Kitty Pryde and Wolverine (Claremont)

The pitch: an asymmetric two-hander, structurally like It Takes Two but reversed — you mostly control teenage Kitty, using her phasing ability as the core traversal and stealth-puzzle mechanic (walk through walls, drop through floors, hide inside solid objects), while Wolverine runs as a protective AI companion who intervenes only when things go physical. The mentor relationship isn't cutscene padding, it's the entire skill tree — Kitty's abilities literally unlock through lessons he teaches her.

6. Uncanny X-Force (Remender)

The pitch: a squad-tactics game in the vein of Marvel's Midnight Suns crossed with Commandos — Wolverine leading a black-ops team (Deadpool, Psylocke, Fantomex, Archangel) on missions the X-Men won't sanction. Permadeath on squadmates, morally grey mission briefs where "success" often means a kill nobody's proud of, and a loyalty meter that tracks how far your team is willing to follow Logan past the line. You're not playing the hero here — you're playing the guy the heroes don't want to know about.

5. Death of Wolverine (Soule)

The pitch: strip the healing factor and you get a survival game, full stop — think Darkest Dungeon's affliction system applied to a single body. Wounds accumulate and don't reset between fights; a broken arm from mission three is still broken in mission five unless you spend real resources treating it. A literal countdown clock (adamantium poisoning) sits on the HUD the entire game. Every fight becomes a resource-management decision, not a power fantasy.

4. Enemy of the State (Millar)

The pitch: a two-act structural swerve. Act one, you play brainwashed Wolverine as a boss-rush spectacle — cutting through iconic heroes with zero restraint, full power fantasy, the game deliberately making you the final boss from someone else's perspective. Act two flips it: you're fighting your own controlled body back from the inside, a corruption-meter mechanic similar to inFAMOUS's karma system but run in reverse, clawing back agency one QTE at a time. Nobody's built a superhero game where you're the villain for the first three hours.

3. Old Man Logan (Millar/McNiven)

The pitch: an open-world road trip in the Death Stranding mould — traversal and resource scarcity over combat, a blind, pacifist Logan crossing a wasteland America controlled by supervillain fiefdoms. The claws are narratively locked behind a single story-critical choice partway through, à la God of War's Leviathan Axe restraint — meaning for hours, the game is about choosing not to fight, which is the whole point of the story.

2. Weapon X (Barry Windsor-Smith)

The pitch: stealth-horror, closer to Alien: Isolation or Outlast than anything Marvel's made. Logan wakes up mid-experiment with no memory, no reliable healing factor awareness, and fragmented control of his own body. No boss fights — the entire game is an escape, told through scattered memory fragments instead of a HUD objective marker. This would be the first Wolverine game where being hurt actually feels dangerous.

1. Wolverine (1982 miniseries, Claremont/Miller)

The pitch: a tight, single-region narrative-action game — think Ghost of Tsushima's scale, not its size — set entirely in Japan, with a Sekiro-style parry-and-posture combat system built around honour duels rather than crowd-clearing. Logan's relationship with Mariko Yashida drives side content and unlocks story-critical choices, and the whole thing runs maybe 15–20 hours instead of a sprawling open world. Still the best standalone Wolverine story ever told, and still nobody's built the tighter, more personal game it's obviously asking for.
