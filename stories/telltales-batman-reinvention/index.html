Telltale’s Batman Understood That Reinvention Begins With the People Around Bruce Wayne

As Absolute Batman finds new life in familiar characters, it is worth revisiting the game that made Gotham’s most famous relationships feel uncertain again.

By Peter D’Souza
GOTHAM
Fourth Frame
Games / Batman

Spoilers for Batman: The Telltale Series and Batman: The Enemy Within, including their major character reveals and ending paths. Mild premise spoilers for Absolute Batman.

In most Batman stories, finding out that the Penguin used to be Bruce Wayne’s friend would be the twist. In Telltale’s Gotham, it is only the beginning. Harvey Dent has a place in Bruce’s life before he becomes a problem for Batman. The Wayne name that opens doors for Bruce also hides a history of harm. Even the man we recognise as the future Joker first appears as someone who wants a friend.

We know many of these characters’ names, costumes and likely destinations. Telltale’s two-season Batman series finds a way to make that knowledge unreliable. It asks what Bruce owes someone he once trusted, what he should do when his family helped create Gotham’s suffering, and whether believing in a friend can become a dangerous form of wishful thinking. Its versions of Batman’s supporting cast are compelling because their changes put pressure on his choices.

That question feels especially timely while Scott Snyder and Nick Dragotta’s Absolute Batman is drawing attention to another radically revised Gotham. DC called its first issue the bestselling comic of 2024, and the series has kept finding fresh uses for Bruce’s oldest relationships. The two continuities are doing different things. Absolute Batman imagines a working-class Bruce without inherited billions; Telltale keeps the billionaire and makes the inheritance itself morally unstable. Both show how much room there still is inside a character we think we know.

The Wayne name becomes a problem Bruce cannot punch

The most consequential change in Telltale’s Gotham is not a new costume or an unexpected villain reveal. It is Thomas Wayne. Bruce discovers that the father he has built his life around was implicated in Gotham’s criminal machinery. DC’s description of the story’s comic continuation makes the lasting damage plain: after the first season, Bruce is trying to repair the Wayne name and reckon with families harmed by Thomas’s actions.

Batman’s origin usually supplies him with moral clarity. A child witnesses an injustice, grows up, and dedicates his life to preventing it. Telltale leaves that motivation intact while complicating the story Bruce tells about his parents. His money, his company and his public standing can no longer serve as uncomplicated tools for doing good. Some of the city’s wounds were made by the institution that equipped him to help.

That is more interesting than turning Thomas into a twist villain for shock value. Bruce cannot undo the past, and arresting a present-day criminal will not make the question go away. He has to decide whether being Batman includes accepting responsibility for damage he did not personally choose but from which he benefited. The conflict gives Bruce Wayne’s scenes a weight that many adaptations reserve for the man in the cowl.

It also changes how Gotham looks. The city’s corruption is no longer simply an infestation Batman can remove from outside. It runs through family wealth, public influence and personal loyalties. Telltale’s Bruce has resources; what he lacks is the comfort of believing those resources were clean.

Harvey and Oswald are painful because they knew Bruce first

Harvey Dent works best when his fall costs Bruce something beyond a useful ally. Telltale gives their relationship room before Two-Face can take over the story. Harvey is a friend with political hopes, someone whose future Bruce has reason to imagine differently. As the pressures around him grow, the familiar name “Two-Face” stops feeling like a destination on a character sheet and starts feeling like the possibility of losing someone Bruce actually knew.

The game cannot make every tragic outcome avoidable, and it does not need to. The tension lies in how we behave when we know more than Bruce can know. Do we try to protect Harvey, challenge him, trust him with the truth, or pull away when he becomes difficult to reach? A familiar transformation gains force when it passes through ordinary acts of friendship first.

Oswald Cobblepot benefits from an equally personal change. Rather than entering the story merely as the Penguin, he arrives with a past shared with Bruce and a grievance tied to Gotham’s privileged families. Their history makes him more than a rogue in need of capture. He can accuse Bruce of inhabiting the very social order he claims to oppose, and Bruce cannot dismiss every part of that accusation just because Oswald’s methods become monstrous.

The game’s Lady Arkham reveal pushes the same idea further. The reporter Vicki Vale is not simply another ally who has been hiding a mask. Her anger grows out of what powerful people did to vulnerable families. The reveal is melodramatic, and some of its mechanics are rough, but its purpose is clear: the costs of Gotham’s respectable past have names and faces. Bruce’s greatest blind spot is his assumption that the Wayne legacy means to others what it meant to him.

These characters are memorable as a group because their stories connect. Thomas’s sins are not background lore. They alter the meaning of Bruce’s friendship with Oswald, the public credibility of his support for Harvey, and the conflict with Vicki. Gotham feels less like a collection of famous adversaries waiting in separate rooms and more like a place where one family’s actions have travelled through other lives.

Selina and Alfred make the mask harder to hide behind

Telltale also understands that the best supporting characters should challenge Bruce without needing to become his enemies. Selina Kyle can meet him as Catwoman and as herself. The attraction matters, but so does the uncertainty of whether either of them is being completely honest. She has her own interests and survival instincts; Bruce cannot make her reliable simply by deciding he wants to trust her.

That gives their relationship a welcome friction. Telltale repeatedly makes Bruce navigate situations where the easiest decision for Batman may be a poor decision for Bruce’s relationship with someone who sees through him. Selina is compelling because she refuses to function as a prize for playing the hero correctly. Trust is negotiated, and sometimes it comes with a cost.

Alfred’s role is quieter and, by the end, even more piercing. He knows the Wayne family’s history and remains beside Bruce as that history changes shape. He can support the mission while still questioning what it asks of the person he raised. The series’ final dilemma places their relationship beside Bruce’s commitment to Batman, asking whether he can keep both on the terms he has chosen.

For a character often treated as an endlessly dependable voice in the earpiece, that is a meaningful change. Alfred is not merely a moral instruction manual. He is a person with limits. Making those limits visible turns Bruce’s devotion to the mission from an automatically admirable trait into something we are allowed to examine.

John Doe makes knowing the ending feel dangerous

Then there is John Doe. Almost everyone who encounters him knows which famous name waits at the end of the road. The Enemy Within uses that knowledge against us. John is awkward, eager for Bruce’s attention and difficult to read. Bruce needs access to his criminal circle, but their conversations can feel uncomfortably like a friendship. The player has to decide how much of that friendship is real.

Telltale’s season lead writer described the central questions: is Bruce treating John as a friend, using him to get closer to the Pact, or doing some uneasy mixture of both? Harley Quinn’s presence sharpens the tension. John wants her affection, seeks Bruce’s advice and looks to both of them for a model of who he should become. Harley has her own force and agency here; she is not waiting to be defined by the Joker we expect.

The choice that follows is the series’ boldest piece of design. John can become a villain who turns his hurt against Bruce, or a vigilante who tries to imitate Batman without understanding the restraint that makes Batman worth imitating. These are two substantially different final stories, developed because the creative team found that John’s possible goals no longer fit inside one shared finale.

Neither route makes Bruce omnipotent. John remains responsible for what he does. But the split gives the player a reason to examine their own conduct: when did empathy become indulgence, and when did necessary suspicion become manipulation? Most Batman adaptations can show us why the Joker is frightening. Telltale asks why Bruce might have wanted, however briefly, to believe this particular person could have been someone else.

That is why John works better here than a mere origin-story trick. The game does not simply hand us a new answer to an old mystery. It lets the expected answer hang over a relationship until our certainty becomes part of the tragedy.

What Telltale and Absolute Batman have in common

Absolute Batman starts from another premise. In a conversation with DC, Snyder described asking who Bruce would be without the advantages of inherited wealth. This Bruce’s friendships are rooted in the neighbourhood where he grew up. Harvey, Oswald, Edward Nygma and Waylon Jones are part of his social world before their names become possible threats. DC’s look at the series’ rogues describes how those bonds become painful as the story develops.

The parallel is specific, but the interpretations should not be flattened into one idea. Telltale’s Bruce still has the manor and the money; Absolute Batman’s class politics depend on taking them away. Absolute’s larger, more physical reinventions are drawn for a comic with its own pace and visual language. Telltale’s quieter changes work because a game can make you choose what to say to an old friend and then make you live with the tone you chose.

What they share is a belief that a Batman story becomes more powerful when changing one relationship changes the whole city around it. A new silhouette may get our attention. A new history between Bruce and Harvey, or Bruce and Oswald, makes us wonder what each man will do when that history no longer protects them.

The best change is one that reveals Bruce

Telltale’s games have their limits. Their action can feel stiff, some choices reconverge quickly, and the first season occasionally favours a dramatic reveal over the patient work of earning it. The praise does not require pretending every branch is equally consequential. It rests on the places where the form and the character meet: a conversation that matters because Bruce is both investigator and friend; a family secret that makes heroism less comfortable; a Joker finale that changes because the relationship leading to it changed.

There will always be room for another Batman. The question is whether a new version has something to say beyond being new. Telltale’s answer was to make Bruce’s certainty about other people and about his own family fragile. He is still recognisably Batman: stubborn, capable, desperate to help Gotham. But being Batman does not excuse him from seeing what his family did, what his friends need, or what his own choices may be doing to them.

That is the version worth revisiting as Absolute Batman continues to redraw Gotham. Reinvention works when we come away understanding the old character more deeply. Telltale did not need to take away Bruce’s money to make him vulnerable. It made him answer to the people who knew his name before they feared the bat.
