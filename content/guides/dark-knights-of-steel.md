Batman makes immediate sense as a knight. Superman as a member of a royal dynasty is a more troubling proposition. If a family can hear conversations across a kingdom and fly over its fortifications, what exactly keeps its subjects safe from the crown?

*Dark Knights of Steel* builds a fantasy world around questions like that. Tom Taylor and Yasmine Putri relocate DC's heroes into courts, castles and rival kingdoms, where bloodlines and prophecy shape public life. Recognising a familiar face helps, but trusting what you remember about that face can get you into trouble.

**Start with the original twelve issues.** Buy Volumes 1 and 2, or the Deluxe Edition that contains both halves and the *Tales from the Three Kingdoms* special. Then choose whether to explore *Allwinter* and continue into *Dark Knights of Steel II*. You need no earlier Batman, Superman or Justice League reading.

## What kind of DC universe is this?

This is an Elseworlds setting: its own continuity, with its own versions of the characters. It has no reading dependency on DC's main universe or the Absolute books. Think of it as a fantasy adaptation of the DC cast, with permission to change their relationships, histories and loyalties.

The central premise asks what happens when extraordinary powers become part of dynastic politics. The House of El, the Kingdom of Storms and the Amazons share an uneasy world. Fear of magic and outsiders makes that world combustible. The opening conflict turns suspicion into a struggle that none of the factions can fully control.

The pleasure comes from watching familiar heroic instincts operate inside institutions that complicate them. Loyalty to a ruler can conflict with protecting the vulnerable. A prophecy can influence events because people believe it. Someone wearing a reassuringly familiar symbol may serve a very different purpose here.

This is also a fast-moving superhero adventure. Expect revelations, battles and reversals rather than a leisurely tour of agricultural policy. The costumes have changed; the appetite for an alarming final page remains healthy.

## The reading route

1. **Dark Knights of Steel #1–6.** Meet the kingdoms and follow the opening crisis. This is Volume 1.
2. **Dark Knights of Steel #7–12.** Finish the original story. Volume 2 includes these issues and *Tales from the Three Kingdoms* #1.
3. **Tales from the Three Kingdoms #1, if your edition has not already included it.** Background stories expand the cast. A first-time reader can safely follow the placement in their collection; this guide recommends the main story first when reading loose issues.
4. **Dark Knights of Steel: Allwinter #1–6.** An optional northern branch, with a Deathstroke story and an Aquaman origin running alongside it. Read it before the sequel for a broader introduction to this world.
5. **Heir to the Sea #1, only if you still need its story.** This 2026 special reprints the Aquaman origin from *Allwinter*. It is a convenient bridge for readers who skipped that book.
6. **Dark Knights of Steel II #1 onward.** Return to the central saga after the original twelve issues. The sequel is a twelve-issue series; it is still in progress at this guide's review date.

Steps 1 and 2 are the essential first story. Step 3 is extra context. The Deathstroke narrative in step 4 is an expansion; its Aquaman companion story is particularly useful before step 6. Step 5 provides that companion material through a separate purchase. There is no reason to buy it twice just to satisfy a checklist.

The numbers above are a reader-friendly route, not a claim that every story takes place in that chronological order. Prequels often work better after you have met the people whose pasts they explain.

## The people and factions to recognise

### Bruce Wayne and the House of El

Bruce's position as a knight places him close to the ruling family and its security. His suspicion of dangerous powers matters differently in a world where those powers may belong to the people he serves. Treat his role here as a fresh introduction. The story will tell you what you need to know about his place in the kingdom.

The Els embody the promise and anxiety of superhuman monarchy. Their ability to protect a realm also gives neighbouring powers a reason to fear them. Keep an eye on the distinction between what the family intends and what other people believe it could do.

### The Kingdom of Storms

This rival power makes prophecy and the fear of catastrophe central to the political conflict. The relevant question is how its leaders act on what they think they know. You do not need a family-tree chart before beginning; concentrate on the decisions that make a feared future more likely.

### The Amazons

The Amazons bring another centre of power, with their own relationships and interests. Their involvement prevents the story from becoming a simple dispute between two crowns. Diana belongs to this version of the world; importing every assumption from mainstream Wonder Woman will obscure some of the surprises.

### Magic users and outsiders

Look beyond the courts. A kingdom's treatment of people it considers dangerous tells you something that its official account of itself may leave out. The story's familiar DC identities make these encounters approachable, but their political position is what gives them weight.

### Deathstroke and Aquaman in Allwinter

*Allwinter* shifts the scale toward a hired killer drawn into a protective role in a frozen northern landscape. Jay Kristoff and Tirso handle the Deathstroke narrative. Tom Taylor and Riccardo Federici tell the Aquaman origin included alongside it. These are distinct stories within the same volume, which explains why the later Aquaman reprint overlaps with only part of the book.

## What Allwinter adds

The first series is concerned with courts and war between realms. *Allwinter* lets another part of the setting establish its own atmosphere. Its wintry, largely monochrome visual approach gives the Deathstroke material an identity beyond a change of location.

Choose it for a harsher fantasy journey, a reluctant guardian story, or a wider view of the world. You can understand the original twelve issues without it. Readers continuing toward the sequel should pay attention to its Aquaman material, which DC repackaged specifically as a companion to the next chapter.

## Where the sequel fits

*Dark Knights of Steel II* follows the first saga. Tom Taylor returns as writer, with Otto Schmidt on art, Arif Prianto on colours and Wes Abbott on letters. DC announced a July 2026 launch and a twelve-issue run. Its premise expands the setting toward an undersea conflict while placing pressure on the alliances created by the original story.

Read it after the original ending. Even the sequel's promotional copy assumes you know how the first series changes the political landscape. If you want to preserve those discoveries, stop browsing sequel solicitations until you have finished issue #12.

## Common questions

### Is this connected to Absolute Batman?

No reading dependency. These are separate versions of DC's characters. For the other experiment, use our [Absolute Universe guide](../../guides/absolute-universe/).

### Is it just Game of Thrones with superheroes?

Court conflict, uncertain loyalties and violence make the comparison understandable. The familiar heroes also bring a more openly heroic tradition into the setting. Go in expecting a fusion of superhero storytelling and fantasy, rather than a promise that either genre has disappeared.

### Do I need to recognise every character?

No. Recognition adds a second layer of enjoyment, but the main narrative introduces the relevant roles. Avoid character-wiki searches during the first read: the identity you are trying to confirm may be the discovery the next issue is building toward.

### Can I stop after the original story?

Yes. Its twelve issues form the first complete saga. Read the expansions because you want more of the world. Buying Volume 1 does not commit you to every future book with a sword on the cover.
