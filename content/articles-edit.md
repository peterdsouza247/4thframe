:::spoiler-note

Spoilers for Batman: The Telltale Series and Batman: The Enemy Within, including their major character reveals and ending paths. Mild premise spoilers for Absolute Batman.

:::end-note

In most Batman stories, finding out that the Penguin used to be Bruce Wayne’s friend would be the twist. In Telltale’s Gotham, it is only the beginning. Harvey Dent has a place in Bruce’s life before he becomes a problem for Batman. The Wayne name that opens doors for Bruce also hides a history of harm. Even the man we recognise as the future Joker first appears as someone who wants a friend.

We know many of these characters’ names, costumes and likely destinations. Telltale’s two-season Batman series finds a way to make that knowledge unreliable. It asks what Bruce owes someone he once trusted, what he should do when his family helped create Gotham’s suffering, and whether believing in a friend can become a dangerous form of wishful thinking. Its versions of Batman’s supporting cast are compelling because their changes put pressure on *his* choices.

That question feels especially timely while Scott Snyder and Nick Dragotta’s *Absolute Batman* is drawing attention to another radically revised Gotham. DC [called its first issue the bestselling comic of 2024](https://www.dc.com/blog/2024/11/18/absolute-batman-1-is-2024-s-bestselling-comic-book), and the series has kept finding fresh uses for Bruce’s oldest relationships. The two continuities are doing different things. Absolute Batman imagines a working-class Bruce without inherited billions; Telltale keeps the billionaire and makes the inheritance itself morally unstable. Both show how much room there still is inside a character we think we know.

## The Wayne name becomes a problem Bruce cannot punch

The most consequential change in Telltale’s Gotham is not a new costume or an unexpected villain reveal. It is Thomas Wayne. Bruce discovers that the father he has built his life around was implicated in Gotham’s criminal machinery. DC’s [description of the story’s comic continuation](https://www.dc.com/blog/2017/11/09/acclaimed-batman-telltale-video-game-expands-the-story-with-comic-book-miniseries-batman-sins-of-the-father) makes the lasting damage plain: after the first season, Bruce is trying to repair the Wayne name and reckon with families harmed by Thomas’s actions.

Batman’s origin usually supplies him with moral clarity. A child witnesses an injustice, grows up, and dedicates his life to preventing it. Telltale leaves that motivation intact while complicating the story Bruce tells about his parents. His money, his company and his public standing can no longer serve as uncomplicated tools for doing good. Some of the city’s wounds were made by the institution that equipped him to help.

That is more interesting than turning Thomas into a twist villain for shock value. Bruce cannot undo the past, and arresting a present-day criminal will not make the question go away. He has to decide whether being Batman includes accepting responsibility for damage he did not personally choose but from which he benefited. The conflict gives Bruce Wayne’s scenes a weight that many adaptations reserve for the man in the cowl.

It also changes how Gotham looks. The city’s corruption is no longer simply an infestation Batman can remove from outside. It runs through family wealth, public influence and personal loyalties. Telltale’s Bruce has resources; what he lacks is the comfort of believing those resources were clean.

## Harvey and Oswald are painful because they knew Bruce first

Harvey Dent works best when his fall costs Bruce something beyond a useful ally. Telltale gives their relationship room before Two-Face can take over the story. Harvey is a friend with political hopes, someone whose future Bruce has reason to imagine differently. As the pressures around him grow, the familiar name “Two-Face” stops feeling like a destination on a character sheet and starts feeling like the possibility of losing someone Bruce actually knew.

The game cannot make every tragic outcome avoidable, and it does not need to. The tension lies in how we behave when we know more than Bruce can know. Do we try to protect Harvey, challenge him, trust him with the truth, or pull away when he becomes difficult to reach? A familiar transformation gains force when it passes through ordinary acts of friendship first.

Oswald Cobblepot benefits from an equally personal change. Rather than entering the story merely as the Penguin, he arrives with a past shared with Bruce and a grievance tied to Gotham’s privileged families. Their history makes him more than a rogue in need of capture. He can accuse Bruce of inhabiting the very social order he claims to oppose, and Bruce cannot dismiss every part of that accusation just because Oswald’s methods become monstrous.

The game’s Lady Arkham reveal pushes the same idea further. The reporter Vicki Vale is not simply another ally who has been hiding a mask. Her anger grows out of what powerful people did to vulnerable families. The reveal is melodramatic, and some of its mechanics are rough, but its purpose is clear: the costs of Gotham’s respectable past have names and faces. Bruce’s greatest blind spot is his assumption that the Wayne legacy means to others what it meant to him.

These characters are memorable as a group because their stories connect. Thomas’s sins are not background lore. They alter the meaning of Bruce’s friendship with Oswald, the public credibility of his support for Harvey, and the conflict with Vicki. Gotham feels less like a collection of famous adversaries waiting in separate rooms and more like a place where one family’s actions have travelled through other lives.

## Selina and Alfred make the mask harder to hide behind

Telltale also understands that the best supporting characters should challenge Bruce without needing to become his enemies. Selina Kyle can meet him as Catwoman and as herself. The attraction matters, but so does the uncertainty of whether either of them is being completely honest. She has her own interests and survival instincts; Bruce cannot make her reliable simply by deciding he wants to trust her.

That gives their relationship a welcome friction. Telltale repeatedly makes Bruce navigate situations where the easiest decision for Batman may be a poor decision for Bruce’s relationship with someone who sees through him. Selina is compelling because she refuses to function as a prize for playing the hero correctly. Trust is negotiated, and sometimes it comes with a cost.

Alfred’s role is quieter and, by the end, even more piercing. He knows the Wayne family’s history and remains beside Bruce as that history changes shape. He can support the mission while still questioning what it asks of the person he raised. The series’ final dilemma places their relationship beside Bruce’s commitment to Batman, asking whether he can keep both on the terms he has chosen.

For a character often treated as an endlessly dependable voice in the earpiece, that is a meaningful change. Alfred is not merely a moral instruction manual. He is a person with limits. Making those limits visible turns Bruce’s devotion to the mission from an automatically admirable trait into something we are allowed to examine.

## John Doe makes knowing the ending feel dangerous

Then there is John Doe. Almost everyone who encounters him knows which famous name waits at the end of the road. *The Enemy Within* uses that knowledge against us. John is awkward, eager for Bruce’s attention and difficult to read. Bruce needs access to his criminal circle, but their conversations can feel uncomfortably like a friendship. The player has to decide how much of that friendship is real.

Telltale’s season lead writer [described the central questions](https://blog.playstation.com/2018/03/13/how-telltale-crafted-a-completely-new-version-of-batmans-most-iconic-villain/): is Bruce treating John as a friend, using him to get closer to the Pact, or doing some uneasy mixture of both? Harley Quinn’s presence sharpens the tension. John wants her affection, seeks Bruce’s advice and looks to both of them for a model of who he should become. Harley has her own force and agency here; she is not waiting to be defined by the Joker we expect.

The choice that follows is the series’ boldest piece of design. John can become a villain who turns his hurt against Bruce, or a vigilante who tries to imitate Batman without understanding the restraint that makes Batman worth imitating. These are [two substantially different final stories](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2018/03/22/batman-the-enemy-within-season-finale-feature/amp/), developed because the creative team found that John’s possible goals no longer fit inside one shared finale.

Neither route makes Bruce omnipotent. John remains responsible for what he does. But the split gives the player a reason to examine their own conduct: when did empathy become indulgence, and when did necessary suspicion become manipulation? Most Batman adaptations can show us why the Joker is frightening. Telltale asks why Bruce might have wanted, however briefly, to believe this particular person could have been someone else.

That is why John works better here than a mere origin-story trick. The game does not simply hand us a new answer to an old mystery. It lets the expected answer hang over a relationship until our certainty becomes part of the tragedy.

## What Telltale and Absolute Batman have in common

*Absolute Batman* starts from another premise. In a [conversation with DC](https://www.dc.com/blog/2024/10/11/absolute-interview-discovering-the-fears-of-a-modern-batman), Snyder described asking who Bruce would be without the advantages of inherited wealth. This Bruce’s friendships are rooted in the neighbourhood where he grew up. Harvey, Oswald, Edward Nygma and Waylon Jones are part of his social world before their names become possible threats. DC’s [look at the series’ rogues](https://www.dc.com/blog/2026-01-28/we-rank-absolute-batman-s-rogues-gallery) describes how those bonds become painful as the story develops.

The parallel is specific, but the interpretations should not be flattened into one idea. Telltale’s Bruce still has the manor and the money; Absolute Batman’s class politics depend on taking them away. Absolute’s larger, more physical reinventions are drawn for a comic with its own pace and visual language. Telltale’s quieter changes work because a game can make you choose what to say to an old friend and then make you live with the tone you chose.

What they share is a belief that a Batman story becomes more powerful when changing one relationship changes the whole city around it. A new silhouette may get our attention. A new history between Bruce and Harvey, or Bruce and Oswald, makes us wonder what each man will do when that history no longer protects them.

## The best change is one that reveals Bruce

Telltale’s games have their limits. Their action can feel stiff, some choices reconverge quickly, and the first season occasionally favours a dramatic reveal over the patient work of earning it. The praise does not require pretending every branch is equally consequential. It rests on the places where the form and the character meet: a conversation that matters because Bruce is both investigator and friend; a family secret that makes heroism less comfortable; a Joker finale that changes because the relationship leading to it changed.

There will always be room for another Batman. The question is whether a new version has something to say beyond being new. Telltale’s answer was to make Bruce’s certainty about other people and about his own family fragile. He is still recognisably Batman: stubborn, capable, desperate to help Gotham. But being Batman does not excuse him from seeing what his family did, what his friends need, or what his own choices may be doing to them.

That is the version worth revisiting as *Absolute Batman* continues to redraw Gotham. Reinvention works when we come away understanding the old character more deeply. Telltale did not need to take away Bruce’s money to make him vulnerable. It made him answer to the people who knew his name before they feared the bat.

Whenever Halo's future comes up, I find myself thinking less about what it should add and more about what it once made me feel. I remember stepping into an open space, seeing enemies move across it, and realising there was no single correct way through. I could take a vehicle, circle around, try a different weapon, or make a terrible plan work through sheer luck. Then a narrow corridor would close in around me, and that sense of freedom would give way to dread.

That rhythm is still compelling: the power fantasy of being a Spartan, the tactical thinking of a sandbox fight, and the science fiction horror waiting at the edge of the light. I want a new Halo that understands all three.

## Start with a campaign worth replaying

Give me a focused campaign of roughly ten to fifteen hours. Every mission should have a clear primary objective. Around it, offer optional objectives, routes, and opportunities that change how I reach that goal. Securing a communications array might reveal a safer path. Rescuing a stranded squad might bring allies into a later battle. Leaving either undone should still let me finish, but the choice should matter.

The best Halo encounters feel like a set of interacting possibilities, not a script waiting for the player to stand in the right place. A broad battlefield makes me experiment with equipment and terrain. A cramped passage makes every sound and shadow count. Both belong in the same game. An open map alone does not create freedom; the missions inside it need to respond to what I do.

## Let me make my own Spartan

Master Chief is an icon, and his relationship with Cortana gave the series its heart. I would still welcome a new protagonist: a Spartan I can build through loadout choices and carry through the campaign. Customisation should shape the way I approach a fight, without turning the game into a spreadsheet or forcing me to grind for the tools I need.

Give that Spartan a new AI companion, with a little room to customise its character. The point is not to copy Chief and Cortana. It is to make space for a bond of our own: an intelligence that learns how I operate, challenges a risky call, and remembers what happened when it went wrong. That relationship could make the choices between battles feel as important as the choices during them.

## Keep the enemies surprising

The Covenant works because its variety changes the question raised in a fight. A familiar enemy can still be dangerous when the terrain, weapons and other threats are thrown into the mix. Preserve that texture. Then let the Flood bring back the unnerving shift from confidence to dread. Horror lands hardest when I have learned to feel strong and the game suddenly gives me a reason to doubt that strength.

I don't need a battle pass to keep returning to Halo. I need encounters I want to replay because there is another route to try, another combination of tools to test, and another consequence to discover. A self-contained campaign can make a far stronger impression than a system designed to keep me checking in.

## Make victory visible

Most of all, I want to feel the impact of my soldier on the world. If I fought to protect a settlement, let me see its lights still burning later. If I chose one objective over another, let the next mission acknowledge what was lost. At the end, I want to look back at the campaign and be stunned by what my Spartan changed.

Halo already knows how to make a single battle feel enormous. Its next step is to make the journey through those battles feel personal. Give me a world worth saving. Then let me decide how to save it.

I was slightly worried about replaying Mass Effect. Some games remain vivid in memory because memory quietly edits out the awkward parts. I remembered the Normandy, the conversations, the impossible decisions and the feeling of arriving somewhere unfamiliar with a crew I trusted. I did not know whether the games themselves would still hold up once the nostalgia wore off.

They do, though not in every way I remembered. The first game's combat can feel stiff. The second moves with greater confidence but trims some of the first game's role-playing texture. The third has to carry the weight of every promise the trilogy made, and its ending remains difficult to separate from the rest. Yet the moments that matter most to me are still there.

## The galaxy has a human scale

Mass Effect sells the size of its universe through small conversations. You can read about its history, but the setting becomes real when someone from another world tells you what that history did to them. The Normandy is the place where politics becomes personal: a ship full of people who disagree about the galaxy and still have to rely on one another.

That is why I kept checking in with the crew between missions. Jack, in particular, is more than a dramatic backstory and a set of combat abilities. Her anger makes sense in the context of what was done to her. Getting to know her asks for patience rather than a quick fix. Characters like her give the bigger conflict something intimate to protect.

## Choices need people attached to them

Virmire still works because it refuses to let a strategic decision remain abstract. The mission asks you to choose under pressure, then live with the absence that choice leaves aboard the ship. You do not need a branching flowchart on screen to understand its weight. An empty place in a familiar group tells the story.

The suicide mission in Mass Effect 2 takes a different approach. It turns everything you have learned about your crew into a test of judgment. You have spent hours helping these people, learning what they can do and what they fear. When you assign them roles, the plan is yours. The tension comes from knowing that expertise, loyalty, and your own confidence may not be enough.

These are two of the trilogy's strongest sequences because the mechanics and the relationships are pulling in the same direction. The game does not merely tell me that my choices matter. It gives me people whose fates I care enough to consider.

## What has aged, and what hasn't

Playing the trilogy back to back makes its changes obvious. The first game gives exploration and worldbuilding room to breathe, even when moving and shooting feel cumbersome. The second is leaner, with a sharper focus on the crew. The third offers its own moments of closure while struggling with the burden of resolving a galaxy's worth of decisions. I still have reservations about its final stretch.

None of that made me wish I had left Mass Effect in my memory. The unevenness is part of the experience of revisiting a long series: each game tries to solve a different problem, and each succeeds at something the others do not. What persists across all three is the sense that the people I met on the way changed how I saw the destination.

After replaying it in 2026, I would not call the trilogy flawless. I would call it worth returning to. I came back for a story about saving the galaxy and stayed for the conversations, loyalties, mistakes and goodbyes that gave that task meaning. For me, that's enough.

title: "Warcraft III Made Its Heroes Matter by Putting Them in Your Hands"
description: "Why Warcraft III's heroes gave its sweeping strategy story a personal stake."
summary: "When Arthas is in danger, you're the one who has to get him out."
deck: "With Forsaken Kingdom returning to Lordaeron, it is worth revisiting the design choice that gave Warcraft III its most memorable characters."

When an ordinary soldier dies in *Warcraft III*, the battle moves on. When your hero is surrounded, everything stops. You pull units back, cast the spell you were saving, and search the inventory for something that might keep them alive. For a moment, managing an army becomes protecting a person.

That is one of the reasons *Warcraft III* has stayed with me. It told a sweeping fantasy story about kingdoms, invasions and the end of the world, but it kept bringing that story back to the units I was personally responsible for. I could watch Arthas make a terrible decision in a cutscene. I also had to select him, move him and fight through the consequences. The distance between spectator and participant grew uncomfortably small.

The release of *Warcraft III Reforged: Forsaken Kingdom* makes that old design choice newly relevant. Blizzard has returned to Lordaeron with a campaign about the Forsaken and their emergence from the Scourge. I have not played the new campaign, so I cannot judge whether it succeeds. What interests me is that Blizzard is once again building a Warcraft story around heroes you guide through a changing world. To understand why that matters, it helps to look at what the original game did so well.

## A character you could command

Earlier strategy games certainly had named characters. *Warcraft III* made its heroes part of the moment-to-moment language of play. They gained experience, learned abilities, carried items and could change the outcome of a fight. In the campaign, they travelled with you from mission to mission. Their growth gave a long story a sense of continuity even as bases fell and armies were rebuilt. [Blizzard's original hero guide](https://classic.battle.net/war3/basics/heroes.shtml) describes those systems plainly; their effect was anything but plain.

A hero also changed where I looked. I still needed to gather resources, build units and read the map. But the centre of a battle was often a particular character and what they could do next. An ability might save a retreat, turn a skirmish or open a path through an enemy force. Choosing when to commit that character created a kind of tension that a larger army alone could not supply.

This was a clever bridge between real-time strategy and role-playing. The hero was powerful enough to feel distinct, but vulnerable enough to need an army. The soldiers gave scale to the conflict; the hero gave me a reason to care about the shape of it. Even an item found along the way could make the next encounter feel like part of the same journey rather than another isolated match.

## When the mission made the story playable

Arthas is the clearest example of that relationship between character and control. His story is often remembered as a sequence of dramatic choices, particularly the culling of Stratholme. But its power also comes from the fact that *Warcraft III* asks you to carry on playing as him. There is no clean point at which the story stops and the strategy game begins again. His determination becomes the work of issuing orders.

That does not mean the player chose Arthas's fate. The tragedy has a direction of its own. The tension comes from doing what the mission requires while understanding more and more about the person whose orders you are carrying out. A game can make us feel close to a character through dialogue. *Warcraft III* did it through responsibility: keep him alive, use his abilities, finish the objective, and then face where the campaign takes him.

This is where its worldbuilding did its best work. Lordaeron was more than a name in a history book because its towns and people became places and units inside missions. Its collapse happened through changes in the enemies you fought, the forces you commanded and the heroes you followed. When the perspective shifted, the world grew larger without feeling like a separate story. You had already seen part of the damage from another side.

## The point where strategy became adventure

*The Frozen Throne* pushed the mixture further with Rexxar's campaign. Its more open structure invited exploration and placed even more weight on hero progression. It was a departure from building a base and marching an army across the map, but it made sense because *Warcraft III* had already taught players to form an attachment to a controllable character. The adventure did not arrive from nowhere. It grew out of a system the game had been developing all along.

That precedent matters now. According to [Blizzard's campaign deep dive](https://news.blizzard.com/en-us/article/24298590/warcraft-iii-reforged-forsaken-kingdom-deep-dive-recap), *Forsaken Kingdom* begins with a four-mission, more traditional prologue before moving into a softer, open RPG structure inspired by Rexxar. Blizzard describes expanded inventory, gear slots, hero kits and talents. Those are descriptions of its design, not a verdict on how satisfying it is to play. But the choice points toward a particular understanding of *Warcraft III*: its characters and its systems were strongest when they worked together.

There is a risk in taking that idea too far. If heroes become so capable that the rest of the army feels incidental, something essential to *Warcraft III* disappears. My favourite version of its design lives in the tension between the two scales. I want to care about the hero at the centre of the screen and the people fighting beside them. I want a new ability to change my plan, not eliminate the need for one.

## A world built through action

*Warcraft III* did not make its characters memorable simply by giving them more dialogue or more lore. It made them matter to the way I played. Their strengths shaped my tactics. Their danger made me pay attention. Their journeys gave continuity to a campaign that might otherwise have felt like a series of separate battles.

I do not know yet whether *Forsaken Kingdom* recaptures that balance. Its return is a good reason to remember what the original achieved. The most powerful stories in *Warcraft III* were not confined to the moments between missions. They happened when the character at the heart of the story was also the one I could not afford to lose.

You can learn the broad outline of *Warcraft III* in a few minutes: Lordaeron falls, Arthas becomes the thing he set out to destroy, old enemies unite against a larger threat, and the survivors build a different future. That summary gives you the facts. It cannot give you the feeling of starting a mission with too few troops, watching a familiar kingdom turn hostile, or having to keep a fragile alliance alive long enough to reach the end.

With *Warcraft III Reforged: Forsaken Kingdom* returning to the ruins of Lordaeron, the original campaigns are worth more than a lore recap. They are still a rewarding way to experience how this world was made. I have not played the new campaign, and this is not a review of it. It is a case for going back through *Reign of Chaos* and *The Frozen Throne* before moving forward.

## You watch a world change from several sides

The campaigns do something that a timeline cannot. They move you between peoples who understand the same crisis differently. Lordaeron's fall is a human catastrophe, an undead conquest, and the beginning of another faction's struggle to survive. By the time the story carries you across the sea, the orcs and night elves are not simply new armies to unlock. They have their own histories, fears and reasons to mistrust the others.

Playing each side matters. A change of faction alters what you can build, what you can afford to lose and how you solve a battle. Human workers build and repair a settlement; undead structures spread their own territory; night elf buildings are living things that can uproot and move. These mechanics do not explain every belief a culture holds. They give its identity a physical presence on the map.

That is one of *Warcraft III*'s great strengths as worldbuilding. Azeroth grows through encounters rather than an encyclopedia entry. Places become meaningful because you have defended them, invaded them or arrived too late to save them. Returning to Lordaeron in a new campaign has a different weight when you remember how it felt before it became a ruin.

## The missions ask more than “destroy the enemy base”

The old campaigns are not a continuous run of conventional RTS matches. They change the pressure on you. At times you are developing an army and deciding when to attack. Elsewhere you are escorting, surviving, exploring or racing against an enemy's progress. The objective changes the story's pace and, with it, your relationship to the character you control.

The culling of Stratholme is the obvious example. Its horror is not confined to the dialogue around Arthas's decision. The mission makes his decision the work you must perform to advance. It is an uncomfortable use of player agency: you do not choose whether he crosses that line, but you carry out the objective as the person he has become. You can understand why he thinks he has no alternative and still recoil from what he does.

Other missions find their tension in holding out rather than conquering. That variety keeps the campaigns from feeling like story scenes attached to a skirmish mode. The rules of each encounter help tell you what kind of moment this is: a desperate defence, a pursuit, an expedition, or a victory that no longer feels like one.

Some of those missions show their age. Pathfinding can turn a clean plan into a muddle, and a long base-building stretch can interrupt the urgency of the plot. Replaying them is not an exercise in pretending those frustrations vanished. It is a chance to see how often the campaigns' ideas still come through despite them.

## Characters earn their place in the world

I love a good fantasy setting, but an enormous history is not enough to make me care about it. *Warcraft III* gives its conflicts faces: Arthas, Jaina, Thrall, Tyrande, Illidan, Sylvanas and others whose choices send consequences far beyond a single mission. They disagree, misjudge one another and sometimes discover too late what their victories cost.

The series that followed has added years of lore and revised how some of those figures are remembered. Replaying the campaigns lets you encounter them at the point when their futures are still uncertain. Arthas is not yet a completed legend. Sylvanas is not yet a collection of later arguments about her character. Their stories unfold in sequence, with space to feel the changes rather than starting from the outcome.

*The Frozen Throne* is particularly useful here. It does not simply tie a bow on *Reign of Chaos*. It follows the damage left behind and the people trying to claim a future inside it. That makes it valuable context for a new campaign about the Forsaken. According to [Blizzard's overview of Forsaken Kingdom](https://news.blizzard.com/en-us/article/24301511/wage-war-once-more-with-warcraft-iii-reforged-forsaken-kingdom), the new story begins in Lordaeron's ruins and follows the Forsaken as they break free of the Scourge and establish a foothold in the Undercity. The original campaigns let you see why that ground matters before a new story asks what can grow there.

## You do not need to play like a competitive RTS player

There is a temptation to look at a classic strategy game and assume that enjoying it requires quick hands, perfect build orders and an appetite for online matches. The campaigns ask something different. They give you time to learn four factions, pay attention to a small group of important units and adapt when the mission changes. You will still have to make decisions under pressure. You do not have to master a competitive ladder to appreciate why a defence holds or why an attack falls apart.

Play for the story and the texture of the world. Notice how a faction gathers resources, which units it relies on, and how a new objective changes the value of the army you brought. Let the game surprise you when it shifts away from the standard base-building pattern. If you know where the story ends, pay attention to how much uncertainty it creates on the way there.

Blizzard says *Forsaken Kingdom* mixes a traditional four-mission prologue with a more open, RPG-shaped main campaign inspired by *The Frozen Throne*'s Rexxar adventure. That description is an invitation to revisit the range already present in *Warcraft III*, not proof that the new campaign delivers on it. [Blizzard's design overview](https://news.blizzard.com/en-us/article/24298590/warcraft-iii-reforged-forsaken-kingdom-deep-dive-recap) makes the connection explicit.

## Return for the journey, not just the answers

The old campaigns will tell you where the Forsaken came from. More importantly, they will remind you how *Warcraft III* made its history feel consequential. A kingdom did not fall because a paragraph said it did. You watched the threat approach, tried to hold a line, changed sides and saw what remained afterward.

That is why I would replay *Reign of Chaos* and *The Frozen Throne* before stepping into *Forsaken Kingdom*. Not to complete homework for a new release, but to spend time in a world whose future once felt undecided. The best reason to return to *Warcraft III* is that its history still feels like something you helped make.

A version of *Tetris* shipped on the Hagenuk MT-2000 in 1994, three years before Nokia put *Snake* on the 6110. *Snake* had the advantage of being there when you switched the phone on. You didn't have to find it or pay for a download, which helps explain why so many people played it.

## 4. The N-Gage made changing games a chore

Changing games on the original 2003 N-Gage meant powering it down, removing the back and battery, and reaching for the cartridge slot underneath. The later N-Gage QD moved the cartridge slot, but the first model's awkward design had already done some damage.

## 5. The App Store changed what happened to old Java games

The App Store changed how players found and bought phone games. Java releases were built for many different handsets and storefronts, which makes them difficult to collect and run now. At BlizzCon 2026, Blizzard introduced *World of Warcraft: Forever* as a permanent third way to play alongside modern and Classic WoW. It returns to an expanded version of original Azeroth, keeps the level cap at 60 and promises new stories within that world. Blizzard's [panel recap](https://worldofwarcraft.blizzard.com/en-us/news/24303862/world-of-warcraft-forever-whats-next-panel-recap) calls the world its main character and puts the journey before the destination.

It is tempting to hear that as Blizzard admitting twenty years of mistakes. That would be too neat. People asked for easier travel, faster grouping and new goals because those things solved real problems. Still, *Forever* asks a question that modern WoW has spent years trying to answer: how much convenience can you add before the place between the quests stops mattering?

## What Azeroth asked of us

Original WoW was slow in ways that made the map feel large. You crossed zones on foot before you could afford a mount. Dying sent you from a graveyard back to your corpse. Forming a dungeon party meant finding other players, travelling to the entrance and keeping the group together long enough to finish. Sometimes that was tedious. Sometimes the travel, the danger and the strangers you met became the story you told afterward.

The inconvenience also made small victories memorable. Reaching a faraway city or buying a mount could feel like progress outside a quest log. I would not want every old delay back, but I understand why players miss the sense that crossing Azeroth required attention.

## The fixes that changed the journey

Dungeon Finder, introduced late in *Wrath of the Lich King*, could assemble a party and move it into a dungeon. It spared people hours of waiting for a tank or healer. It also reduced the need to recruit from your own realm and make the trip together. Both consequences can be true, which is why players still argue about the feature.

Flying mounts changed the relationship with terrain in a different way. They made long journeys quicker, first in Outland and later across the original continents. Once you can pass above a hostile road, its threats no longer interrupt you. That freedom is welcome when you're repeating a route. It can also make the ground below feel like scenery.

Later expansions found other ways to keep characters busy. *Legion*'s Artifact Weapons, *Battle for Azeroth*'s Azerite systems and *Shadowlands*' Covenants each gave players new progression to pursue. Those systems helped define their expansions, but they also made power feel temporary when the next chapter replaced it. Modern WoW has done plenty well since; its changes cannot be reduced to one long slide away from a golden age. The question is whether a player still has reasons to inhabit the world when the immediate reward is gone.

## Players kept returning to the older game

Fans made their own answer visible. Nostalrius, a private server recreating early WoW, shut down in 2016 after Blizzard took action against it. Its popularity and the response to its closure showed demand for a version Blizzard was not then offering. Blizzard did not announce official Classic that November, as some retellings suggest. The [announcement came at BlizzCon 2017](https://worldofwarcraft.blizzard.com/en-us/news/21182074), and [Classic launched in August 2019](https://worldofwarcraft.blizzard.com/en-us/news/23099309).

The later Dungeon Finder debate captures the limits of a simple nostalgia story. Blizzard [left it out when *Wrath Classic* launched](https://worldofwarcraft.blizzard.com/en-gb/news/23793177/return-to-the-icy-realm-of-northrend-in-world-of-warcraft-wrath-of-the-lich-king-classic) in 2022. Some players welcomed that choice; others wanted a quicker way into dungeons. The [Random Dungeon Finder arrived in a 2023 update](https://worldofwarcraft.blizzard.com/en-gb/news/24013830/wrath-of-the-lich-king-classic-fall-of-the-lich-king-is-now-live). Players did not agree on which inconveniences were worth preserving.

*Forever* looks like another answer to that disagreement, built for people who want new adventures at the original game's scale rather than another march through old expansions. It includes changes and additions of its own, so it is no museum piece.

## A familiar argument for Holly Longdale

Before leading WoW, Holly Longdale worked on *EverQuest*, another MMO whose danger, travel and dependence on other players made its world difficult to forget. That experience does not prove she prefers every old hardship. It does make Blizzard's new emphasis on place and community an interesting turn in a career spent with both styles of MMO.

## What Forever still has to prove

A permanent level-60 game needs things for people to do when they know the roads. Blizzard has outlined new zones, quests and an account-wide [Legacy system](https://worldofwarcraft.blizzard.com/en-us/news/24307383/get-to-know-the-world-of-warcraft-forever-legacy-system), but no announcement can show whether those systems will sustain a community for years. Familiarity may bring players in. A living world has to give them reasons to remain.

I don't read *Forever* as a confession that everything after 2004 was wrong. I read it as Blizzard taking an old player complaint seriously enough to build a new version around it. I want to see whether it can make the journey feel worth taking again, even after the first trip is over.

*Elden Ring* sold more than [12 million copies shortly after release](https://www.fromsoftware.jp/ww/pressrelease/20220316_eldenring_salesdata.html) and passed [20 million by February 2023](https://www.fromsoftware.jp/ww/pressrelease/20230222_eldenring_salesdata.html). Those figures tell us it reached far beyond FromSoftware's existing audience. They do not tell us why I could spend an evening heading towards one castle and end up underground, nowhere near my original goal.

I think three choices made that possible. The game had a history big enough to invite questions, an open world that made detours worthwhile and tightly designed dungeons waiting when I wanted to commit to a destination.

## What George R. R. Martin wrote

The marketing made Martin's involvement easy to misunderstand. He helped build the Lands Between's past: its mythology, broken families and older conflicts. He did not write a conventional story that the game then delivered scene by scene. Miyazaki explained in a [New Yorker interview](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/persons-of-interest/hidetaka-miyazaki-sees-death-as-a-feature-not-a-bug) that the story had to serve the player's experience.

That division of work suits this game. Martin's history gives the demigods and their wars a sense of scale. FromSoftware leaves much of it in item descriptions, character conversations and places that do not stop you to explain themselves. You can follow the main path without understanding every family connection. If you become curious, the fragments are there to piece together.

## The open world did not replace the dungeons

Making a Souls game open world was a risk. Earlier FromSoftware levels rewarded attention with shortcuts and spaces that folded back on themselves. A vast map could have spread those ideas thin.

*Elden Ring* kept the intricate spaces. Stormveil Castle, Raya Lucaria and Leyndell still demand that you learn their corridors, rooftops and routes. Outside them, Torrent lets you leave a difficult encounter and head somewhere else. The world gives you a reason to approach a dungeon; the dungeon gives your wandering a change of pace and a test of everything you have learned.

That balance matters when a boss stops you. In a narrower game, persistence may be your only practical answer. Here, you can explore, find a new weapon, meet another character or gain levels before trying again. The detour feels like part of the adventure rather than time spent away from it.

## Discovery without a checklist

The map is not empty of guidance. Sites of Grace point towards important routes, and players can place their own markers. But the game rarely presents every useful cave, character and secret as a task to tick off. Malenia is optional. A trail of item descriptions can send you after a story that no main quest requires you to understand.

That means some players will miss things, including excellent fights. I think the trade is worth it. When I find a hidden route because I noticed an opening in the rocks, the discovery belongs to my journey. Even the familiar dungeons are stronger because I chose when to enter them.

## Why the pieces fit

Martin's backstory gives the ruins something to suggest. The open world gives me room to follow a suspicion, and the legacy dungeons reward me with the deliberate level design that made FromSoftware's earlier games so absorbing. None of those ideas would carry the game alone.

*Elden Ring* gave me an answer to a question I did not know I was asking: how could a Souls game get larger without losing the pleasure of looking closely? It made the spaces between major fights worth remembering too.

Blizzard's pitch for *World of Warcraft: Forever* puts Azeroth's world and community back at the centre. That sent me thinking about other MMOs where the best story might begin when another player interrupted your plans. Some are still running, some survive through fans and one ended years ago. All five understood the appeal of feeling as though you had gone somewhere.

## 1. Ultima Online (1997)

You could own a house, trade with other players and make enemies who remembered you after you logged off. *Ultima Online*'s most famous accident came during beta, when a player killed Richard Garriott's supposedly invulnerable Lord British after the protection on his character had not been restored. Garriott [later recounted](https://morganramsay.com/2017/06/30/richard-garriott-on-the-murder-of-lord-british/) the incident. The detail that stays with me is that even the world's ruler could lose control of what happened in it.

## 2. EverQuest (1999)

Death could leave your equipment on a corpse in dangerous territory. Getting it back might require help from people who had never met you, and a group had to travel and work together before it could clear anything. Norrath could be exhausting. It could also make an unexpected rescue or a hard-won trip feel like something your group had accomplished rather than a box the game had checked for you.

## 3. RuneScape (2001)

*RuneScape* ran in a browser, which made Gielinor accessible on machines that could not handle many other online worlds. It grew into a game with [more than 200 million registered accounts](https://www.runescape.com/game-info), according to its own site. Those are registrations, not simultaneous players. Its strength was that you could enter this huge shared world from an ordinary family PC and find something worth doing, even if you only had a short time.

## 4. City of Heroes (2004)

Creating your own costume, powers and name made Paragon City feel different the moment you arrived. The people flying overhead had invented heroes too. The official game closed in 2012, but its players kept working to bring it back. In January 2024, [Homecoming announced a licence from NCSOFT](https://forums.homecomingservers.com/topic/47223-ncsoft-homecoming-license-announcement/) to run and further develop its server under agreed conditions. A world built around players' invented identities survived because those players refused to leave it behind.

## 5. Star Wars Galaxies (2003)

*Galaxies* let players become entertainers, merchants and city builders as well as fighters. It offered room to live in Star Wars without following a hero's script. The 2005 New Game Enhancements changed its professions and combat, angering many of the people attached to that older, less directed experience. There were reasons its developers wanted a more approachable game, but the changes showed how much of this world existed in what players had built together. The official servers closed in 2011; players still discuss the version they lost.

These worlds weren't free of frustration, and several are hardly forgotten. Their shared strength was giving people enough freedom to make a story that no quest designer had prepared for them. That is a harder thing to patch in later than a new set of objectives.

## The range argument

There are three places a fight can happen: Far, Close and Ground. Boxing wants the pocket. Taekwondo has reasons to stay out of it. ## What still needs testing

The design target is for pure and mixed decks to remain competitive across matchups. A simulator helps reveal lopsided combinations, but players may find problems that a matchup matrix cannot: confusing grappling, a dead hand at the wrong range, or a mastery that feels better on paper than it does in a duel.

That is what I want the browser playtest to answer. A good mixed deck should feel resourceful; a single-art deck should feel like mastery, with meaningful weaknesses. If one choice simply wins more often, the argument at the heart of the game disappears.

Maddie Kim gets help from a stranger online. Then she begins to suspect the person sending the messages is her father, David, who died. It sounds like the beginning of a mystery about a new technology. For Maddie, the first question is much smaller and much harder: if this really is him, what does she say?

I watched *Pantheon* a while ago, and that is the feeling that has stayed with me. The show moves from a family computer to a struggle over the future of humanity, but it never lets the people inside that struggle become mere examples in a debate. Its technology matters because it can bring someone back within reach, take their freedom away, or leave their family wondering what survives of the person they loved.

:::spoiler-note

Spoilers for both seasons of *Pantheon* follow.

:::end-note

## A father on the other side of the screen

The series calls David an Uploaded Intelligence, or UI: a human mind recreated in a digital environment after a destructive scan. That is a speculative premise, not a description of the generative AI we have today. [AMC's introduction to the series](https://www.amc.com/blogs/amc-announces-cast-for-first-ever-primetime-animated-drama-pantheon--1008346) establishes the mystery of David's return and the company behind it. *Pantheon* asks us to imagine what that premise would do to a daughter still grieving him and to a wife trying to understand whether she has lost her husband or found him again.

David can speak, think and help Maddie. He is also separated from the ordinary life in which he was her father. Ellen cannot simply resume their marriage because a voice has returned. Maddie cannot get back the years she missed by finding a way to contact him. Their reunion is precious, but it does not erase the death that came before it. That tension gives the science fiction its weight: even if technology could preserve a mind, it would not automatically repair what grief has done to a family.

The people around David also have to ask who controls the system that contains him. A private company can present an upload as a breakthrough while keeping the person it created in its own infrastructure. The question of whether David is truly himself sits beside a more immediate one: can he choose where he goes, what he does and whom he speaks to?

## When someone else designs your life

The show gives that question other faces. Chanda is a gifted engineer whose expertise makes him valuable to people with the power to upload and exploit him. His digital existence offers extraordinary ability without guaranteeing safety or autonomy. Caspian, meanwhile, grows up inside a carefully engineered life designed to make him serve a purpose chosen by others. One is trapped after a technological transformation; the other has his choices shaped long before he understands the experiment.

Neither story fits neatly into a warning that intelligence itself is dangerous. The harm comes from what people decide to do with it and from how much control a small group has over everyone involved. *Pantheon* has characters who act with care and characters who cause terrible harm, on both sides of the screen. That is more unsettling than a single evil machine. It means there is no switch to throw that will settle the moral question for us.

## We have seen a smaller version of this choice

This is where the show began to make me think about the internet. The web was built on [open standards](https://www.w3.org/standards/) that let different people create sites and software that could work together. That openness is real, and it is worth defending. Yet much of what we do online now happens inside services that decide how we find one another, what we can carry elsewhere and which rules govern our accounts. The network can be open while the places where people spend their time feel much less so.

Uploaded minds are fiction. Our current AI tools are different in both capability and kind. Still, the internet offers a useful lesson about technological beginnings: a tool's possibilities do not tell us who will eventually control access to it. We make choices about ownership, consent, portability and oversight while something is being built. By the time it has become part of daily life, changing those choices can be much harder.

We have lived through a remarkable compression of change. Within living memory, going online became an everyday activity, then something many of us carry everywhere. It can be difficult to remember how contingent those shifts once felt. *Pantheon* takes that sense of acceleration and gives it a family to follow. Maddie does not get the luxury of debating the future from a distance. It arrives in her messages, her home and the people she loves.

## A future we may never see

As the story grows, it looks beyond one family and eventually beyond the span of one human life. Even at its most enormous, the finale returns to Maddie and the relationships that made the journey matter. Ken Liu, whose linked stories inspired the series, has [described](https://bookandfilmglobe.com/television/pantheon-creator-ken-liu-the-bfg-interview/) how the adaptation drew on *Seven Birthdays* to connect its distant future with its beginning. That return keeps the cosmic scale from becoming a set of numbers.

It also leaves me with the question the show asks most persistently: what do we owe people who will live with the systems we put in place, even if we will never meet them? We cannot predict every consequence of a new tool. We can decide whose consent counts, who gets a say in its rules and whether a person can leave a system without losing everything they have put into it.

That is why *Pantheon* feels personal to me long after watching it. Its great leap is not simply imagining minds in the cloud. It lets the future be shaped by people who are grieving, ambitious, frightened, generous and capable of getting things wrong. Maddie wants her father back. Others want to change the world. They all have to live with the gap between what they intend and what their choices set loose. So will the people who come after them.

title: "Why Heroes Is Still Worth Watching 20 Years Later"

The first episode of *Heroes* asks what you would do if you discovered you could do something impossible. Its answer depends on who you are. Claire Bennet tests the limits of a body that keeps healing, then has to go back to high school. Hiro Nakamura can bend time and space, and is delighted to find himself inside the sort of story he loves. Peter Petrelli thinks he might be able to fly, though he is much more certain that he wants to help people. The abilities are exciting. The lives they interrupt are what make you stay.

*Heroes* premiered in September 2006, [twenty years ago this month](https://ew.com/heroes-turns-20-creator-tim-kring-reflects-casting-hayden-panettiere-12137112). Watching *Lanterns* has put superhero television back on my mind, and this feels like a good moment to revisit a show built before a shared cinematic universe was the default model for the genre. Its first season remains an unusually satisfying recommendation. The later seasons are a more complicated proposition, but they do not erase what that opening run gets right.

## A power is also a problem

Claire's regeneration does not give her a ready-made place in the world. She still has to decide who can know about it and whether her family is protecting her or keeping secrets from her. The show's early scenes of her recording injuries are memorable because she is trying to understand herself with the tools available to a teenager. Her ability makes her vulnerable in ways a conventional origin story might hurry past.

Peter's story has a different pull. He wants to believe that a meaningful life involves saving someone, long before he understands the scale of what he can do. His brother Nathan worries about reputation and a political future. Their conflict gives both characters something tangible to lose. Hiro, meanwhile, brings a welcome sense of joy to the premise, but the show tests whether enthusiasm can survive the consequences of changing events. These people approach heroism from different starting points, which makes their eventual connections feel earned.

The series is most compelling when it lets an extraordinary event make an ordinary relationship harder. A parent with a secret, a brother who refuses to believe you, a friend asked to trust a story that sounds absurd: those are easier to recognize than a grand fight over the fate of the world. [The opening episode's premise](https://tv.apple.com/us/episode/genesis/umc.cmc.4e5nsvedwyzobvk7hc81ol1k3?showId=umc.cmc.2qp5mxx3mr2tmsvl484jrury2) is simple enough to hold all of them.

## The pleasure of watching the stories meet

The first season moves among people in different cities who have no reason, at first, to think their lives belong to the same story. An image or prediction that means little to one character becomes urgent once we have followed somebody else. Each new connection changes what the audience thinks it knows. It is an ensemble mystery with the momentum of a page-turning comic and the space to spend time with each person's fears.

That structure also gives the show's biggest question some force. The characters learn that a catastrophe may be coming, but none of them has a complete account of how to stop it. They have to judge whom to trust with incomplete information. The famous injunction to save Claire works because one teenager's safety suddenly seems tied to everyone else's. The scale grows without entirely losing the person at its centre.

The early episodes understand the appeal of withheld information. They also know when to give us an answer and let it create a new problem. Creator Tim Kring has [discussed the first season's planning](https://ew.com/heroes-turns-20-creator-tim-kring-reflects-casting-hayden-panettiere-12137112), including the difficulty of sustaining so many interlocking stories on a network television schedule. You can feel the ambition in the finished season. Sometimes its effects show their age, but the suspense rarely depends on spectacle alone.

## What does not hold up as well

It would be misleading to recommend all four seasons with the same confidence. The later run keeps finding interesting characters and ideas, yet some revelations arrive with less impact once the series has established how readily it can change the rules. Familiar faces remain central even when a fresh set of people might have opened up the premise. The second season was cut short during the 2007 writers' strike, as Kring [recounted in the anniversary interview](https://ew.com/heroes-turns-20-creator-tim-kring-reflects-casting-hayden-panettiere-12137112), though the strike alone cannot account for every later creative choice.

The first season has its own weaknesses. Certain plot turns rely on people failing to share information, and the pace can make a character's decision feel convenient. A twenty-episode-plus network season also asks for more patience than many viewers now expect. If you want every mystery resolved quickly, its pleasures may take time to appear.

Those reservations make the recommendation more useful. Start with season one, which tells a substantial story and gives its central characters room to become more than their powers. If their company is enough to bring you back, the remaining seasons have moments worth finding. There is no need to pretend the whole run is equally strong.

## Why return to it now?

*Heroes* catches people at the instant their lives stop making sense and lets them disagree about what comes next. Some want to hide. Some want an explanation. Some see an opportunity to control others. Hiro wants an adventure; Claire wants room to decide who she is. Twenty years of superhero television have produced bigger images and more elaborate worlds, but this show's first season still understands the nervous excitement of discovering you might be different.

Its question has aged well: once you can do something remarkable, what responsibility follows? *Heroes* works because it makes that question personal before anyone gets to call themselves a hero.

In *Ted Lasso*'s third season, Edwin Akufo gathers club owners to sell them a lucrative new league. ## Rebecca's change is the point

In the pilot, Rebecca hires Ted because she expects him to fail. Richmond's staff, players and fans face the consequences of a plan they know nothing about. Her ownership gives her the freedom to try it. As the series continues, Ted helps create a culture in which players support each other and staff members are heard. Rebecca recognizes that a competition built for them could leave the people who fill the stands with much less to believe in. She persuades the room by reminding her peers that they were once fans too.

It is a fine character moment. It is also a very *Ted Lasso* solution: a person with power looks at other people more generously, then uses that power well. By the end of the third season, Rebecca even offers Richmond supporters a share of the club. Her change of heart matters, but she still chooses the terms on which they can participate.

## A real club has no writer to make the owner listen

Manchester City has a different history and vastly different resources. [City Football Group says](https://www.cityfootballgroup.com/company) it is majority owned through a company wholly owned by Sheikh Mansour, with Silver Lake holding a substantial minority stake. City's investment has helped build a club with global reach and success on the pitch. It has also made questions about financial reporting consequential for competitors who have to play under the same rules.

On [29 September 2026, the Premier League published the findings of an independent commission](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4727779/premier-league-statement-manchester-city-fc/). It upheld all substantive charges concerning breaches of financial rules from 2009/10 through 2017/18, as well as three of four charges concerning failure to cooperate with the league's investigation. The commission found that sham arrangements overstated revenue and understated costs by more than £900 million, giving the appearance that City complied with spending limits when it did not. [City maintains its innocence and says it will appeal](https://www.mancity.com/news/club/manchester-city-club-statement-premier-league-63926128). A sanction has not yet been imposed.

This is a question about accounts, oversight and competitive rules. It cannot tell us how much a City supporter loves the club, or dismiss the work of players and staff. It also cannot be resolved by asking whether the owner feels an emotional connection to football. The league has to establish what happened and enforce its rules through a process that can withstand challenge.

## Football's invisible decisions

*Ted Lasso* makes club administration visible through Rebecca and Higgins. They have names and faces, so we can see when a decision hurts someone and when another decision begins to repair it. Real administration often consists of contracts, accounts and regulatory proceedings. It shapes what clubs can spend, who can compete and whether other clubs trust the result.

The City process shows how difficult accountability can be after the fact. The [league says](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4727779/premier-league-statement-manchester-city-fc/) its investigation began in December 2018; the commission hearing ended in December 2024, and its core findings were published in September 2026. City has the right to appeal and the question of sanction remains open. Whatever follows, those seasons cannot be played again. That delay matters to City's supporters as well as its rivals, all of whom have had to live with the unresolved case.

Richmond's pub regulars remind us why any of this has stakes. A club is part of how people spend their weeks and remember their lives. City's fans have that relationship with their team too. Ownership can fund a stadium, recruit talent and take a club across the world, but those decisions do not give owners a greater claim to its meaning than the people who have stayed with it.

## The part television cannot settle

Rebecca's speech is moving because it imagines someone powerful remembering what it felt like to have no power in the game. More owners could stand to remember that. But the scene ends when the owners agree. It has little reason to ask what happens when they do not, or when a decision is hidden in financial paperwork for years.

That is why the City ruling belongs in this conversation without turning City into *Ted Lasso*'s villain. The commission's findings, City's appeal and the eventual sanction need to be judged on their own evidence. Richmond supplies a question worth bringing to that process: who pays for the choices made at the top of a club? The real answer has to be written into rules and enforced in time for the people outside the owners' room to trust them.

A cardboard box is a surprisingly good weapon when you are a chipmunk. Pick it up, throw it at a mechanical dog, then duck beneath another while your partner decides whether to help or hurl you across the screen. *Chip 'n Dale: Rescue Rangers* understood that a small set of good actions could carry an entire adventure.

That is the appeal of this corner of platforming history. Mario and Sonic are obvious return visits, but the cartridges and early PC games around them held cartoon adaptations, odd mascots and action games with ideas worth borrowing. Some of these sold well. *Aladdin* and *The Lion King* are familiar names, hardly secret discoveries. The case here is for playing them again, alongside games that receive much less attention.

These are nine picks across consoles and DOS, not a strict ranking. The list stretches from straightforward platformers to action games where jumping shares the controller with fighting. Versions matter: a familiar title on a different console can be a different game altogether.

## 1. Chip 'n Dale: Rescue Rangers

**Version: NES. Best for: two-player mischief.**

Capcom makes being small the whole point. Boxes become ammunition and hiding places, while oversized furniture turns an ordinary room into a landscape. You can understand the basic moves almost immediately, then spend the rest of a stage discovering what else those moves permit. The [original manual](https://www.world-of-nintendo.com/manuals/nes/rescue_rangers.shtml) even explains how steel boxes can be stacked to reach higher platforms.

Two-player play adds a useful amount of chaos. Carrying and throwing a partner can solve a problem or create one, depending on the mood of the person beside you. It gives cooperation a physical, slightly mischievous quality. The game is also perfectly enjoyable alone; having someone to blame for a bad landing is a bonus.

## 2. Mighty Morphin Power Rangers

**Version: the 1994 SNES game. Best for: a short burst of Saturday-morning action.**

This is the genre-borderline pick. Natsume's game is primarily a side-scrolling brawler, with climbing, jumping and platforming sections along its single-plane stages. What earns it a place is how neatly it stages the television fantasy. The early levels begin with your chosen Ranger in civilian clothes, then let you morph before the boss encounter. Later, the scale changes again for Megazord battles.

The fighting is simple and the enemy designs repeat, so this is a modest recommendation rather than a lost masterpiece. Still, the transformation gives a familiar stage structure a little anticipation. If your memory is of a different *Power Rangers* cartridge, check the platform: the Genesis original is a fighting game, not this SNES adventure. [SNES version details](https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/snes/588493-mighty-morphin-power-rangers/reviews/175599).

## 3. The Lion King

**Versions: SNES and Mega Drive/Genesis. Best for: animation and a challenge you may still resent.**

The opening has a confidence few film adaptations manage. Young Simba bounds through bright scenery, and his movement sells the character before the game needs much explanation. Growing into adult Simba changes the emphasis from a cub's pouncing and roaring toward more direct combat. Even in a short platformer, that change gives the journey a sense of development.

Then there is the obstacle course accompanying *I Just Can't Wait to Be King*. Its monkeys, giraffes and timing demands make a cheerful scene feel considerably less cheerful. The difficulty is uneven, and attractive animation cannot excuse every awkward jump. Revisit it for the craft and the changing character, with realistic expectations about the frustration. This entry concerns the 16-bit games; the NES and handheld releases should not be treated as identical versions. [SNES gameplay reference](https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/snes/588444-the-lion-king/reviews/168498).

## 4. Aladdin

**Versions: Capcom's SNES game and Virgin's Mega Drive/Genesis game. Best for: comparing two interpretations of one film.**

There are two recommendations under this heading. Capcom's *Aladdin* puts its emphasis on movement, jumps and acrobatics. Apples can stun enemies before you bounce on them; the pleasure is in keeping the journey moving. Virgin's version gives Aladdin a sword as well as throwable apples, with animation that makes even a brief exchange with a guard entertaining to watch.

Choose the SNES game if platforming is the main attraction. Choose the Genesis game if expressive characters and swordplay are what you remember. Better still, try both and notice how the same source material produces different rhythms. They are separate games, not a single port with altered colours. The [Disney Classic Games Collection](https://www.digitaleclipse.com/games/disneyclassicgames) includes both 16-bit interpretations; it is an expanded package compared with the original 2019 collection. [Capcom version background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney%27s_Aladdin_%28SNES_video_game%29); [Genesis mechanics](https://www.sega-16.com/2004/06/disneys-aladdin/).

## 5. Shadow of the Ninja

**Version: NES, also known as Blue Shadow in Europe. Best for: a ninja adventure you can share.**

A second ninja changes the appeal immediately. Hayate and Kaede can fight through Natsume's action platformer together, with enemies and gaps demanding attention even when there are two of you. Hanging from platforms and choosing when to commit to an attack make movement part of the fighting, rather than a pause between encounters.

The futuristic streets and machinery give the adventure a harsher atmosphere than the cartoon games here. Its difficulty asks both players to learn the route, and company does not make every jump forgiving. That is part of the satisfaction: clearing a troublesome section together feels earned. This entry is for the original NES game, called *Kage* in Japan and *Blue Shadow* in Europe; the later *Shadow of the Ninja Reborn* is a separate remake. [Original game and cooperative play](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Games/NES/Shadow-of-the-Ninja--278865.html).

## 6. Earthworm Jim

**Versions: the original Mega Drive/Genesis and SNES games. Best for: strange sights and physical comedy.**

Jim's suit gives him a gun, but his own body is the better joke. He can use his worm head as a whip to strike enemies and catch hooks, turning a ridiculous character design into a useful platforming move. The animation carries that absurdity into the way he stands, moves and reacts. Even the opening's cow-launching gag makes the world feel happily unhinged.

Shiny keeps interrupting the usual run-and-jump routine with different situations, including rocket races and the job of protecting Peter Puppy. Those detours give the game personality, although some are more frustrating than funny when you have to repeat them. Revisit it for its expressive action and willingness to be strange, rather than expecting every experiment to land. The 16-bit releases, later Special Edition and handheld adaptations are not all interchangeable. [Original versions and gameplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthworm_Jim_%28video_game%29).

## 7. Darkwing Duck

**Version: NES. Best for: careful jumps and a hero with more confidence than health.**

The gas gun is the obvious tool, but Darkwing's cape and ability to hang from ledges make the game more interesting than a simple march to the right. You can block certain incoming attacks, wait for an opening, then climb back into the action. It asks you to notice enemy placement and make room for a clean shot.

Special gas adds further possibilities, including arrows that can help with traversal. It is a compact example of how a character's equipment can shape a platformer. Expect to learn troublesome sections rather than sail through on cartoon charm. If *DuckTales* is already on your replay list, this is a worthwhile companion with a sharper action emphasis. [Controls and special weapons](https://www.world-of-nintendo.com/manuals/nes/darkwing_duck.shtml).

## 8. Jazz Jackrabbit

**Version: the original 1994 DOS game. Best for: fast movement with a gun in your hands.**

Jazz brings mascot-platformer energy to the PC: a green rabbit, a red bandana and enough speed to make you watch the next stretch of ground carefully. His gun changes the rhythm. You can clear threats while moving, but running too eagerly still puts you into trouble. The pleasure is in finding a route you can take at pace without losing control of the situation.

Collecting ammunition lets you switch away from the basic blaster, with weapons such as bouncing projectiles and missiles that split into a V-shaped shot. The [original on-disk manual](https://www.abandonwaredos.com/docawd.php?idg=1762&sf=jazz-jackrabbit-manual.txt&sg=Jazz+Jackrabbit&st=manual) explains the arsenal. This recommendation is for the first *Jazz Jackrabbit*, not its sequel. The [Jazz Jackrabbit Collection](https://www.gog.com/en/game/jazz_jackrabbit_collection) groups the original episodes, additional CD episodes and Holiday Hare outings, making the scope of the release worth checking.

## 9. Dangerous Dave

**Version: the 1990 DOS game. Best for: a small challenge with no room for careless jumps.**

Find the trophy, then reach the exit. *Dangerous Dave* gives you a goal that fits in a sentence, but the route can make you think twice. Fire, awkward ledges and moving enemies turn a simple screen into a sequence you need to understand. A gun or jetpack can change your options, making the journey to a useful pickup part of the problem.

It is plainer and less fluid than the big 16-bit productions in this list, and its unforgiving hazards can make a short attempt end abruptly. The appeal is how quickly it gets down to business. There is very little between starting the game and having a jump worth concentrating on. Here, “Dave” means the original DOS *Dangerous Dave*, rather than *Dangerous Dave in the Haunted Mansion*. [John Romero's series history](https://www.dangerousdave.com/saga) identifies the 1990 PC release; his [level notes](https://rome.ro/dangerous-dave) describe the trophies, weapons and jetpack routes.

## Where to start

For an easy first decision, choose *Rescue Rangers* with a friend or Capcom's *Aladdin* for platforming. Try *Earthworm Jim* for strange comedy, *Jazz Jackrabbit* for speed, or *Dangerous Dave* for a compact DOS challenge. Pick *Shadow of the Ninja* when you want to learn a demanding route with someone beside you.

The strongest reason to revisit these games is a specific action that still feels good: throwing the box, catching the ledge, landing the head whip. Get that right and an old game needs very little help to become interesting again.

A green plane, a blue ocean and enemy fighters growing larger as they approach. *Sky Destroyer* creates the feeling of flying somewhere with very little scenery. The aircraft stays in view, the horizon gives you a direction, and a threat can arrive from the sky or the water below.

That image is one reason to return to an old shooter. A good mechanic is another. *Macross* lets a fighter grow arms and legs to change how it handles a battle. *Gradius* turns an unremarkable little gun into an arsenal you have chosen and built. *Galaga* makes losing a ship the beginning of a dangerous rescue opportunity.

These are five favourites worth revisiting, ranked by how much their distinctive ideas give me reason to recommend them today. The list includes aircraft and spacecraft, as well as Japan-only Famicom releases. Each entry concerns the original home version, rather than assuming an arcade game or later adaptation with the same name plays identically.

## 5. Sky Destroyer

**Version: Famicom, Japan-only. Style: behind-the-plane aerial combat.**

The viewpoint is its best idea. Looking past your own aircraft toward the horizon gives enemy approaches a sense of distance that a flat formation cannot provide. Machine-gun fire handles aerial threats, while torpedoes let you attack targets at sea. You have a small set of controls, but two different parts of the scene to watch. [Aircraft, weapons and objectives](https://openretro.org/game/e0610bc2-ab17-42b1-83fb-9785f28beb2d).

The cycle of changing light helps sell the illusion of a sortie. Reaching a fortified island gives that flight a destination; taking it out is more satisfying than simply seeing another wave appear. Those details are modest, yet they give the game a recognisable atmosphere.

Its limits are equally clear. The encounters repeat, and the action offers less variety than the games above it. Treat it as a short return visit, rather than expecting an elaborate campaign. If your memory is of a plane suspended over an endless ocean, the opening should bring it back quickly. The [1985 Famicom release](https://www.arcade-history.com/game/54627/sky-destroyer-model-tfc-sd-4500) is the version covered here.

## 4. Galaga: Demons of Death

**Version: NES; Galaga on Famicom. Style: fixed-screen space shooting.**

Moving left and right beneath a formation sounds restrictive until the formation starts coming apart. Enemies dive toward you, changing the safe space at the bottom of the screen. Chasing the next target is tempting, but watching the direction of an approaching attack is often more useful. The controls stay simple while your reading of the screen improves.

The capture mechanic gives that small battlefield another layer. Allow a fighter to be taken, then successfully rescue it, and you can form a dual fighter with greater firepower. You are risking a ship to improve your position. The [NES manual](https://www.nintendo.co.jp/clv/manuals/en/pdf/CLV-P-NABNE.pdf) explains how the rescue works.

That gamble is the main reason I would choose *Galaga* over a more basic formation shooter. It introduces a decision that can change the feel of the run without adding a menu or complicated controls. The scenery hardly changes, so the satisfaction has to come from better judgement, cleaner shooting and a higher score. For a few concentrated minutes of play, that remains a very good bargain.

## 3. Xevious

**Version: NES/Famicom. Style: vertically scrolling air-and-ground combat.**

A gun fires forward at enemies in the air. A targeting reticle marks where your bombs will hit the ground. That separation is the whole appeal of *Xevious*: lining up a ground installation can put your aircraft in a bad position for an aerial attack. A safe route and a profitable bombing run are not always the same route.

The scrolling landscape gives those decisions a setting. Ground defences pass beneath you while airborne enemies interrupt your plan, and the large floating fortress adds a threat with more presence than a small formation. [Nintendo's description of the NES game](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Games/NES/Xevious--754678.html) identifies the two kinds of attack.

The presentation is restrained, and the fixed arsenal will disappoint anyone looking for a stream of spectacular upgrades. Give it a little time to establish its rhythm, though, and the two-layer combat becomes rewarding. You improve by placing the ship better and recognising which threat needs attention first. It is a useful step up from *Galaga* if you want a moving world and more freedom without learning a lengthy equipment system.

## 2. The Super Dimension Fortress Macross

**Version: Famicom, Japan-only. Style: horizontal space shooting with a transforming fighter.**

The Valkyrie can be a fighter, the halfway GERWALK form, or a humanoid Battroid. Changing form affects speed and firing capabilities, so the transformation has a purpose beyond looking impressive. Fighter mode lets you move quickly; Battroid's ability to fire behind itself changes how you deal with an enemy that has slipped past. The machine's appearance tells you something useful about what it can do.

The mission structure supports that fantasy. You fight through space, approach a large enemy warship, then enter its interior to destroy the reactor. That progression makes each sortie feel like an assault on a target, with the final approach asking you to handle a different kind of space. [Forms and mission stages](https://www.arcade-history.com/game/53920/choujikuu-yousai-macross-model-nmr-4500).

Repetition eventually catches up with it, and the early graphics cannot disguise how often the same basic mission returns. Still, the transformations make familiar combat more engaging. Learning when to change the craft feels different from merely collecting a stronger gun. This is Namco's 1985 Famicom game, a specific recommendation within a much larger family of *Macross* adaptations.

## 1. Gradius

**Version: NES/Famicom. Style: horizontal space shooting with selectable upgrades.**

Every red capsule moves the highlight along the power meter. You decide when to activate it: take an improvement now, or keep collecting toward something else. That is a small but powerful change to the usual pickup system. Building the ship becomes a succession of choices made while you are still dodging enemies.

The weapons make those choices visible. Missiles deal with targets below you, a laser improves your forward attack, and Options follow the ship and copy its firepower. Getting those companions into position turns a narrow stream of shots into something much more capable. You can feel the difference between the fighter you started with and the formation you have assembled. [Konami's NES manual](https://www.world-of-nintendo.com/manuals/nes/gradius.shtml) details the upgrades.

Losing the equipment is painful, especially when you have grown accustomed to clearing threats quickly. Recovery can feel punishing. Yet that also gives a good run its tension: you are protecting a machine you helped build. Of these five games, *Gradius* offers the strongest combination of immediate shooting and decisions worth thinking about between attempts. The next capsule is rarely just another object to collect.

## Where to begin

Choose *Sky Destroyer* for the ocean view, *Galaga* for formation reading, or *Xevious* for the challenge of watching two layers of combat. *Macross* is the pick if you want to transform the machine itself. Start with *Gradius* if the memory that brought you here was a small spaceship gradually gaining missiles, lasers and companions.

Each asks you to notice something different: an approach from the horizon, a formation breaking, a ground target passing beneath you, a reason to transform, or an upgrade worth waiting for. Learning to see those opportunities is what makes another short flight worthwhile.

Scrooge McDuck can still make a pogo stick out of a walking cane. Wolverine can still fill half a screen with claws. Four turtles can still turn a street full of disposable enemies into a very good evening. The appeal of returning to these games begins with an action you remember, then grows when the rest of the game proves worth remembering too.

A retro collection needs to make that return easy. The right versions, useful controls and a sensible way to save matter more than a huge number on the box. Artwork and development notes can deepen the experience, especially when they help explain why an old game made the choices it did. But there should also be something inside that you want to keep playing after the museum closes.

These four collections offer different routes back: cooperative arcade action, superhero fighting, cartoon platformers and a tour of SNK before the Neo Geo. They are four recommendations, rather than a ranking of interchangeable packages. Each earns its place for a different reason, and each has a few details worth checking before you buy.

## 1. Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles: The Cowabunga Collection

**Start with: Turtles in Time, The Hyperstone Heist and The Manhattan Project.**

The turtles are unusually well suited to a collection. Their games share enough characters and ideas to feel like one shelf of adventures, yet change substantially as you move between arcade cabinets, Nintendo consoles, the Genesis and Game Boy. A sewer, a skateboard and a familiar villain can become very different things depending on the hardware.

*The Cowabunga Collection* gathers [13 games](https://www.konami.com/games/eu/en/topics/16620/), including both the arcade and SNES versions of *Turtles in Time*. Having them together makes comparison part of the fun. You can see how a home adaptation reshapes a game, rather than treating every console release as the same experience with fewer colours.

The strongest reason to start here is the brawling. *Turtles in Time* keeps changing the backdrop while letting you settle into the satisfying business of clearing a screen. *The Hyperstone Heist* gives Genesis players their own take on that rhythm, while the NES *Manhattan Project* squeezes an energetic adventure out of simpler hardware. They work particularly well when another player joins the argument over which turtle to choose.

The package also makes room for the less obvious attraction, *Radical Rescue*. That Game Boy adventure gives different turtles abilities that open routes through its map. It is a useful reminder that the licence supported more than one kind of action game.

Digital Eclipse treats the surrounding material with care. Restored manuals, development artwork, music and strategy guides sit alongside save and rewind features. The guides even offer help with the original NES game's notorious underwater section. Those additions give younger players a chance to understand the games without inheriting every frustration that came with them.

**The catch:** thirteen titles do not mean thirteen equally strong games. The fighting games and early handheld outings have different appeals from the headline brawlers, and online play is limited to selected titles. Buy it for the adventures you want to play, then let the rest become discoveries.

## 2. Marvel vs. Capcom Fighting Collection: Arcade Classics

**Start with: Marvel vs. Capcom 2, X-Men vs. Street Fighter and The Punisher.**

Few collections announce themselves quite like this one. Superheroes leap far above the ground, special attacks spill across the screen, and the roster treats comic-book absurdity as a design brief. The pleasure is partly in the fighting and partly in seeing how enthusiastically Capcom translates these characters into animation.

The [seven-game lineup](https://www.capcom-games.com/marvel-vs-capcom-fc/en-us/title) follows that partnership from *X-Men: Children of the Atom* and *Marvel Super Heroes* through *X-Men vs. Street Fighter*, *Marvel Super Heroes vs. Street Fighter*, *Marvel vs. Capcom: Clash of Super Heroes* and *Marvel vs. Capcom 2: New Age of Heroes*. The seventh game takes a different route: *The Punisher* is a side-scrolling beat 'em up.

*Marvel vs. Capcom 2* is the obvious centrepiece. Choosing a team gives even a casual match its own little fantasy: favourite characters, unlikely combinations and the possibility that someone dismissed as a novelty will become your favourite fighter. Learning what those teams can actually do is a much larger project. The roster's appeal survives that gap between picking characters and mastering them.

*X-Men vs. Street Fighter* is a rewarding earlier stop. Its pairings feel immediately legible if you grew up with either franchise, and swapping partners gives familiar one-on-one fighting a different rhythm. Playing the series in sequence lets you watch the crossover idea expand.

Then there is *The Punisher*. It is reason enough for arcade-brawler fans to pay attention, even if competitive fighting leaves them cold. Frank Castle and Nick Fury take Capcom's street-fighting formula through a nastier comic-book world, with weapons and environmental destruction adding to the spectacle. Its presence gives the package a welcome change of pace.

Training options, galleries and rollback online play help these games find a place beyond a nostalgic afternoon. The collection is available for Switch, PS4, Xbox One and Steam; console compatibility with newer hardware depends on the version you buy.

**The catch:** six of the seven games are fighters. If *The Punisher* is your only attraction, wait for a price you can justify for that game alone. There is plenty here for anyone interested in learning the others, but a large roster does not make a demanding fighter automatically approachable.

## 3. The Disney Afternoon Collection

**Start with: DuckTales, Chip 'n Dale Rescue Rangers and Darkwing Duck. Add Goof Troop on the newer Nintendo edition.**

There is a satisfying economy to Capcom's Disney games. Scrooge's cane, the chipmunks' throwable boxes and Darkwing's gas gun give each character an immediately useful identity. Their television personalities survive through movement and tools, without needing long conversations to establish who they are.

The original collection contains six NES games: *DuckTales*, *DuckTales 2*, *Chip 'n Dale Rescue Rangers*, *Rescue Rangers 2*, *TaleSpin* and *Darkwing Duck*. The [2026 Switch and Switch 2 edition](https://atari.com/products/disney-afternoon-collection) adds the SNES games *Goof Troop* and *Bonkers*, bringing its lineup to eight. Check the edition carefully: those additions are exclusive to the newer Nintendo release.

*DuckTales* remains the most inviting starting point. The pogo jump turns enemies into stepping stones, and the levels give you reasons to look around rather than hurry straight to the exit. *DuckTales 2* offers a less famous continuation with new uses for the cane. Together they make a compact case for character-driven platforming.

*Rescue Rangers* shifts the pleasure towards cooperation. Throwing a box is simple; deciding whether to throw your partner is a matter of character. Both games are enjoyable alone, but another player brings the sort of mild mischief that a perfectly orderly cooperative game can miss.

*Darkwing Duck* supplies the sharper action challenge. Its shooting, hanging and special weapons reward more deliberate movement. For players who like Capcom's NES action games, it is a natural companion to the more exploratory *DuckTales*.

On the newer edition, *Goof Troop* adds a different kind of partnership. Its overhead puzzles turn cooperation into a problem of positioning and timing. That change of genre helps the Nintendo package feel broader, while keeping its cartoon charm. Rewind and saves throughout the collection also make a return visit much less dependent on spare lives or remembering passwords.

**The catch:** the weaker games remain weaker games. *TaleSpin* has awkward shooting and a slow plane; *Bonkers* has attractive animation without the same quality of level design. A [detailed review of the newer edition](https://cogconnected.com/review/the-disney-afternoon-collection-switch-2-review/) makes the same distinction. The stronger platformers and *Goof Troop* carry the recommendation. Also, these are the original games: *DuckTales Remastered* is a separate release.

## 4. SNK 40th Anniversary Collection

**Start with: Crystalis, the arcade Ikari Warriors and Psycho Soldier. Try Prehistoric Isle for dinosaur-powered arcade nonsense.**

SNK's name tends to bring *Metal Slug* and *The King of Fighters* to mind. This collection looks further back, at the company's work before the Neo Geo. Its appeal is finding the experiments that preceded the familiar identity: tanks, soldiers, strange platformers and shooters with ideas that had not yet settled into a house style.

The strongest discovery is *Crystalis*. This NES action RPG gives the collection an adventure substantial enough to occupy more than a short arcade session. Its sword combat, exploration and progression make it a particularly good choice for anyone who enjoys the early *Zelda* games. [Nintendo World Report](https://www.nintendoworldreport.com/review/48851/snk-40th-anniversary-collection-switch-review) and the much more sceptical [GameCritics review](https://gamecritics.com/jarrod-johnston/snk-40th-anniversary-collection-review/) both identify it as the standout.

The arcade games reward a more exploratory approach. *Ikari Warriors* lets you see why the overhead soldier-and-vehicle format became recognisable. *Psycho Soldier* brings cooperative shooting to a colourful platform-action framework. *Prehistoric Isle in 1930* sends a plane into a world of dinosaurs, a premise whose appeal needs very little historical explanation.

Some games include their NES adaptations as well as the arcade originals. That makes the collection especially interesting as a study of compromises. It also makes choosing the right version important. The arcade *Ikari Warriors* and its NES port have very different reputations; *Athena* is another game where the format changes the recommendation substantially.

The surrounding features are unusually useful. Rewind helps tame coin-hungry difficulty, and the watch mode lets you study a recorded playthrough before taking control. The museum provides artwork, promotional material and background that can turn an unfamiliar title into something worth investigating.

**The catch:** this is the least consistently enjoyable lineup of the four. Historical interest does a fair amount of the work. [SNK's official Steam roster](https://www.snk-corp.co.jp/us/press/2019/061401/) lists 24 games; console editions also include *Iron Tank*, and Xbox adds *Baseball Stars*. Check your platform's contents, and do not buy it expecting the Neo Geo favourites.

## Which collection should you choose?

For cooperative brawling, start with *Cowabunga*. For comic-book spectacle and a fighter you can keep learning, choose *Marvel vs. Capcom*. Disney is the most reliable route to colourful platforming, especially if the newer edition's *Goof Troop* is an option. SNK suits a more curious mood, with *Crystalis* giving its scattered discoveries a strong centre.

The best return visits begin with something small: a well-timed throw, a pogo jump, a partner tagging into a fight. These collections make those actions easy to find again. The good ones can still turn a quick look into an evening.

Leaning around a doorway should be a small action. In a good VR game, it can become the whole encounter: a glimpse of an enemy, a hand searching for ammunition, the decision to pull back before something notices you. That change in scale is what the medium does best. Familiar actions acquire weight because your body has to take part in them.

The recent Sony and Meta headlines make this a timely moment to revisit that achievement. [Road to VR's 7 October 2026 report](https://roadtovr.com/sony-sells-xr-headset-patents-meta/), drawing on ZDNet Korea's reporting, describes 43 recorded US patent transfers between June and September, within a wider agreement covering XR patents. That is a reported patent transaction, not a confirmed sale of Sony's entire VR business or an announcement that PSVR2 has ended. Both companies had yet to comment in that report.

The uncertainty still raises a useful question: which games deserve to outlast the hardware arguments? These fifteen are an editorial selection of the best VR games, ranked for how well they use the medium, the strength of their underlying design and their lasting appeal. The list includes dedicated VR games and substantial VR modes, across generations. It is not a ranking of headset specifications or an average of review scores.

Platform notes identify the relevant VR editions, not every possible hardware configuration. An original PSVR game does not automatically work on PSVR2; [Sony's compatibility guidance](https://www.playstation.com/en-in/support/hardware/ps-vr2-compatibility/) explicitly separates the libraries. Check the linked edition before buying. Research and version notes were checked on 14 October 2026.

## 1. Half-Life: Alyx

**VR edition: SteamVR. The benchmark for a sustained VR campaign.**

The gravity gloves are a clever answer to an everyday problem: an object you want is slightly too far away. Flicking it towards your hand is convenient, expressive and enjoyable often enough to become a habit. *Alyx* builds that kind of thoughtfulness into an entire adventure.

Combat gains tension from the work between shots. Looking for ammunition, handling a reload and making use of cover become decisions made with your hands and posture. Meanwhile, drawers and shelves give exploration a useful physical texture. You examine a place by doing something in it.

Valve balances those details with strong pacing, recognisable *Half-Life* humour and carefully staged fear. Its [official overview](https://www.half-life.com/alyx) places the story between the first two main games. The achievement is how comfortably that full adventure accommodates interaction at arm's length. It remains the first recommendation for someone who wants to see what a major campaign can gain from VR.

## 2. Astro Bot Rescue Mission

**VR edition: original PSVR, with PS Camera and DualShock 4.**

*Rescue Mission* makes your position in the world part of its platforming. Astro has somewhere to go, but you have somewhere to look. Leaning, peering around scenery and noticing a tiny robot tucked into an unlikely corner become pleasures alongside the jumps.

The game keeps finding ways to let the player participate without taking the controller away from the little hero. The result is an adventure with an unusually warm relationship between scale and character. Astro feels small, the scenery feels substantial, and helping him feels personal.

The [original game's hardware requirements](https://www.playstation.com/en-in/games/astro-bot-rescue-mission/) matter here. This is the 2018 VR adventure, not the later flat-screen *Astro Bot*, and its PSVR edition is not playable through PSVR2. That separation is also an argument for preserving it. One of Sony's most inventive uses of VR should remain easy to experience as hardware changes.

## 3. Beat Saber

**VR editions: Meta Quest, SteamVR, PSVR and PSVR2.**

Coloured blocks arrive, arrows tell you where to cut, and your arms learn the song. *Beat Saber* makes the rules clear enough to grasp immediately, then gives their execution enough room for years of improvement.

The satisfaction comes from the whole motion: preparation, swing and the cut landing with the music. A difficult pattern gradually becomes a sequence you can recognise and perform. It is a strong example of a game where physical repetition is the pleasure, rather than a cumbersome substitute for a button press.

Its place on this list also rests on accessibility. You do not need to understand a fictional world before you understand what is fun. Platform support has changed, though: the [official FAQ](https://beatsaber.com/faq.html) says new PlayStation updates ended in June 2025 and PlayStation multiplayer ended in February 2026. Existing single-player content remains playable. Quest and Steam are the relevant editions for continuing content support.

## 4. Resident Evil 4 VR Mode

**Selected edition: the PS5 remake's PSVR2 mode.**

The village attack is a very different proposition when a chainsaw approaches at your own apparent height. *Resident Evil 4* already understands pressure, crowd management and the need to keep moving. Its VR mode brings those demands closer without losing the larger adventure that makes the game special.

Handling weapons, aiming with both hands and meeting an attack with a knife make familiar combat newly absorbing. The spectacle becomes more immediate, but the decision-making remains important. Being inside Leon's view does not remove the need to know when a fight is getting out of hand.

Capcom's [VR development interview](https://blog.playstation.com/2023/12/06/resident-evil-4-vr-mode-interview-new-gameplay-features-learnings-from-re-village-and-more/) confirms that the PSVR2 mode covers the remake's main campaign and is an additional download for owners of the PS5 game. Armature's Quest adaptation of the 2005 original is a separate, excellent interpretation. They should not be confused at the checkout.

## 5. Gran Turismo 7

**VR edition: PS5 with PSVR2.**

Racing makes a persuasive case for VR without needing to reinvent its controls. You are already meant to be sitting inside a machine. Giving that cockpit depth changes the relationship between your car, the corner ahead and the rival drawing alongside.

The gain is especially clear in ordinary racing decisions. You can look towards the exit of a bend, judge another car's position and glance at the interior without switching between fixed camera views. A steering wheel can deepen the effect, but it is not required to understand it.

The [PSVR2 update](https://www.playstation.com/en-in/games/gran-turismo-7/ps-vr2/) is included with the PS5 game at no additional charge. *GT7* earns its position because VR serves a substantial racing game with reasons to return beyond the novelty of a first lap. The menus and occasional non-VR elements also make it worth understanding the mode's scope before expecting every moment to happen inside the cockpit.

## 6. SUPERHOT VR

**VR editions: Quest, SteamVR and original PSVR.**

Holding still gives you time to think. Moving commits you to the consequences. That relationship turns a room of red enemies into a problem you solve with your body: lean away from a shot, reach for a weapon, throw something across the space and follow the opening it creates.

The sparse visuals help enormously. Threats are readable, objects are distinct, and very little scenery competes with the action. A successful sequence can feel choreographed even when it began as a desperate improvisation.

The [developer's FAQ](https://superhotgame.com/faq) makes an important distinction: *SUPERHOT VR* is a separate game with its own levels and mechanics, rather than a headset toggle for the flat-screen original. Its compactness is part of the appeal. It can demonstrate a very specific use of VR in minutes, then keep asking you to perform it better.

## 7. Batman: Arkham Shadow

**VR edition: Meta Quest 3 and Quest 3S.**

Turning Arkham combat into first-person action could easily have reduced Batman to a pair of swinging fists. *Arkham Shadow* keeps more of the identity intact: reading threats, using gadgets, moving through dangerous spaces and making a group of enemies manageable through controlled aggression.

The physical actions give that fantasy a useful new texture. A punch belongs to your arm; a stealth encounter puts a target within reach. At the same time, the campaign retains investigation and atmosphere, giving Batman things to do between fights.

The [official game page](https://www.meta.com/in/quest/batman-arkham-shadow/) identifies this as a full-length Arkham entry for Quest 3 and 3S. Its [Best VR/AR win at The Game Awards 2024](https://www.meta.com/blog/batman-arkham-shadow-the-game-awards-tgas-2024-best-vr-ar-game-of-the-year/) is useful recognition, but the stronger recommendation is the design itself. It makes a familiar superhero game work from an unfamiliar viewpoint.

## 8. Asgard's Wrath 2

**VR edition: Meta Quest standalone.**

An expansive RPG is a different test of VR from a perfect ten-minute encounter. It needs travel, character growth, combat and puzzles to remain worthwhile after the first impressive vista. *Asgard's Wrath 2* takes on that larger problem with unusual confidence.

Its changes in scale are especially effective. Fighting from a mortal's perspective and manipulating a puzzle at godly scale give the adventure different kinds of physical involvement. Companions and evolving equipment then help turn those moments into an ongoing journey.

The [official player's guide](https://www.meta.com/blog/asgards-wrath-2-players-guide-game-overview/) details its RPG systems and exploration. The attraction is the commitment to an extended adventure on standalone hardware. Choose it when you want a game to settle into over many sessions, rather than something to demonstrate to a friend before passing the headset over.

## 9. Resident Evil 7: Biohazard

**Selected edition: the PS4 game's original PSVR mode.**

The Baker house is effective because so much of it is horribly ordinary. A hallway, a table and a doorway can carry the dread before anything monstrous arrives. VR makes their dimensions feel harder to dismiss.

*Resident Evil 7* uses that proximity to support a full survival-horror campaign. It does not need elaborate hand interactions to make a room threatening. Looking towards a sound, hesitating before entering a space and checking what is behind you are already powerful actions.

The [official PSVR edition](https://www.playstation.com/en-in/games/resident-evil-7-biohazard/) belongs to the older headset library. The PS5 flat-screen release is not a PSVR2 upgrade. Its presence here reflects the strength of that original VR experience, including its limitations, rather than an assumption that every modern *Resident Evil 7* purchase includes the same mode.

## 10. The Walking Dead: Saints & Sinners

**VR editions include SteamVR, Quest and PlayStation VR releases; choose the version for your headset.**

Survival becomes more persuasive when the equipment has to be handled. Reaching for a weapon while a walker closes the distance changes a familiar inventory decision into an immediate task. The fighting has a physical quality that suits its desperate world.

The [original Saints & Sinners](https://store.steampowered.com/app/916840/The_Walking_Dead_Saints__Sinners/) also gives you reasons to think beyond the next enemy. Scavenging, limited resources and encounters with the living make each trip into New Orleans a calculation. You are deciding what a risk is worth, then carrying out the decision at close range.

That connection between preparation and action is why the game belongs here. VR contributes to the systems rather than merely putting zombies nearer your face. This recommendation is for the first game, not the separately sold *Chapter 2: Retribution*.

## 11. Moss: Book II

**VR editions: PSVR/PSVR2, Quest, SteamVR and Pico.**

Quill gives VR a protagonist you can care about without pretending to be her. You guide the mouse through the adventure while also reaching into her world to help. That partnership makes the viewpoint emotionally useful.

Looking closely at a scene can reveal a route or a detail, and interacting with the environment gives the miniature world a pleasing solidity. The sequel adds tools and possibilities while preserving the appeal of watching a small, expressive hero tackle a large problem.

Polyarc's [Book II page](https://www.polyarcgames.com/games/moss-book-ii) confirms its VR platforms and 2022 Best VR/AR award. Start with the first *Moss* if you want Quill's story in order. *Book II* takes the place here as the richer continuation, but both demonstrate how well VR can serve an adventure observed from the outside.

## 12. Walkabout Mini Golf

**VR editions include Quest, PC VR, PSVR2 and Pico.**

There is a particular pleasure in lining up a putt, choosing how hard to hit it and watching the ball obey a plan that was nearly good enough. *Walkabout* builds its appeal around that ordinary, repeatable satisfaction.

The courses then give the act of putting somewhere memorable to happen. Their layouts encourage you to examine the scenery, find another angle and occasionally wonder whether a ridiculous bank shot might work. Searching for hidden balls supplies a second reason to linger.

Mighty Coconut's [course overview](https://www.mightycoconut.com/minigolf) and [platform guide](https://www.mightycoconut.com/wmg-resources) explain the available editions and cross-platform play. In company, the game becomes a place to spend time as well as something to win. It deserves a place among the grand adventures because it understands a smaller ambition so completely.

## 13. Lone Echo

**VR edition: Meta's Rift/PC VR release; not a native Quest standalone game.**

Reaching for a surface and pushing away gives zero-gravity movement a satisfying logic. Your hands explain the environment: how to stop, how to turn, how to travel without a floor doing the work.

*Lone Echo* combines that movement with the relationship between Jack and Liv aboard a mining facility near Saturn. Being beside another character, helping with practical tasks and inhabiting the same space make the science-fiction story more immediate.

The [original PC VR listing](https://www.meta.com/experiences/pcvr/lone-echo/1368187813209608/) is an important version distinction for Quest owners. The game's place here rests on its strong marriage of movement and companionship. Its sequel is another worthwhile stop, but the first establishes why reaching through a virtual space can feel so natural.

## 14. Pistol Whip

**VR editions: Quest, SteamVR and PlayStation VR releases, including PSVR2.**

*Pistol Whip* gives a shooting gallery a beat, then asks your body to keep up. A shot, a dodge and a close-range strike become parts of the same performance. The appeal is how quickly they can begin to feel connected.

Its visual scenes are deliberately stylised, helping the action remain readable while the music supplies momentum. Chasing a better run can mean learning where threats appear, tightening your timing or choosing a different challenge. Familiarity improves the fantasy of moving cleanly through a dangerous scene.

The [official Steam description](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1079800/Pistol_Whip/) outlines that blend of rhythm and film-inspired gunplay. It sits alongside *Beat Saber* because it offers a different physical conversation with music: shooting and evasion instead of cutting patterns.

## 15. No Man's Sky

**VR editions include SteamVR and PlayStation VR/PSVR2.**

Stepping out of a ship gives *No Man's Sky* a different sense of scale. The cockpit you occupied a moment ago becomes a machine beside you, and the landscape extends beyond the edge of a monitor. Exploration gains a useful pause: a reason to stand still before deciding where to go.

It is a less tightly designed VR showcase than *Alyx* or *SUPERHOT*. The attraction is the freedom to make a longer journey through its existing universe: flying, gathering, building and finding somewhere you want to remain.

Hello Games' [Fractal update notes](https://www.nomanssky.com/fractal-update/) document the PSVR2 implementation. Performance and presentation depend on your hardware and settings, so expectations matter. Its place on this list comes from scale and possibility. Few games offer quite the same feeling of owning a little ship in a very large space.

## A few more worth making room for

*[Tetris Effect: Connected](https://www.playstation.com/en-in/games/tetris-effect/)* and *Rez Infinite* deserve attention for turning sound and imagery into spaces you can occupy. *[Red Matter 2](https://redmatter2.com/)* is a strong choice for tactile puzzles and a carefully realised science-fiction atmosphere. *[Eleven Table Tennis](https://store.steampowered.com/app/488310/Eleven_Table_Tennis/)* makes another persuasive case for keeping a familiar sport's physical actions intact. *[Blade & Sorcery](https://warpfrog.com/)* suits players who want to experiment with the possibilities of physical combat.

Fifteen places cannot cover every useful route into VR. These omissions are reasons to keep exploring, especially once you know whether you prefer rhythm, careful puzzles, extended campaigns or a world to inhabit.

## The games are worth keeping within reach

If you want a first step, choose *Beat Saber* or *Walkabout*. For a major campaign, start with *Alyx*, *Arkham Shadow* or *Asgard's Wrath 2*, depending on your hardware. For Sony's strongest demonstrations, *Gran Turismo 7* and the PSVR2 *Resident Evil 4* mode make an immediate case; *Astro Bot Rescue Mission* remains a compelling reason to preserve the older headset's library.

The patent reports invite questions about what Sony intends to build next. The games leave a more practical question: how easily will people be able to experience what has already been built? A catalogue spread across incompatible headsets can be difficult to revisit even when its best ideas remain fresh.

There is still something remarkable about finding yourself beside Quill, inside a racing car or holding a putter over an improbable little course. Whatever the next hardware cycle brings, those are experiences worth keeping within reach.

*From stolen hatchbacks to criminal empires, how GTA changed its cities, its stories and its idea of freedom, and what GTA VI needs to get right.*

There is a moment in Grand Theft Auto when the mission stops mattering.

You were supposed to collect somebody. Then a better car went past. Then the radio played something you liked. Then the police arrived, an unfortunate misunderstanding involving several pedestrians having occurred, and now you are reversing a burning ambulance up a motorway exit.

Your contact can wait. He has been standing in the same place since Tuesday.

Almost thirty years into the series, that diversion remains GTA's most persuasive sales pitch. Rockstar can write the betrayal, hire the actor and choreograph the helicopter crash. It still has to compete with whatever happens when you take the wrong turn.

GTA VI is currently scheduled for **19 November 2026**, on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S. Rockstar's launch trailer arrived on 15 October. The return to Vice City is close enough to require actual calendar management, rather than another theory about the moon.[^1]

But what, exactly, are we waiting to return to?

For some players, GTA is the neon of Vice City. For others, it is a bicycle outside the Johnson house, a boat arriving in Liberty City, or three men arguing their way through a robbery. A growing part of its audience knows Los Santos primarily as somewhere to meet friends, run businesses and be murdered by equipment that appears to have escaped from a defence contractor's cocaine drawer.

These games share a name and a remarkably durable appetite for stealing cars. Their ambitions have changed considerably. They have offered the fantasy of becoming powerful, asked whether power fixes anything, and eventually built an online economy that can make criminal prosperity feel suspiciously like maintaining a second job.

To understand what VI might become, it helps to follow how that happened. The story starts in Scotland, well before anyone needed to count the hair on a trailer character's arm.

*Spoiler note: this article discusses premises, themes and several mid-story developments across the released games. It avoids explaining their final choices and endings. GTA VI discussion uses public material available by 16 October 2026.*

## 1. Before the empire: GTA discovers that the detour is the game

### Grand Theft Auto, 1997

The original GTA arrived in a period when games were enthusiastically learning to look expensive. PlayStation was selling the promise of three-dimensional worlds; here was a top-down game in which your criminal ambitions were represented by a small person you could lose beneath the scenery.

DMA Design's project had begun as **Race 'n' Chase**. Its development was messy, and the eventual formula emerged through iteration rather than a single immaculate design revelation. In a later interview, developer Gary Penn recalled instability, awful vehicle handling and a police-behaviour bug that made officers aggressively ram the player. He remembered that aggression helping the game become entertaining. It is a valuable recollection, with the uncertainty he attached to it intact: one developer's account of a breakthrough, rather than proof that the entire franchise happened by accident.[^2]

The result let you move between vehicles, crimes and jobs across early versions of **Liberty City, San Andreas and Vice City**. Those names preceded their famous later incarnations. Narrative arrived in small messages and instructions; the real continuity was the money you earned and the trouble you accumulated.[^3]

That abstraction mattered. Early GTA treated the city as something to interfere with. Missions gave you reasons to travel, but stealing a different vehicle could change the texture of the next minute. You could understand the place through what it allowed, and through the speed at which it became annoyed with you.

It also established the radio. Much of the first game's music was created in-house by Colin Anderson, Craig Conner and Grant Middleton, rather than assembled from the licensed hits that would later become a series trademark.[^4] Even here, a car was both transport and a little room with music in it. GTA had found a way to make travelling entertaining before it could afford to make travelling beautiful.

Its humour was less discriminating. The notorious bonus for running down a procession of Hare Krishnas belongs to this history too. Some of the early transgression is clever interaction; some is simply a game noticing that it can be offensive and getting rather pleased with itself. Those impulses would remain difficult to separate.

The surrounding panic arrived astonishingly early. In May 1997, before the game's release, GTA was being discussed in the House of Lords. Lord Campbell of Croy raised concerns about a game involving theft and police pursuits.[^5] The institution was taking the threat seriously while the developers were still trying to make the cars behave.

That mismatch became part of GTA's public identity. Adults arguing that a game was dangerous gave it an aura no advertising department could buy quite as convincingly. Yet outrage alone does not explain its endurance. Plenty of offensive games have disappeared without requiring an international countdown for their sequels. GTA had a repeatable pleasure beneath the scandal: a small decision could produce a large mess.

### London 1969 and London 1961, 1999

The two London mission packs are often treated as a geographical footnote. They deserve better. They showed, early, that changing a GTA's time and place could change its personality even when its underlying actions stayed familiar.

**London 1969** moved the formula into a British period-crime setting: left-hand traffic, different slang, vehicles and a soundtrack that helped establish the era. The smaller **London 1961** followed as a free PC expansion.[^6]

This was the seed of an approach Vice City would make famous. A setting could determine the jokes, the musical references and the sort of criminal fantasy being offered. You could steal a car in several games; the question was what stealing that car made you feel you were starring in.

The London packs also complicate the claim that GTA has always been exclusively about America. It became overwhelmingly associated with America. For a brief period it was perfectly happy to send you down a British street, where organised crime could be conducted in the vicinity of a reasonably priced sandwich.

### GTA 2, 1999: somebody remembers who you annoyed

GTA 2 kept the overhead view but moved into the deliberately strange, retrofuturistic **Anywhere City**. It also made gang relationships central. Work for one faction, damage another, and watch your standing change. Hostile territory could become a practical problem rather than a different colour on the map.[^7]

Here was an early attempt to give freedom consequences beyond the police chase. The city had competing interests. Your access to work depended on how you positioned yourself among them.

Its premise remained broader and less intimate than the character dramas to come. Claude Speed was not asking players to spend fifty hours processing the emotional history of a tracksuit. The gangs were extravagant types, with the Zaibatsu corporation making the overlap between criminal and corporate power especially unsubtle.

At launch, however, refinement could look like repetition. GameSpot's contemporary review enjoyed the game while stressing how much of it remained familiar.[^7] That is useful perspective. GTA was not born as an unstoppable sequence of unanimously recognised masterpieces. The early games were finding their limits, too.

One of their most interesting ideas would later become oddly peripheral: working relationships that change the way the city receives you. GTA VI could learn as much from that respect meter as from another decade of visual improvements.

## 2. GTA III: suddenly, the city has a horizon

**GTA III arrived on PlayStation 2 in October 2001.** Putting the series into 3D changed the player's relationship with almost everything.

The road now ran away from you. A building could conceal an escape route. A hill could reveal the next district. Driving towards a bridge felt different from moving a small car towards a line on a map. You occupied the city from inside the traffic.

The breakthrough was the combination: driving, on-foot movement, gunfights, ambient pedestrians, police escalation and a crime story all operating in one explorable place. GTA III did not invent open worlds. It made this particular mixture feel like something an enormous number of people had been waiting to play. Its launch reviews recognised that immediately, even while acknowledging awkward controls and other rough edges.[^8]

Claude, betrayed during a robbery, works through Liberty City's criminal networks. He barely needs a personality for the structure to function. Other people supply the threats, plans and self-importance; he supplies the labour. It is an arrangement familiar to anyone who has attended a meeting where somebody says, “We should really own this.”

The mob-film influences gave the game an accessible dramatic language. You knew roughly what kind of room you were entering when a crime boss called. The city supplied the atmosphere: industrial grime, rain, commercial clutter and an impression that almost everybody was making a living at somebody else's expense.

Its side activities helped loosen the boundaries of the fantasy. A taxi, ambulance or police vehicle could become the starting point for another sort of play. You might be the least trustworthy emergency worker in the borough, but the city still had a vacancy.

Then there was **Chatterbox**. Radio turned the journeys between missions into encounters with people you never met. Commercials, callers and presenters suggested a society that continued being ridiculous while Claude was elsewhere. It was world-building that did not require the player to stop, read a document and demonstrate sufficient interest in the lore.

GTA III's release environment also demands care. It launched weeks after the September 11 attacks, with Rockstar's New York operation directly affected. Later, the studio explained that it made limited changes, including alterations to packaging and some content. Its own retrospective pushed back against the mythology of a wholesale, last-minute rebuilding of the game.[^9]

The historical distinction matters. A game developed before the attacks entered a profoundly different public atmosphere after them. That does not make every bridge, damaged car or police encounter an intentional response to the event.

GTA III nevertheless established the series' defining tension. Liberty City appeared open to improvisation, while its story required a particular sequence of jobs. The player could discover a hundred ways to make trouble between missions, then encounter a mission that preferred one approved kind of trouble.

For the moment, the city was extraordinary enough to make that bargain easy.

## 3. Vice City: ambition gets a swimming pool

One year later, **Vice City** returned with an argument for style.

Its 1986 setting brought pastel suits, beachside hotels, cocaine fortunes and the combined gravitational pull of *Scarface* and *Miami Vice*. Tommy Vercetti, voiced by Ray Liotta, arrives after a prison sentence and immediately loses his employer's money in a drug deal gone wrong. He spends the game recovering his position, discovering the city's opportunities and becoming considerably more difficult to manage.[^10]

Giving the lead a voice changed the transaction. Claude received everybody else's personality. Tommy pushed back. He could be impatient, sarcastic and furious, allowing a scene to become a contest rather than a briefing delivered to a leather jacket.

The property system gave his ascent a physical shape. Businesses generated work and eventually income. The city could gradually become a collection of things you owned.[^10] Buying a building was satisfying because it made the fantasy legible: you had started as somebody being shouted at over a telephone, and now other people were doing the worrying.

This was GTA's American dream at its most seductive. Yes, the route involved murder. But look at the house.

Vice City's critique of greed and its pleasure in acquisition continually fed each other. The game mocked status while furnishing an excellent selection of status symbols. It understood the attraction of the thing it was ridiculing, which helped the satire avoid the deadness of a lecture written by somebody who had never wanted anything expensive.

The music performed a similar trick. Pop, rock, new wave, electro, soul and talk radio made the period feel coherent without making every inhabitant listen to the same thing. The game could sell a fantasy of the eighties and make fun of the people selling that fantasy, sometimes within the same drive.[^11]

Vice City was released in 2002, looking back at 1986. The distance was sixteen years. GTA VI is arriving twenty-four years after Vice City, with more than twice as much distance from 1986 as the original game had.

Nostalgia has had time to develop nostalgia.

That arithmetic matters because Vice City's atmosphere now comes to players through two memories: the decade it depicted, and the period when they first played it. A modern Vice City cannot reproduce that by adding pink lights. Some of the glow belongs to an old television, a particular room and the absence of adult responsibilities.

The game had limits. Its ethnic gang caricatures can make its supposedly expansive city feel small-minded. Its treatment of women often reduces them to scenery, temptation or a joke. Calling the setting satirical does not settle whether an individual gag has observed anything worth laughing at.

Even so, Vice City made a lasting discovery. A GTA city could become memorable because its parts belonged together. Its strongest identity came from the relationship between the light, the music, the vehicles and Tommy's appetite for ownership. More activities would have meant little if they had broken that mood.

## 4. San Andreas: home becomes an entire state

If Vice City was a beautifully dressed argument for excess, **San Andreas** brought a removal van.

Released in 2004 and set in 1992, it followed **Carl “CJ” Johnson** home to Los Santos after his mother's murder. Family obligations, a weakened neighbourhood gang and corrupt police established a more personal starting point than Tommy's missing investment. The game drew on the cultural world of films such as *Boyz n the Hood* and *Menace II Society*, while its Los Angeles references included gang conflict and the history surrounding the 1992 unrest.[^12]

CJ has a home that matters before the player begins buying anything. That gives his story a different emotional centre. Where Tommy wants to acquire a city, CJ has to decide what he owes the place he left.

The eventual journey through **Los Santos, San Fierro, Las Venturas and the spaces between them** expands into something much stranger than that premise initially suggests. Street drama gives way to rural escapades, elaborate criminal operations and increasingly outrageous machinery.[^13]

The tonal lurch can be wonderful. It can also make the opening's human stakes feel as though they have been packed away for later. San Andreas sometimes handles narrative escalation like a child explaining a dream: and then there was a casino, and then the military, and then you could fly.

It works as often as it does because CJ remains personable. His exasperation makes a useful companion to the player's own. He gives the enormous game somebody to come back to.

Mechanically, San Andreas chased an unusually broad idea of inhabiting a character. Food, exercise, body shape, clothing, haircuts, skills and relationships could affect the experience. Swimming and climbing expanded how you moved. Gang territory made parts of Los Santos contested space. There were bicycles, aircraft and long journeys into places that felt distant from the starting neighbourhood.[^14]

Some of these systems were richer in possibility than in practice. A stat can encourage attachment, or it can ask you to perform maintenance before you are allowed to enjoy yourself. CJ's body gave player choices a visible consequence, but being told to improve a capability could also reveal how quickly immersion becomes homework.

The geography had a deeper effect. Rural roads, forests and desert provided contrast. Distance gave arrival a sense of occasion. San Fierro felt like somewhere you had travelled to, with a different pace and shape, rather than another set of streets unlocked on the same commute.

The radio helped carry those changes. Radio Los Santos grounded the West Coast rap identity, while K-Rose, K-DST and Radio X broadened the musical America surrounding it. Country music could accompany a stretch of open road; alternative rock could change the mood of another journey. A large map became several overlapping cultural spaces.[^15]

San Andreas is therefore a useful rebuke to the idea that density always beats scale, or scale always beats density. Its expansiveness worked because the places differed. A hundred extra kilometres of interchangeable motorway would not have done the same job.

### The scandal that swallowed the conversation

The game launched near the end of 2004, amid arguments about what blockbuster games could depict and who was actually buying them. The following year's **Hot Coffee** controversy transformed those arguments.

The important detail is that the sexual material was present but disabled in the shipped software. The modification exposed it; it did not simply invent the content from nothing. The ESRB changed the rating to Adults Only, retailers pulled copies, and a revised Mature-rated edition followed. In 2006, the FTC announced a settlement over allegations that relevant content had not been disclosed properly.[^16]

The comic irony is obvious: an industry and political culture already familiar with the game's extensive violence became convulsed by a hidden sex minigame. But reducing the whole episode to American prudishness misses the substantive ratings issue. Parents and retailers were relying on a description of what the product contained. The dispute was also about the accuracy of that description.

It helped make San Andreas a cultural object people argued about without necessarily understanding what playing it involved. CJ's family, the state's musical variety and its odd experiments in character embodiment had to compete for attention with something most ordinary players had never seen.

GTA was becoming so notorious that its reputation could obscure the game itself.

## 5. The pocket GTAs tried more than they get credit for

The numbered entries dominate the history. The portable games deserve room because they tested which parts of GTA were essential when the hardware could not do everything.

### GTA Advance, 2004: the alternative route

Released in the same year as San Andreas, **GTA Advance** returned to an overhead view on Game Boy Advance. Digital Eclipse built an original Liberty City story around **Mike**, whose plans to leave crime with his associate Vinnie collapse into a revenge plot.[^17]

In a market being shown the scale of San Andreas, its compromises were easy to notice. The setting and mission patterns echoed GTA III, while the hardware narrowed how the city could be presented. It was a separate game, though, rather than a tiny port of the year's console release.

Its historical value is partly that coexistence. For a while, GTA could mean both a sprawling console production and a more modest interpretation designed for another machine. The brand had not yet become synonymous with a single development event capable of swallowing a decade.

### Liberty City Stories, 2005: the city fits in a bag

**Liberty City Stories** made the more immediately spectacular handheld argument: a recognisable 3D GTA city could run on PSP.

Set in 1998, it follows **Toni Cipriani** back into the Leone family's work. Liberty City is familiar enough to create recognition, but earlier enough in its history for streets, relationships and power to sit differently.[^18]

The story returns to mob obligation, career advancement and the difficulty of pleasing people whose preferred management technique is murder. Its compact mission rhythm suited portable play, even where the underlying design remained familiar. Radio preserved much of the sense of occupying a place rather than visiting a succession of levels.

Released in the year of Hot Coffee, it also made the separation between an adult game and an adult-sized machine impossible to maintain. GTA could now accompany you on public transport. Everyone else on the bus would have to supply their own moral crisis.

### Vice City Stories, 2006: empire-building has a family cost

Set in 1984, **Vice City Stories** centres on **Victor Vance**, whose attempt to support his family through military service goes disastrously wrong. His brother Lance helps connect this earlier story to Vice City's familiar world.[^19]

Victor is an interestingly uncomfortable fit for GTA. He objects to the criminal world while repeatedly doing what it demands. That tension can communicate entrapment; it can also make the character seem less coherent than the game wants him to be. Contemporary reviews noticed the problem. The later series would revisit it with much greater ambition in Niko Bellic.

Its criminal-business system is the more useful forgotten inheritance. Businesses could be established, developed and fought over. Acquisition was attached to a contested city, making empire-building more active than simply collecting revenue from a completed property.[^20]

The musical period-setting remained central, and Phil Collins appeared as himself. It is difficult to imagine a more specific measure of the series' cultural reach than a game about criminal entrepreneurship making room for an actual Phil Collins performance.[^21]

Stories also hints at the kind of future GTA could choose: revisit a strong location with another protagonist and a different social position. A new camera angle on a city can be more revealing than another island attached to it.

## 6. GTA IV: the American dream answers the door in its underwear

**GTA IV arrived in April 2008**, and its Liberty City offered a different sort of promise.

**Niko Bellic**, an Eastern European war veteran, comes to America carrying trauma and unfinished business. His cousin Roman has advertised an enviable new life. The reality involves an unglamorous apartment, a taxi business and debts. Roman has, in effect, been maintaining a highly selective social feed by telephone.[^22]

The joke has affection behind it. Roman's exaggerations conceal hope as well as embarrassment. Niko can be funny, loyal and frightening within the same conversation. These are people with enough warmth to make their circumstances matter.

That warmth distinguishes IV's disillusionment from a general declaration that everybody is awful. Liberty City's promise is hollow, but friendship is not automatically meaningless. The game leaves you something to lose.

Its reputation as the sombre GTA is justified, though incomplete. Brucie exists. A city containing Brucie cannot reasonably claim to have abolished farce.

### Fewer toys, more weight

Compared with San Andreas, IV removed or narrowed many activities and systems. There was less scope for body transformation and no equivalent journey across three cities and their countryside. The emphasis shifted towards a denser urban setting, physical detail, cover-based combat, a more involved phone and social life, and online multiplayer.[^23]

The cars felt heavier. Braking became an opinion you needed to express early. Bodies and vehicles reacted with a physicality that could produce either ugly comedy or an unexpectedly uncomfortable sense of impact.

Those choices served the setting. Niko's Liberty City resists you. Traffic is traffic; a corner requires attention; a gunfight can feel messy rather than merely stylish. The game asks you to spend more time inside an ordinary urban life, even when that life has an extraordinary ammunition budget.

Not every demand improved the experience. Friendships made the supporting cast more present, but the requests could become irritating. A relationship system is doing something peculiar when the player starts regarding an invitation to socialise as an attack on their remaining free time.

Then there is Niko's central contradiction. He wants distance from violence, yet the game repeatedly uses his ability to inflict it as the engine of progress. Some of that is deliberate: his skills keep returning him to the world he hopes to escape. Some is the old GTA mission structure straining under a more serious character.

Players can deepen the mismatch by treating free roam as an extended atrocity exhibition. The story cannot account for everything you do between scenes. Niko's dramatic life and the player's recreational behaviour sometimes inhabit neighbouring apartments and refuse to speak.

Still, IV's ambitions matter. It asked a blockbuster audience to inhabit someone for whom arriving in America did not mean becoming important. Its Liberty City could make opportunity feel physically near and socially inaccessible. You could drive past the life you wanted without getting any closer to it.

### A game released into 2008

It is tempting to make IV sound like a direct response to the financial crash. The dates require more restraint.

The US recession had begun in December 2007, according to the NBER's later dating. IV launched the following April, months before Lehman Brothers collapsed in September.[^24] A game of this size was not written in response to events that happened after it shipped.

Its timing nevertheless changed the resonance of its story. A city built around money, ambition and unequal chances arrived while the real economy was moving towards disaster. The gap between the sales pitch and lived experience was becoming difficult to ignore outside the game, too.

Meanwhile, GTA itself was prospering spectacularly. Take-Two reported roughly six million copies sold to consumers and more than $500 million in estimated retail sales in the first week.[^25] A story about the unreliability of American opportunity had become a demonstration of the entertainment industry's considerable opportunities.

The radio made Liberty City feel inhabited by different communities. Russian-language music, reggae, hip-hop, rock and ambient selections offered more than a generic New York mood. Choosing a station could change which city's inhabitants seemed nearest to you.[^26]

This is one reason later soundtrack changes matter. In 2018, Rockstar confirmed that expiring licences would remove music from IV and its episodes, with a substantial effect on Vladivostok FM.[^27] A patched game could preserve the streets while altering part of their cultural memory. The map remained. The drive was different.

## 7. The 2009 games found three more ways to inhabit Liberty City

### The Lost and Damned: belonging can be a trap

**Johnny Klebitz** approaches Liberty City through the Lost motorcycle club. His conflict with its returning leader, Billy Grey, gives **The Lost and Damned** a more focused concern than becoming rich: what happens when loyalty to a group becomes loyalty to its worst decisions?[^28]

Its motorcycles, gang presence and harder musical identity give the same urban space another rhythm. Riding with a club changes the image of arrival. Niko often looks like someone trying to enter a world; Johnny looks like someone struggling to preserve the world he already has.

The tragedy lies in how little romantic freedom the biker ideal finally offers. The open road can still lead back to the same obligations, grudges and destructive people. Owning a leather jacket does not make a dysfunctional organisation less dysfunctional.

### The Ballad of Gay Tony: loyalty with better lighting

**The Ballad of Gay Tony** moves through **Luis Lopez**, his partnership with nightclub owner Tony Prince, celebrity culture and the expensive lunacy of people such as Yusuf Amir. Parachutes, extravagant missions and nightclub life restore a degree of showmanship that some players missed in IV.[^29]

Tony's sexuality is part of the story's world, but his relationship with Luis gives the episode more to work with than a title designed to cause a conversation. Luis has to navigate affection, exasperation, business and danger. He is protecting a person whose capacity for self-sabotage makes the job unusually demanding.

The game can be loud and ridiculous while allowing that bond to matter. It supplies a particularly useful answer to the idea that GTA must choose between feeling something and blowing something up. The explosion improves when you care who is standing nearby.

Together, the episodes form one of Rockstar's best experiments. Incidents and characters intersect, giving the impression of a city containing several stories rather than waiting exclusively for one protagonist. The same location becomes poorer, richer, more intimate or more absurd depending on who takes you through it.[^30]

For a series now burdened by the expectation that each new entry should rebuild the industry, these shorter works remain an appealing precedent. A city can sustain more than one substantial story. The next project need not begin by moving the coastline.

### Chinatown Wars: the spreadsheet is contraband

**Chinatown Wars**, first released for Nintendo DS in 2009, made an apparently backward-looking camera into a fresh design choice.

**Huang Lee** arrives in Liberty City carrying a family sword, is ambushed and becomes entangled in Triad succession and betrayal. Comic-style scenes and touchscreen interactions establish a different presentation from IV's performances.[^31]

The crucial system is drug trading. Buying cheaply, following market opportunities and selling elsewhere give you reasons to cross the city that arise from its economy. Stock in your possession changes what you stand to lose when the police become interested.[^32]

It is a nasty little commercial simulation, and unusually effective GTA design. The money is attached to decisions. The journey has stakes before a character phones to announce them.

Its wanted system also rewards knocking pursuing police vehicles out of action at lower wanted levels, putting more of the escape into what you do during a chase. The device-specific interactions can be fussy, but the game repeatedly tries to make the player participate rather than watch.[^33]

The release context was revealing. The DS's huge audience and GTA's huge name did not guarantee an immediate sales triumph. Contemporary reports described a disappointing US opening, and Nintendo later publicly discussed its frustration with the game's commercial performance.[^34]

That should not be inflated into a definitive explanation of lifetime sales, or evidence that adult games could never work on Nintendo hardware. It does show that acclaim, brand recognition and platform reach are different things. Chinatown Wars remains valuable because its solutions were specific to its format. Bigger GTA was not its only ambition.

## 8. GTA V: three men, one very expensive identity crisis

**GTA V launched on 17 September 2013**, at the end of the PlayStation 3 and Xbox 360 generation. It went on to pass $1 billion in retail sales within three days.[^35]

By then the franchise was a major entertainment event before anybody had played the new release. GTA III had surprised people. V had to satisfy a world that expected to be astonished on schedule.

Its solution was partly structural: **Michael, Franklin and Trevor** offered three positions within the same Southern Californian economy.

Michael has the house, pool and money. He remains unhappy. Franklin wants a route out of small-time work and a neighbourhood future that seems already mapped out. Trevor operates with a mixture of grievance, cruelty and freedom that makes him both magnetic and exhausting. Switching between them changes geography, class position and temperament, sometimes in the middle of a mission.[^36]

Michael is especially useful to the series' argument. Vice City lets you earn the mansion. V asks you to visit the man inside it. He has acquired the lifestyle and now needs an audience for his dissatisfaction.

Franklin gives the story its clearest route of ascent, but too often becomes the reasonable person standing between more aggressively written personalities. Trevor brings the chaos some players expect from GTA into the authored story itself. His volatility closes part of the gap between a supposedly reluctant protagonist and a player who wants to turn a junction into an insurance event.

It creates other problems. Once atrocity is a regular means of establishing character, escalation can exhaust itself. Shock eventually needs something underneath it. Otherwise the game is just pressing harder on a doorbell everybody has stopped answering.

### The heist becomes the organising fantasy

V's heists give its criminal careers a compelling shared purpose. Planning, selecting approaches and moving between characters make a robbery feel like a coordinated production. The player gets several jobs in the same action film.[^36]

The lighter, more accessible driving and the return of a broad city-and-countryside environment support a sunnier, more elastic game than IV. Los Santos and Blaine County can accommodate an urban commute, a desert disaster and a scenic excursion without asking them to feel like the same experience.

The limitation is how much apparent planning remains under authorial control. You choose within a designed structure. The city outside offers improvisation; the spectacular mission often requires its machinery to run in a particular order. The better the production becomes, the more conspicuous its barriers can feel.

### America discovers it can film itself

V also arrived at a useful cultural hinge. Pew found that a majority of American adults owned smartphones in 2013: 56 per cent.[^37] Self-presentation, social platforms and access to the internet were becoming ordinary features of daily life on a much wider scale.

V's technology companies, entertainment culture, paparazzi and personal brands belong to that environment. Its Los Santos wants to be seen. Even failure benefits from suitable coverage.

The game is often sharpest about people who convert insecurity into products or power. It is less effective when every person enters a scene already carrying the entire joke about them. Constant contempt can flatten a society until its absurdity stops being surprising.

Its women suffer especially from that narrowness. Too many are written mainly to irritate, expose a man's frustrations or decorate an environment. Carolyn Petit's contemporary GameSpot review praised the game's achievements while criticising its misogyny and confused politics.[^38] These objections were present at launch; they have not been retroactively invented by people who arrived too late to understand the fun.

There is also the torture mission. The scene can be read as attacking the usefulness and moral evasions of state violence, but the game makes the player perform the violence before delivering its critique. That is a deliberate, troubling construction. It is fair to ask whether the disgust serves the argument, or whether the argument is being asked to excuse another exercise in provocation.[^39]

### The music learns to follow the mission

V's score added another layer to the series' musical identity. **Tangerine Dream, Woody Jackson, The Alchemist and Oh No** contributed to the original score, with DJ Shadow involved in shaping its soundtrack presentation.[^40]

Radio could keep defining the world around you while composed music shaped the immediate action. A chase now had the city in one ear and a thriller gathering momentum in the other.

That expansion makes music's importance even clearer. GTA is unusually good at giving players memories that are half action and half song. You may forget which mission paid for a car. You remember what was playing when you drove it into the sea.

## 9. GTA Online: the empire becomes your employer

**GTA Online launched on 1 October 2013**, two weeks after V. Its opening was beset by connection and progression problems, with Rockstar publishing updates as it tried to stabilise the service.[^41]

That matters to the retrospective. Online did not arrive fully formed as the indestructible money-making institution later history can make it resemble. It grew through additions and revisions. The original cooperative Heists update arrived in March 2015; Cayo Perico followed in December 2020 and allowed its heist to be tackled solo as well as with others.[^42]

Over time, Los Santos became a place to accumulate businesses, vehicles, properties, routines and shared memories. The audience was creating continuity that did not depend on a new authored protagonist.

This fulfilled a long-standing GTA possibility. The city could be a social setting. Friends could turn a well-designed operation into an appalling rescue attempt, with the resulting failure more memorable than a clean completion.

The cost was a different relationship with money.

In a conventional GTA campaign, acquisition tends to move you towards an eventual state of abundance. In Online, a continuing catalogue of desirable things gives abundance somewhere else to go. Businesses, requirements, payouts and purchasable GTA$ can make the pursuit of freedom feel like managing an expanding portfolio of obligations.[^43]

The joke writes itself: the series spent years mocking American consumerism, then offered a persistent world in which your criminal ambitions could be accelerated at checkout.

That does not make every transaction exploitative or every session a chore. Online has supplied enormous pleasure and sustained relationships. It does make monetisation part of the experience's meaning. When a game jokes about greed, the way it sells you things belongs in the conversation.

### Why people wanted to play ordinary jobs inside GTA

Roleplay communities revealed another appetite. A player might want to become a paramedic, mechanic, officer or small-time operator and maintain relationships inside a city, rather than pursue the next increase in destructive capability.

In August 2023, Cfx.re, the team behind FiveM and RedM, officially joined Rockstar.[^44] That establishes a real relationship between the company and major roleplay infrastructure. It does not, by itself, confirm how VI's multiplayer will operate.

The lesson is still useful. People can want *more life* in a GTA city without wanting more ammunition. A reliable neighbourhood, a small business or a reputation can give an ordinary street enormous significance.

The series began by letting you disrupt a city. Some players have spent years finding ways to belong to one.

## 10. The games changed. So did the company selling them

A retrospective that stops at the software risks making GTA seem like an unbroken stream of ideas from an unchanged group of people.

It is a large, collaborative production, and its personnel have changed. Dan Houser left Rockstar in 2020. Leslie Benzies and Lazlow are other prominent figures from earlier GTA eras who departed.[^45] Recognising their contributions is reasonable. Declaring that a vast studio's future can be calculated from one departure is considerably less so.

The work between GTA V and VI also included **Red Dead Redemption 2**. Thirteen years between numbered GTA releases does not mean thirteen years spent solely on the same game. Nor can the gap simply be explained as Rockstar discovering that Online made money and locking the writers in a cupboard.

Success does, however, change the pressure on a new release. A franchise expected to sustain a large audience for years faces different incentives from one turning out a strange handheld experiment. Financial scale can buy extraordinary craftsmanship while making risk more expensive to approve.

Preserving the earlier games has exposed another problem. **The Trilogy: The Definitive Edition** launched in November 2021 with technical and presentation issues serious enough for Rockstar to issue an apology and announce improvements.[^46] A new version can increase availability while weakening the atmosphere people wanted to revisit.

Alongside the soundtrack alterations in IV, it raises a question the franchise's future should not evade: what does it mean to preserve a GTA? Its atmosphere depends on lighting, weather, voices, music, visual clutter and small incongruities. A cleaner texture does not necessarily preserve those relationships.

And there are the people making it all. The approach to VI's launch includes an employment dispute over Rockstar's dismissal of workers in the UK and Canada. The union alleges anti-union action; Rockstar disputes that and says the dismissals concerned confidentiality breaches. Reporting in October describes tribunal proceedings, not a final ruling resolving those competing accounts.[^47]

The contradiction is uncomfortable without requiring an invented verdict. GTA is expert at finding comedy in powerful organisations. The organisation producing GTA should receive the same scrutiny as any other employer.

## 11. What GTA VI has actually shown us

The return to Vice City is an attractive hook, but the public material describes a broader project: contemporary **Leonida**, with **Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos** at its centre. Rockstar's premise puts the pair inside a criminal conspiracy after an apparently straightforward score goes wrong.[^1]

The important change from 2002 is time. VI is returning to a place associated with nostalgia while setting its story in the contemporary world. The city can no longer rely on the eighties to supply a safely completed cultural identity.

### A criminal partnership, with somewhere to go wrong

Rockstar describes Jason as having grown up around criminals, served in the Army and drifted into drug-running work in the Keys. Lucia has recently left prison and wants a better life. Their relationship offers each a possible route forward.[^48]

That is an especially promising centre for GTA. The series is very good at writing unstable business relationships. A romantic partnership introduces a different set of questions: what do these people owe each other, what do they conceal, and what happens when a good decision for one threatens both?

The appeal is intimacy. A betrayal need not involve an entire criminal empire to matter. It may be enough that somebody you trust has lied about the money.

Lucia is also a major change in the authored leading cast. Calling her GTA's first playable woman would erase the optional women in the original PC game and the female characters players can create in Online. Her significance is as a central, extensively written female lead in the modern cinematic series.[^49]

That distinction gives the writers an opportunity to change who gets to have an inner life. It does not require Lucia to become a model citizen. A GTA protagonist can be selfish, cruel, funny and wrong. The improvement would be letting a woman possess the same range of dramatic possibilities that the men have enjoyed.

### Florida has more to offer than a compilation of arrests

Rockstar has introduced locations beyond Vice City, including **Leonida Keys, Grassrivers, Port Gellhorn, Ambrosia and Mount Kalaga**. Its published cast includes people involved in music, property, robbery, smuggling and conspiracy-minded online life.[^50]

The potential is geographic and social contrast. Wealth, tourism, industrial decline, wetlands, small communities and entertainment ambition can create different relationships to the same state. The danger is treating everything outside the glamorous centre as a supply of eccentric strangers to laugh at.

Rockstar's research account is encouraging here. Aaron Garbut has described trips through Miami and the Keys, aerial reference work and time spent with local people. Rupert Humphries has discussed choosing durable targets for satire rather than chasing short-lived memes.[^51]

Those are statements of intent. Their value will depend on whether Leonida feels observed at human scale. A crowd can contain a lot of polygons and very few people.

### We have moved beyond the first two trailers

The public record now includes **An Extended Look**, released on 27 August 2026, alongside press previews. Rockstar identifies the showcase as footage captured on PS5. Contemporary coverage describes a nearly twenty-seven-minute presentation combining narrative sequences, locations and stretches of play, including shootouts, pursuits and leisure activities.[^52]

That is substantial promotional evidence. It still cannot tell us how consistently the complete game will behave over dozens of hours. A demonstration can show an interaction working; it cannot establish how quickly the player will exhaust its variations.

More interesting than a tally of activities are Rockstar's comments about **mission flexibility**. Rob Nelson has described opening up routes where possible and trying to keep systems such as pursuits consistent between missions and free roam. The examples reported include multiple exits from a tower block and a getaway that can continue without following its suggested optimal route.[^53]

That addresses a longstanding fault line in the series. It suggests Rockstar knows the difference between showing the player something spectacular and letting the player make something happen. We will need the released game to judge how far it has gone.

Nelson has also said money will matter more to progression, including moments when players need to earn cash through activities such as stealing cars or robberies before continuing the story.[^54]

There is an excellent version of that idea. You need money; the city offers several risky opportunities; you choose one; the consequences become your story. Chinatown Wars already demonstrated how an economy can create reasons to move.

There is a tedious version, too. The next interesting mission is waiting, but first you must repeat a reliable cash-producing action until the number is large enough. A requirement to earn money becomes meaningful through the decisions it creates. Poverty alone is not a gameplay system, as several billion people could confirm.

### Stealing a car is becoming a decision again

The reported changes reach the activity on the box. Nelson has described tools and progression governing access to some parked vehicles, while carjacking a driver remains an option. A phone app can provide information about a vehicle's security and value.[^58]

That could make looking at a car more interesting than immediately acquiring it. Is it worth the risk, do you have what you need, and what happens if somebody notices? The possibility of making a poor choice is useful here. A theft game benefits from the occasional theft being a bad idea.

The wanted system is also being described in terms of information. According to Nelson's reported comments, crimes require a witness or alarm to reach the police. Preview coverage identifies recognition of clothing, faces, vehicles and the pair being together, alongside a return to a six-star maximum.[^59]

A pursuit can become more interesting if the police know something specific that you can do something about. Changing vehicles, breaking sight lines and moving through alleys could offer decisions beyond outrunning the nearest patrol car. Whether that information stays legible to the player will matter as much as its complexity.

For the driving itself, Nelson has pitched a combination of IV's sense of weight and V's accessibility.[^60] That is an appealing promise, and remains a developer's description rather than an independent verdict from someone holding the controller.

Refuelling has also appeared in preview reporting, with a brief fill-up sequence.[^61] Its value will depend less on the existence of a petrol station than on when the game makes you need one. Running low during an improvised escape could create a story. Interrupting an enjoyable drive for routine maintenance could simply interrupt an enjoyable drive. GTA has been negotiating this balance since Gary Penn complained about the original game's excessive simulation.

### The newest music news belongs in the picture

The musical plans are clearer than they were when the first trailer sent everyone back to Tom Petty. October's announcements include radio hosts and stations spanning Latin music, African music, metal, pop, Southern rap and country, with names including Bad Bunny, RaiNao, Burna Boy, Robyn, Trick Daddy and Trina.[^55]

On 15 October, Pitchfork reported Rockstar's reveal that **Justice composed the original score**, alongside news of the launch trailer and music from the game's album.[^56]

These are different layers of the soundtrack. The original score, songs made for an accompanying album, and the tracks available across in-game stations should not be collapsed into one number. An album track count is not the total number of songs in the game.

The artistic test is the old one. Does a station sound like part of a place? Can the radio suggest a community, an argument or a history? Celebrity names may attract the headline, but the pleasure of GTA radio often comes from a song you did not know, introduced by somebody you would cross the street to avoid.

### What the announcements cannot settle

The current official release page lists PS5 and Xbox Series X|S. A specific PC launch date should be treated as unconfirmed unless Rockstar announces it; October reporting about a possible PC window is reporting, not a platform announcement.[^1]

Likewise, the future multiplayer economy, the long-term place of roleplay, the full scope of interiors and the quality of individual systems should not be reconstructed from rumour lists. A visible building is not a promise that you can enter it. Two protagonists do not automatically mean cooperative story play.

We have enough real information to discuss VI intelligently. There is no need to promote every plausible wish into a feature.

## 12. What the next GTA should make possible

The history suggests more useful expectations than “everything, but bigger.”

### Let a failed plan become a good story

GTA's finest free-roam comedy comes from a system absorbing a mistake. A minor collision becomes an argument; an argument attracts police; escape requires abandoning the vehicle you meant to keep.

More of that resilience inside missions would be a genuine advance. A getaway should be able to become a different getaway. A surviving witness should sometimes create a complication rather than invalidate the scene. A player who solves a problem in an unexpected, believable way should not always be escorted back to the approved solution.

There will still be reasons to script an encounter tightly. A whole game needs rhythm, and performances need staging. The opportunity is to choose carefully where control matters and where a little disorder could improve the scene.

### Give wealth a scale we can feel

Money works when spending it reveals a priority. Do you replace a damaged car, improve a safe place, buy equipment for a riskier job or help somebody whose favour you might need?

The sums do not need to grow indefinitely. The early struggle for something modest can be more engaging than a late purchase that barely affects how you play. GTA has repeatedly made the climb vivid and abundance comparatively shapeless.

For Jason and Lucia, shared money could also expose character. Two people can agree that they need a better life while disagreeing completely about what one looks like.

Humphries has described the contemporary American dream in more modest terms: a home and freedom from debt, rather than extravagant success.[^57] There is considerable satirical potential in a world where the opening ambition of a criminal epic resembles the target on a mortgage calculator.

### Make the relationship worth the journey

A convincing partnership needs unremarkable moments. Familiar jokes. Small disagreements. The ease of sitting beside someone when neither has anything useful to say.

GTA already spends a lot of time in cars. That is an ideal space for intimacy. People can explain themselves badly, change the subject, argue about music and reveal what a grand confession would conceal.

The worst outcome would be a romance that exists only between gunfights, announced through dramatic declarations while the player does unrelated work. The best would let affection alter how ordinary play feels. A risky decision becomes different when somebody whose company you enjoy is in the passenger seat.

### Find sharper targets than the person nearest the camera

Contemporary satire faces a practical problem: reality can produce a more absurd story before a game has finished rendering its joke.

The answer is observation. There is plenty to say about how money buys innocence, how platforms reward humiliation, how precarious people sell success and how powerful people recast consequences as persecution. The mechanisms endure longer than a particular viral clip.

GTA also needs people who do more than embody the joke. IV's Roman remains funny because optimism and self-deception coexist with affection. Luis and Tony work because loyalty complicates exasperation. A whole city of loud idiots can be entertaining for an hour. Eventually somebody needs to make you care whether they get home.

The satire should also survive a glance at the checkout screen. A new Online can offer spectacular toys without making every evening a negotiation with an economy designed to consume it.

### Keep a future for the smaller story

The main release can be enormous. The franchise's future should have room for work that is more concentrated.

A Leonida story from another class position. A new business system attached to a small cast. An episode that examines one corner of the map properly. The Lost and Damned, Gay Tony and Chinatown Wars show that a GTA can distinguish itself through focus, viewpoint and design rather than a larger promotional number.

A city this expensive to create ought to be good for more than one kind of visit.

## The road back to Vice City

Across GTA's history, the same criminal fantasy has kept changing its address.

The original games offered the pleasure of disrupting a system. GTA III let us inhabit the disruption. Vice City made ownership irresistible. San Andreas gave ambition a home to answer to. IV asked what opportunity meant to somebody who had arrived too late and brought too much with him. V put the successful man beside the aspirant and the man who had abandoned most pretence. Online made the pursuit continue after the story should have ended.

VI's opportunity is to make all that scale answer to two people.

It can give us the spectacular state, the crowded beach, the wildlife, the music and the terrible decisions. The test is whether the details produce a place worth spending time in, and whether the people at its centre make leaving the next mission waiting feel like part of the experience.

For all its money and cultural weight, GTA still needs to understand the appeal of a stolen car with a good song playing.

We will probably crash it before the chorus. It would be nice to care who we crash it with.

Charles Xavier has spent a great deal of his publishing life explaining what the future should look like. In *House of X/Powers of X*, he finally arrives with something more substantial than an aspiration. There is a place. There is a plan. There are terms.

And there is that helmet, which does very little to suggest that questions will be taken from the floor.

I loved these books. I loved the scale of their ideas, the confidence with which they rearrange familiar relationships, and the sense that the X-Men have acquired a future worth arguing about. The political maturity appealed to me as much as the revelations. Jonathan Hickman gives mutant survival an institutional shape, then lets the people inside those institutions remain difficult, ambitious and capable of deceiving one another.

The result makes it possible to feel exhilarated by what the mutants are building while wondering who has authorised the builders. Xavier, especially, becomes fascinating in that uncomfortable space.

**This is a combined review of the two interlocking 2019 miniseries.** The opening sections avoid their major revelations. A clearly marked, closed spoiler section below discusses the full story. Later books receive a short, separately labelled recommendation section because the wider Krakoa era develops several of the things I enjoy most, particularly the Quiet Council and Arakko.

[TOC]

## A new country changes the question

The X-Men's familiar struggle is exhausting by design. They save people who fear them. They ask to belong in a world that keeps finding reasons to exclude them. Their school offers shelter, but a school can be attacked. Their heroism is visible, but gratitude rarely becomes security.

Krakoa changes the conditions of that struggle. Mutants establish a nation on a living island. They have territory, access controls, resources and a diplomatic proposition. The outside world has to respond to an organised political presence, rather than deciding whether it likes the latest group of costumed people on the evening news.

That shift is enormously satisfying. It gives the X-Men room to think beyond surviving the next attack. What does safety require? Who belongs? What will other governments demand? What happens when people with incompatible histories have to share the same future?

Hickman's writing allows those questions to coexist. Krakoa can offer something precious and still require scrutiny. Its critics can identify genuine problems without making every hostility toward mutants reasonable. A reader does not have to hand in their enthusiasm at the first sign of a moral complication.

That is the maturity I responded to. The story takes a persecuted people's desire for security seriously, including the compromises that emerge when desire becomes policy. Its characters have reasons to want the island to succeed. Their reasons do not automatically make every decision defensible.

## House of X builds the present; Powers of X changes its meaning

*House of X* and *Powers of X* work together as one twelve-issue narrative. *House* gives Krakoa its immediate presence: the island, the new arrangements and the people making them real. *Powers* widens the field, moving through different periods and making apparently settled information less comfortable.[^1]

The alternating structure gives the reader two kinds of momentum. One comes from wanting to see the new society work. The other comes from wanting to understand why the story has arranged its history in this particular way. Each answer can change the terms of the first question.

This is a large part of the pleasure. Hickman's revelations reorganise what you have already read. Returning to an earlier scene can expose a different motive beneath a conversation that initially seemed straightforward. The books reward attention without requiring a reader to arrive carrying an encyclopaedia.

The data pages help make that possible. They offer definitions, classifications and diagrams where a conversation would otherwise have to stop and explain an entire system. They also make the world feel administered. Someone has decided what belongs in a category, what deserves a chart and what information is worth recording. Even an apparently neutral explanation contributes to the atmosphere of a society being designed.

Read the books in their intended interleaved order. The chronology is a narrative device; untangling it before the first read would sacrifice some of the experience the structure is built to produce.

## The artists make an enormous idea feel inhabitable

Pepe Larraz and R.B. Silva deserve their names alongside Hickman's whenever these books come up. Larraz gives *House of X* a world that can feel lush, strange and inviting, while preserving the unease of encounters in which someone clearly holds more power than the person opposite them. The island needs to feel like somewhere a person might want to live. Otherwise its political promise would remain an abstract claim.

Silva's *Powers of X* has a different challenge: allowing the reader to follow unfamiliar settings, bodies and technologies as the scale expands. Clean visual storytelling makes the speculative ambition readable. There is a difference between making a future mysterious and leaving the reader unable to tell what happened in a panel.

Marte Gracia's colour work helps bind these different spaces into a coherent experience, with David Curiel also contributing colours. Clayton Cowles's lettering and Tom Muller's design belong to the same achievement. The visual identity is unusually assured, from the organic world of the island to the disciplined presentation of information. The book looks as though its new order has already commissioned a complete set of stationery. That is both reassuring and faintly alarming.

Most of all, the art gives the ideas a seductive surface. You can understand the attraction of Krakoa before considering the terms of admission. A purely ominous island would let us make up our minds too quickly.

## Why Xavier is the most interesting person in the room

Xavier's ambiguity is one of the reasons this reinvention works so well for me. He has long been capable of withholding information and deciding that other people need only part of the truth. Here, that tendency acquires a far larger field of operation.

His confidence can be comforting. Someone has thought ahead. Someone is refusing to accept the old pattern of catastrophe as inevitable. But confidence also makes it harder to know when conviction has become an exemption from accountability.

The helmet reinforces the effect. Xavier is recognisable, yet access to his face is restricted. Other people must interpret the voice, the posture and the assurances. It is a striking visual choice for a man whose project asks so many people to trust his judgement.

I find him more compelling when the book leaves that trust unsettled. I can want his plan to work without wanting his judgement to become the constitution. The possibility that he is doing something necessary makes the problem harder. So does the possibility that he knows it.

## The verdict, before the revelations

*House of X/Powers of X* gave me the excitement of discovering that familiar characters could still move into unfamiliar territory. Its politics, speculative scale and formal design all contribute to that feeling. The books ask the X-Men to organise a future, and the change makes their old conflicts newly productive.

They are demanding in places. Some concepts arrive before their purpose becomes clear; parts of the cast serve the architecture more than an intimate character arc. Those are useful expectations to set for another reader, rather than reservations that meaningfully diminished my enjoyment. I was happy to meet the books on those terms.

The twelve issues tell the founding story while opening questions that later comics pursue. You can appreciate their construction as a single work, but you will finish with a strong invitation to keep reading. For me, that invitation was earned.

If your interest in the X-Men includes difficult leadership, uneasy alliances and the consequences of power, this is an exceptional place to spend time. Let the books introduce their world before looking up the answers.

## Moira makes hope an act of desperation

The revelation that Moira relives her life with the knowledge of previous lives changes the emotional foundation of the project. Krakoa becomes an answer to accumulated failure. Its architects have information that other people do not possess, including reasons to distrust solutions that might once have seemed promising.[^2]

That device gives the scale a human cost. Moira has to carry the knowledge. Every apparent fresh beginning belongs to someone who remembers what another beginning produced. Her exhaustion and determination make the political gamble more understandable without settling whether the people recruited into it have been given enough information to consent.

It also puts pressure on Xavier's original dream. The commitment to coexistence can be sincere while the strategy built around it proves inadequate. Learning that inadequacy through repeated catastrophe would be devastating. The books let that accumulated experience explain a radical change in approach.

Yet superior information creates its own temptation. If you believe that you know how other options end, every objection can start to look like a delay. Moira's secret helps explain the urgency, and it exposes how much of the new society rests on knowledge unavailable to the people being asked to believe in it.

## The Quiet Council turns reconciliation into government

The Council is one of the most exciting creations here. Bringing old enemies into a shared political structure gives the promise of mutant unity a concrete test. Amnesty cannot remain a stirring word once people have to sit beside someone who harmed them.

Xavier, Magneto, Apocalypse, Emma Frost, Mystique and Mister Sinister do not become interchangeable because they have acquired seats. Their presence raises questions about what the nation needs from powerful people, what it is prepared to forgive and who bears the consequences of that forgiveness. The seating arrangement could sustain a diplomatic incident before anyone reaches the agenda.

The treatment of Sabretooth exposes the difficulty sharply. The Council establishes laws and delivers a terrible punishment through a process it controls. His brutality makes it easy to accept that consequences are deserved. The procedure still matters. Who can challenge the rulers? What protections exist when the accused is someone the audience finds less convenient to condemn?

This is where the book's grey morality becomes most rewarding. A political institution can make a decision we understand while establishing a precedent we should fear. Enjoying the Council means wanting to see those consequences explored, rather than assuming that the presence of beloved heroes has made the structure safe.

## Resurrection changes what survival can mean

The Five and Xavier's stored consciousnesses offer an answer to one of mutant history's defining horrors: lives erased before they could become futures. Resurrection becomes a collective achievement, dependent on people working together and on the infrastructure supporting them.

The scene of return has the force of a public ritual. Recognition, belonging and leadership all become part of what could otherwise have been treated as a technical explanation. Krakoa has found a way to make survival visible to itself.

That exhilaration is accompanied by harder questions. Who controls the records? Who determines the queue? Who verifies the person who returns? What becomes of political independence when the continuity of your life depends on systems maintained by the same small group that governs you?

The story gives death a new set of stakes. The ability to restore a body does not erase the experience of dying, the risk of failure or the power attached to controlling restoration. The rescue is extraordinary. So is the responsibility it creates.

## The future makes the present more frightening

The distant horizons of *Powers of X* move beyond the straightforward proposition that mutants must survive humanity's fear. The story considers machine intelligence, directed human development and forms of existence that can make a victory look temporary or misidentified.

That widening scale changes how Krakoa reads. A nation is an enormous development for the people who need a home. Against a sufficiently long future, it may be one strategy in a contest whose boundaries its citizens cannot see.

I like the tension between those scales. The possibility of a cosmic defeat does not make safety today worthless. A place to live still matters. A person brought home still matters. But knowing how far the threat can extend makes the founders' certainty both more understandable and more worrying.

The last turn leaves the celebration intact while changing the light around it. We have watched people make something remarkable. We have also learned how much of the foundation they cannot inspect. That is a much richer ending than a clean assurance that the right people are finally in charge.

## Beyond these books: the Council and Arakko

Some of what I enjoy most about the wider era develops after *House of X/Powers of X*. **Arakko's expanded cast and political culture belong primarily to later stories.** They should not be credited to these twelve issues as though the whole development is already here. What HoX/PoX provides is the foundation and appetite for an X-Men world with more than one way to organise mutant life.

For readers drawn to the same aspects, these are destinations within the later era, not a complete consecutive reading order:

- **Hickman's X-Men, starting with the 2019 series:** the immediate expansion of the new world. Follow its collection notes when it reaches crossovers.
- **X of Swords:** the major next step for Arakko and its people. Read the event in its own chapter order rather than collecting only issues with X-Men on the cover.
- **Planet-Size X-Men #1:** an important bridge toward the setting developed in *X-Men Red*. Read it after *X of Swords* and with its Hellfire Gala context.
- **Immortal X-Men by Kieron Gillen and Lucas Werneck:** the strongest destination for more Quiet Council politics. It starts later, after the status-quo changes of *Inferno*; follow that bridge before beginning.[^3]
- **X-Men Red by Al Ewing, beginning with Stefano Caselli on art:** the destination for Arakko, Storm and a different political culture. It is also a later entry, rather than the issue immediately following HoX/PoX.[^4]

That breadth is part of the original books' success. They create a setting capable of sustaining disagreements between people who all have a stake in its future. The X-Men can fight over what kind of society to become, as well as whether they will be permitted to exist.

I loved being given that possibility. I loved that the people promising it could still make me uneasy. A better future is a powerful thing to offer. These books leave me interested in the people asking for my trust, and alert to the terms.

Jonathan Hickman has a reputation for making comics that require diagrams. This is unfair. Sometimes he supplies the diagrams himself.

There are charts, organisational hierarchies, ominous symbols, impossible histories and people who appear to have been hired specifically to say that everything is proceeding according to plan. Often, everything is proceeding according to three incompatible plans. Somewhere in the building, Reed Richards has neglected dinner.

The reputation catches the surface of the work and misses its most compelling feature. Hickman's best comics put faith in human intelligence, then ask what intelligent people will excuse once they believe everyone else's survival depends on them. Their scale is cosmic. Their recurring problem is painfully recognisable: someone has decided that being the cleverest person in the room also makes them the person entitled to lock the door.

That tension connects a remarkably varied body of work. *Fantastic Four* makes a family the necessary check on genius. *New Avengers* examines the moral collapse of an elite convinced it has no alternative. *House of X/Powers of X* gives an endangered people the machinery of a nation and invites us to admire it, fear for it and scrutinise it simultaneously. *East of West* imagines a political order that has turned apocalypse into an appointment.

These are the essential books, chosen for the force of their ideas, their visual storytelling and how well their ambitions become drama. The order below is an editorial ranking, with the interconnected Avengers saga treated as one undertaking. Major endings and the central HoX/PoX revelations stay unspoiled. For purchases and sequence, use the [Jonathan Hickman reading guide](../../guides/jonathan-hickman/); for other writers, visit [our five-author starting list](../../stories/comic-book-writers-worth-following/).

## 1. Fantastic Four and FF: the family comes first

**The strongest all-round Hickman work.** With Dale Eaglesham, Steve Epting, Nick Dragotta and a substantial artistic ensemble, Hickman turns Marvel's first family into an argument about what progress is for. The collected route covers *Dark Reign: Fantastic Four*, his main-series run and *FF*, the Future Foundation companion book.[^1]

Reed Richards wants to solve problems on the largest possible scale. This is admirable until solving everything becomes a reason to spend less time with the people who make any of it matter. The Council of Reeds gives that temptation a particularly sharp form: intelligence can multiply across realities while emotional judgment stays stubbornly scarce.

Hickman understands that Reed's defining flaw does not have to be stupidity disguised as genius. He can be extraordinarily capable and still need Sue to tell him that a useful discovery is not an adequate substitute for a husband. Family becomes a way of testing ambition against actual obligations. A universe full of theoretical beneficiaries does not release you from the child standing in front of you.

The Future Foundation broadens that argument. A school of unusual children makes the future something adults must prepare other people to inhabit, rather than a private monument to their own brilliance. Valeria's confidence is funny because she belongs to this household; it is disquieting because the household already contains a cautionary example of intelligence outrunning judgment. Franklin gives the story another scale of vulnerability and possibility. Ben supplies the emotional weight that a lesser version of this run would try to replace with another galactic war.

The artists keep its two scales in contact. Eaglesham's physical solidity makes Reed's cosmic work feel like something a body must live through. Epting's faces and staging give grief room to register. Dragotta brings a different energy to the children and the extraordinary spaces around them. The Future Foundation's visual identity makes intellectual cooperation look exciting without requiring every member to acquire matching shoulder armour.

There is a substantial price of admission. Locations, factions and future promises accumulate. Reading only the issues branded *Fantastic Four* eventually means missing half the conversation. But the apparent sprawl has an emotional centre clear enough to hold it: can a man who wants to save everyone remember how to belong to someone?

This is the best first sustained encounter with Hickman because it demonstrates both his architecture and his warmth. It also improves the experience of the later Marvel saga. Understanding Reed as a father, husband and friend gives his relationship with Doctor Doom a force that no multiverse glossary can supply.

## 2. Avengers, New Avengers and Secret Wars: intelligence meets its limit

**The great large-scale undertaking.** *Avengers* expands the public team; *New Avengers* follows a secret group confronting incursions, collisions between universes that make survival an intolerable problem. *Infinity* intersects the two series. *Time Runs Out* carries them toward *Secret Wars* in 2015. Marvel's own collections gather these linked strands, and the eventual event is nine issues by Hickman and Esad Ribić.[^2]

The public Avengers project is built on recruitment and the belief that a sufficiently capable team can meet a sufficiently large threat. The private Illuminati story asks what happens when the threat makes that belief inadequate. Between them sits the difference between a heroic institution's stated purpose and the decisions made in its closed meetings.

Hickman gives the Illuminati a problem that cannot be dismissed by announcing that superheroes should simply be nicer. He then refuses to let the difficulty of their situation cleanse their choices. The most frightening developments arrive when deliberation becomes routine. Something that would once have seemed unthinkable turns into the next item on the agenda.

Black Panther and Namor are crucial because their responsibilities cannot be reduced to an abstract equation. They bring nations, histories and incompatible obligations into the room. Captain America's presence tests the claim that moral refusal is merely an indulgence for people who have not understood the stakes. The series makes room for both the force and the limits of that objection.

Its most effective repetition is structural. A plan is made. Its inadequacy emerges. Another plan requires an additional compromise. The heroes accumulate knowledge faster than they recover moral ground. Apocalypse acquires the atmosphere of a committee that keeps extending its emergency powers.

The art is essential to that escalation. Jerome Opeña helps establish the enormous scope of the early *Avengers* material; Steve Epting makes the rooms of *New Avengers* feel heavy with unspoken decisions. Across the run, artists including Mike Deodato and Stefano Caselli give its confrontations different textures. This is a collaborative visual undertaking, with the inevitable unevenness of a long monthly schedule.

*Secret Wars* finally gives Ribić space to make the consequences monumental. His figures have the weight of damaged sculpture, while Ive Svorcina's colour helps the world feel ancient, unstable and lived in. The setting could have become an excuse to display alternate costumes. Its dramatic centre remains authority: who claims the right to preserve a world, what that preservation costs, and whether creation can be disentangled from possession.

Some of the journey is forbidding. Secondary characters can function more as components than people. Several scenes gain meaning only after later issues. Readers who want every chapter to provide immediate closure may find the run exhausting. Its strongest defence is the destination: the scale contracts at the moment that matters, until the future of everything can be understood through a few deeply personal relationships.

Read the main *Secret Wars* series if you want a shorter encounter. Read the Avengers road to it if you want its ethical pressure. Add *Fantastic Four/FF* first if you want the fullest emotional argument. You do not need every tie-in with a Battleworld logo; the multiverse has already suffered enough administration.

## 3. House of X/Powers of X: a revolution with terms and conditions

**The most electrifying reinvention.** Two six-issue series, with Pepe Larraz and R.B. Silva and colour led by Marte Gracia, establish a new political reality for mutants. They form one twelve-part work and depend on their intended interleaving; Marvel explicitly describes both as essential.[^3]

The remarkable decision is to give the X-Men's familiar argument a change of position. Mutants have spent decades asking whether they will be permitted to exist safely. Here, their leaders begin making offers, setting conditions and constructing institutions. Readers accustomed to treating the X-Men as a besieged school encounter the much stranger prospect of a people learning how to wield power.

That movement is exhilarating because the desperation behind it is legible. It is unsettling because legitimate fear does not make a government's every decision legitimate. Hickman lets the language of liberation coexist with secrecy, hierarchy and the confidence of leaders who have made extraordinary decisions on everyone else's behalf.

The data pages are part of that effect. They make a new society feel as though it possesses rules, supply chains, doctrines and a functioning information system. At the same time, an official document always raises a question: who gets to write the official account? The form makes certainty attractive before the narrative asks us to inspect its authors.

Larraz gives the new homeland a physical generosity that makes the promise feel worth defending. Silva handles temporal and technological complexity without reducing it to a procession of diagrams. Gracia's colour gives the two books a shared sensual vocabulary. The future is persuasive because it looks like somewhere people might actually want to live.

For a reader who loved HoX/PoX, the next Hickman book depends on which pleasure mattered most. Choose *Fantastic Four* for the emotional consequences of an ambitious plan. Choose *New Avengers* for the hidden cost of elite decision-making. Choose *East of West* for rival powers caught in an enormous historical machine.

The qualification matters: HoX/PoX is a brilliant foundation, not the complete history of the Krakoan era. Hickman's later *X-Men* and *Inferno* develop his contribution, but many writers shape the wider line and its eventual conclusion. Selling the whole era as a single author novel misrepresents what the books are. Our [dedicated HoX/PoX review](../../stories/house-of-x-powers-of-x-review/) examines why the opening works so well without pretending it seals every question it raises.

## 4. East of West: the apocalypse is a political project

**The strongest independent epic.** Hickman and Nick Dragotta, with colour by Frank Martin, build an alternate America of competing nations, prophecy, technology and the Horsemen of the Apocalypse. The main series is complete at forty-five issues, collected in ten standard volumes and a full-story compendium.[^4]

The early density can be intimidating. There are people to identify, territorial claims to understand and factions that appear to have spent the previous century rehearsing how best to betray one another. An initial reading can feel like arriving late to the least reassuring diplomatic summit in history.

Dragotta is the reason that density becomes a world rather than a dossier. His silhouettes make powerful figures instantly legible. A pause before violence can feel as charged as the violence itself. Martin's colour lets barren spaces, technological grandeur and sudden brutality coexist without flattening the book into a uniform apocalypse brown.

At its centre is Death, whose personal attachments interfere with the role the world expects him to perform. That gives the political scale an emotional lever. History says events must proceed in one direction; a person has reasons to want otherwise. The question is whether those reasons can withstand institutions built to make the catastrophe happen.

Its leaders also expose a recurring Hickman concern: systems can become self-justifying. A prophecy provides a reason to act, and the resulting actions provide evidence that the prophecy was right. The story's political orders do not merely anticipate disaster. They invest in it.

The book is less immediately welcoming than *Fantastic Four* and more consistently cruel. Some of its enormous cast remains more memorable as an image than as a fully developed person. But its original setting allows consequences and relationships to acquire a finality that shared superhero universes can rarely sustain. It offers a major Hickman construction with an actual ending and no request that you first familiarise yourself with forty years of somebody else's mutants.

## 5. Secret Warriors: the organisational chart has teeth

**The essential espionage book.** Hickman, working from a project launched with Brian Michael Bendis, follows Nick Fury and a younger covert team through a war between secret organisations. Stefano Caselli and Alessandro Vitti are major artistic voices. The original run ends at #28.[^5]

The series is a useful bridge between Hickman's reputation for intricate plotting and the experience of reading him. Its hierarchies, hidden connections and loyalties are recognisable as spy-fiction machinery. You may not know what every symbol means, but you understand that the man withholding information probably has a contingency plan for your funeral.

Fury's competence is part of the attraction and part of the danger. His capacity to see several moves ahead does not make being one of his pieces a healthy profession. The younger characters give that imbalance a cost. From the commander's perspective, a sacrifice may be necessary. From the recruit's perspective, necessity still arrives with a name and a body.

Caselli brings clarity and urgency to the team material; Vitti's harsher textures suit the story's attrition. The run makes the logistics of espionage feel consequential without losing the appeal of people doing dangerous things extremely well.

Its weaknesses are those of a compressed conspiracy story. Revelations can run ahead of emotional processing, and some supporting players never become as substantial as the machinery around them. Even so, it is a more manageable demonstration of the long game than the entire Avengers project, and a good choice for readers who prefer betrayal at human scale to metaphysics at universal scale.

## 6. Decorum: the visual argument

**The best reminder that Hickman's work is not merely a matter of plot.** With Mike Huddleston, *Decorum* offers an eight-issue science-fiction story involving assassins, a courier and extraordinary institutions. Its defining attraction is the breadth of Huddleston's image-making.[^6]

Painted grandeur, graphic abstraction, spare drawing and extravagant design can share the same book. The shifts in register help distinguish kinds of experience, power and space. A world can look overwhelming because the person entering it has been overwhelmed; another can feel precise and remote because its inhabitants have learned to treat living things as manageable material.

That range gives the central contrast between ordinary vulnerability and extraordinary professional capability a visual life. Expertise looks seductive. So does belonging to an organisation with beautiful clothes and terrifying standards. The book is interested in what passes from mentor to pupil besides technique.

It is not the most emotionally rounded selection here. The setting sometimes appears ready to support a much larger story than the one we receive. But as a finite encounter with Hickman's design sensibility and a collaborator capable of answering it with remarkable invention, it earns its place.

## The other books worth knowing about

*The Nightly News* matters because Hickman draws as well as writes it. Its aggressive page design and media satire make the later fascination with information systems easier to recognise. It is a sharper, angrier and more abrasive proposition than his family superhero work, rather than a necessary first step.[^7]

*S.H.I.E.L.D.*, with Dustin Weaver, transforms historical geniuses into participants in Marvel's hidden cosmic history. Choose it for elaborate visual imagination and arguments about whether history has a predetermined end. Its two principal collections, *Architects of Forever* and *The Human Machine*, belong together; the shared acronym does not make it an ordinary Nick Fury mission.[^8]

*The Black Monday Murders*, with Tomm Coker, deserves attention for making finance and occult horror feel like natural colleagues. Its material already published is compelling, but the publisher's catalogue lists the original run through #8 rather than a completed final movement. Coker discussed returning to conclude it in August 2026, but that intention is not a published ending. Readers seeking a finished story should choose *East of West* first and check for a confirmed final collection before buying on a promise of closure.[^9]

*Ultimate Spider-Man*, launched in 2024 with Marco Checchetto and contributions from David Messina, is especially appealing if you want Hickman's interest in possible futures placed inside adult domestic life. This is a different Ultimate continuity from the original Brian Michael Bendis series. Marvel bills #24 as the end of this Hickman/Checchetto run; a cover label saying “Ultimate” does not mean an indefinitely continuing Hickman project.[^10]

## Why the diagrams keep leading back to people

The fairest criticism of Hickman is that the machinery sometimes takes over. A revelation can be easier to admire than to feel. A character can enter a room as an interesting person and leave as a necessary part of a future chart. The density also rewards patience unevenly: an enormous payoff does not retroactively make every intervening conversation graceful.

His best work succeeds by making those liabilities part of its subject. Plans promise to relieve people of uncertainty. They can also turn other people into things that fit. Again and again, the decisive question is what happens when a relationship resists the scheme: a family, an old friendship, a national obligation, a child who cannot be reduced to an expected role.

Start with *Fantastic Four/FF* for the fullest expression of that idea. Start with *House of X/Powers of X* for its most concentrated shock. Take *East of West* if you want a complete original universe, or the Avengers saga if you want to watch a system exhaust every justification it has.

The charts explain how the world is supposed to work. The people explain why it might deserve another chance.

A living street. A superhero who borrows animal abilities. A cat, a dog and a rabbit turned into military hardware. A dying Superman trying to put his affairs in order. Grant Morrison's best comics do not share a sensible definition of sensible.

They do share a moral instinct. A person can be strange, damaged, artificial, unfashionable or extremely difficult to explain at a dinner party and still deserve love. Often the institutions demanding that everyone become normal are far more frightening than the people who cannot comply.

Morrison's reputation for difficulty is earned, but unevenly useful. Some works expect you to assemble connections across decades of superhero history. Others can break your heart in three issues. Advising everyone to begin with the most cryptic book is like recommending swimming lessons by pointing at the Atlantic.

The strongest way into the work is through what each comic asks its artists and readers to do. Frank Quitely gives compassion a physical form. Chas Truog makes an ordinary family's world legible before its borders grow uncertain. Richard Case makes the impossible sufficiently concrete that it can become someone's home. Morrison supplies ideas that need those collaborators to become experiences.

This is an editorial ranking of essential works, with major endings withheld. It favours achievement and range over a complete career inventory. The [Grant Morrison reading guide](../../guides/grant-morrison/) separates approachable books from continuity-heavy projects; our [writer starting list](../../stories/comic-book-writers-worth-following/) connects this feature to Hickman, Moore, Brubaker and Vaughan.

## 1. All-Star Superman: the extraordinary work of being kind

**The best first book and the strongest single recommendation.** Morrison, Frank Quitely and Jamie Grant give Superman twelve issues in which to face his mortality. DC collects the entire series in one volume. You need familiarity with the broad idea of Superman, not a degree in Kryptonian genealogy.[^1]

The impending end changes the meaning of the hero's power. Strength can accomplish extraordinary tasks, but it cannot make time irrelevant. The question becomes how someone with immense abilities chooses to use the time left to him.

Morrison takes the exuberance of older Superman stories seriously. The impossible experiments, extravagant threats and strange relatives are part of the world through which his responsibilities must move. A smaller, more respectable version of that world would give him less to love.

Quitely's contribution is particularly important when Clark Kent occupies the page. His posture changes what people see. Apparent awkwardness can conceal extraordinary attention to the people around him. The double identity becomes a performance made out of weight, shoulders, space and timing, rather than something a reader must politely overlook.

The series repeatedly makes saving a person feel as consequential as confronting a cosmic danger. Its celebrated acts of reassurance work because the book has established a man who notices. Compassion is a use of attention before it becomes a demonstration of strength.

Lex Luthor supplies the counterargument. Exceptional intelligence can become a prison when its owner interprets another person's existence as an insult. Superman's approaching death exposes the poverty of a life organised around defeating him. The contrast makes the story's optimism feel considered rather than automatic.

Its episodic structure can initially seem loose. Some chapters feel like doors into stories the series does not have time to pursue. The accumulation is the point: a life becomes visible through obligations, unfinished work, old relationships and the things someone chooses to repair. By the end, the extravagance has acquired the intimacy of a farewell.

## 2. Animal Man: who is responsible for the pain?

**The clearest encounter with Morrison's ideas about fiction.** The twenty-six-issue run, principally drawn by Chas Truog with Doug Hazlewood and other collaborators, starts with Buddy Baker trying to be a superhero while maintaining a family and taking animal suffering seriously. It develops into a much stranger examination of who controls a story.[^2]

Its ordinary domestic footing is crucial. Buddy has bills, children and a marriage. His moral concerns create friction rather than automatically making him admirable. Because his life feels inhabited, the book can question its rules without treating that life as disposable.

“The Coyote Gospel” is the early demonstration of what the series can do. Its shift in visual and narrative logic turns a familiar entertainment tradition into a question about suffering. The violence that one genre asks us to find funny can look unbearable from inside the victim's experience.

That concern reaches beyond formal cleverness. A story can always arrange another ordeal for a character; an audience can always ask for greater stakes. *Animal Man* asks what desire is being satisfied and whose pain is being used to satisfy it. The book becomes most moving when an ingenious formal device produces an ethical problem that ingenuity cannot neatly solve.

Truog's directness gives this development its foundation. The everyday world does not look as if it was designed solely to demonstrate a philosophical thesis. The drawings allow Buddy's bewilderment and concern to remain human when the narrative becomes increasingly unstable.

Some of the topical material carries the texture of its late-1980s context. The changes in register will also frustrate readers who hoped for a straightforward animal-powered hero. But the run's endpoint has a rare quality: it makes the relationship between creator, character and reader feel like a matter of responsibility. Read it before reading explanations of it.

## 3. Doom Patrol: a home for people the world cannot categorise

**The essential long strange trip.** Morrison's run begins at *Doom Patrol* #19 and continues through #63, with *Doom Force Special* #1 included in the omnibus. Richard Case is its defining artistic collaborator.[^3]

The book's weirdness is not random decoration. It gives physical form to the experience of being told that your mind, body or identity makes you unfit for ordinary life. A person whose situation resists a simple category needs more than an invitation to become less inconvenient.

Cliff Steele's robot body makes separation from ordinary human experience brutally literal. Crazy Jane's presentation is more complicated and should not be treated as clinical guidance, but the story's interest in trauma and fragmented experience helps distinguish her from a mere catalogue of powers. Danny the Street turns the idea of refuge into geography.

Case's great achievement is consistency within impossibility. An absurd entity occupies space and interacts with other entities. That gives the characters a world to negotiate. Without that discipline, the book could dissolve into a collection of surreal descriptions waiting for someone to draw them.

The antagonists also challenge the assumption that order must be good. Systems of classification and conformity can become violent when confronted with lives they cannot process. The Doom Patrol's solidarity matters because it is practical: these people keep trying to live alongside one another when conventional heroism has no useful instructions.

The run is demanding, sometimes abrasive and very far from a tidy adventure series. Its treatments of trauma and bodily difference can be uncomfortable. Its lasting strength is the conviction that the misfits deserve a world, rather than an explanation for why they cannot belong to this one.

## 4. We3: three issues, almost no protection

**The most concentrated work.** Morrison and Quitely's three-issue story follows three animals transformed into weapons and attempting to escape their intended function. Jamie Grant's colour and digital production help give its violence an unnerving precision.[^4]

The animals' limited speech does an enormous amount of work. They do not need to become witty human personalities to earn the reader's concern. Their needs are immediate and comprehensible. The gap between that simplicity and the elaborate technology imposed on them is the story's wound.

Quitely makes action feel distributed across time and space. Small panels and fragments isolate movements that a conventional splash page might compress into a single heroic image. The resulting spectacle is impressive, but it also makes bodies vulnerable. The reader experiences destructive capability alongside the fact that someone inside the machine is afraid.

There is sentiment here, unapologetically. The book earns it through specificity rather than presenting generic animal innocence as a substitute for character. The creatures disagree, misunderstand and rely on one another. Their relationship is legible before the story asks you to mourn anything.

The warning is simple: **expect graphic violence and animal suffering**. This is an easy continuity entry point, not an emotionally gentle one. If that material is a firm boundary, begin with *All-Star Superman* instead.

## 5. New X-Men: the school becomes a culture

**The most consequential Marvel reinvention in this selection.** Beginning at #114, Morrison's run takes the X-Men through #154 and the 2001 annual. Frank Quitely establishes the new visual direction; artists including Igor Kordey, Phil Jimenez, Chris Bachalo and John Paul Leon contribute to the run.[^5]

The central achievement is to imagine mutants as a developing culture. Xavier's school becomes a place with students whose bodies, tastes and anxieties cannot all be translated into glamorous combat roles. The difference between being a member of a super-team and belonging to a minority becomes more visible.

That gives the adult cast a more difficult job. They must teach people to live in a world, not simply train future recruits. Emma Frost's presence is productive because her judgment, vanity and perceptiveness complicate the school's preferred account of itself. The students can expose contradictions that their teachers have grown accustomed to overlooking.

The run is also willing to make familiar relationships awkward. Scott and Jean do not become uninteresting because readers recognise them. Their history makes the distance between them more painful. The book treats emotional habits as things that can fail under pressure, even when those habits have supported decades of stories.

There are substantial qualifications. The visual changes between arcs are pronounced. Some revelations and portrayals remain divisive, and later continuity reworks parts of the material. Its sexual and body-horror elements are stronger than a new reader might expect from the franchise name.

It belongs here because its ambition is social as well as spectacular. Read it alongside Hickman's *House of X/Powers of X* and an illuminating contrast emerges: Morrison foregrounds the strange, often untidy experience of a mutant culture, while Hickman's opening concentrates on the institutional force of mutant nationhood. Neither supplies the final word on what the X-Men can mean.

## 6. Batman: an inheritance large enough to change

**The most rewarding commitment for established superhero readers.** Morrison's Batman project moves from *Batman and Son* and *Batman R.I.P.* through *Batman and Robin*, *The Return of Bruce Wayne* and *Batman Incorporated*. Andy Kubert, Tony S. Daniel, Frank Quitely, Frazer Irving, Yanick Paquette and Chris Burnham are among its key artists.[^6]

Its generous premise is that Batman's strange history can belong to the character. The bizarre adventures do not all have to be filed away as embarrassing evidence of an era before the franchise discovered rain. They can become experiences Bruce has absorbed and prepared for.

This makes Batman's identity expansive, but the most appealing movement is the change in partnership. Dick Grayson as Batman and Damian Wayne as Robin invert familiar expectations. The older partner brings relative warmth and social ease; the younger one brings severity, suspicion and the belief that he already knows everything worth knowing. The friction supplies both comedy and genuine growth.

The Batman idea also becomes transmissible. It can survive an absence, move between people and operate internationally. That raises questions about whether Bruce is building something that can outgrow him or merely enlarging his personal mission. The family remains the most reliable test of his answer.

The difficulty is structural. *Final Crisis* supplies a major transition that the Batman collections do not simply replace. *The Return of Bruce Wayne* and *Batman and Robin* overlap. A bare list of trade titles can give a newcomer a misleading sense of straightforward sequence. Our guide explains a manageable collected route and what remains outside it.

For a focused sample, read *Batman and Robin: Batman Reborn*, accepting its changed status quo. For the full statement, begin earlier and follow the arc through *Batman Incorporated*. The reward is a Batman expansive enough to contain both impossible preparation and the uncomfortable work of being a father.

## The advanced shelf: The Invisibles, Seven Soldiers and Final Crisis

*The Invisibles* is the major countercultural project: conspiracy, identity, liberation and reality itself made unstable across three volumes of numbering. Its collaborators include Steve Yeowell, Jill Thompson and Phil Jimenez. It is a substantial adult commitment, and the first volume contains disturbing violence and sexual material. Choose it because that investigation interests you, not because someone has mistaken confusion for a qualification exam.[^7]

*Seven Soldiers* builds an ensemble event from an opening special, seven four-issue miniseries and a closing special. Its pleasures include watching separate lives contribute to a shared outcome without becoming an ordinary team adventure. Use the assembled collection's sequence. J.H. Williams III and the individual miniseries artists are indispensable to how the architecture becomes readable.[^8]

*Final Crisis* is rewarding when its mythic compression meets a reader who already enjoys DC's history. It is an unnecessarily difficult sales pitch for someone who just wants to understand why Morrison matters. The fact that *We3* and *All-Star Superman* exist should retire that particular initiation ritual.

## What the strange worlds make possible

Morrison can be uneven. Formal ambition can move faster than explanation, and emotional transitions occasionally arrive with less preparation than the conceptual machinery. Some readers will find the work liberating; others will feel that they are doing too much of the assembly themselves.

But the finest books make a persuasive case that imaginative freedom is an ethical resource. If a street can be a person, the category of person has expanded. If Superman can be both cosmically powerful and attentive to a stranger, kindness need not be an apology for weakness. If an animal built to kill can want a home, its assigned function has failed to describe its life.

Start with *All-Star Superman*. Follow with *Animal Man* or, if you can bear its subject, *We3*. Then let your interests decide how strange the next room should be. There are plenty of doors. Morrison's best work wants more people to fit through them.

Alan Moore's influence has occasionally suffered the same fate as a complicated recipe copied by someone who remembered only the chilli. There were troubled superheroes, so troubled superheroes must have been the point. Add violence. Reduce sunlight. Serve with an expression of adult seriousness.

The actual books are much stranger and more various. *Swamp Thing* finds romance in a body that appears to make romance impossible. *From Hell* turns a city into an instrument of class and gendered violence. *Top 10* treats a world of superpowers as a place where people still have shifts, neighbours and embarrassing problems. *Promethea* asks the page to become a route through an entire cosmology.

Moore's strongest work repeatedly tests the stories through which people understand their lives. A superhero story can conceal a fantasy of authority. A monster story can depend on an unnecessarily narrow definition of humanity. An institution can explain away cruelty by placing it inside a respectable narrative. The interest lies in what happens when those explanations stop being sufficient.

This is a selected ranking of the work, judged through its ideas and its execution with extraordinary artistic collaborators. It withholds major resolutions, including *Watchmen*'s central revelation. The [Alan Moore reading guide](../../guides/alan-moore/) handles editions and routes; the [five-writer starting list](../../stories/comic-book-writers-worth-following/) gives alternative paths through modern comics.

## 1. Watchmen: the panel is part of the argument

**The strongest complete statement.** Moore, Dave Gibbons and colourist John Higgins build a twelve-issue investigation into masked heroism, fear and authority. It is self-contained; the surrounding franchise is not required reading.[^1]

Its most important formal resource is the regular panel grid. Repetition lets a tiny change acquire significance. An object, a phrase or a gesture can recur in a different setting until the reader has to reconsider what it means. The apparent discipline of the page also makes deviations feel deliberate.

Gibbons's clarity is fundamental. The book asks readers to track objects, expressions, spatial relationships and visual echoes. That only works if those things can be seen. Higgins's colour adds emotional pressure and distinguishes environments without sacrificing their relationship to the larger design.

The result makes rereading unusually productive. Knowing the plot changes the function of the evidence. A detail that first seemed atmospheric can become an indictment, an admission or a warning. The formal pleasure and the ethical discomfort deepen together.

The characters are compelling because their explanations for themselves are inadequate. Rorschach's severity offers a seductive simplification of the world, but the book supplies ample reason to distrust it. Dr Manhattan's extraordinary perspective does not automatically equip him to attend to other people. Nostalgia, impotence, terror and vanity enter the argument alongside explicit political beliefs.

The book's violence matters, but cruelty alone does not account for its achievement. Its essential question concerns the authority people claim when they decide they understand what everybody else needs. The tension survives without reducing the answer to “superheroes are bad”. Several characters can perceive something true while remaining disastrously wrong about what that truth permits.

There are limitations worth acknowledging. Women carry a heavy burden in the book's treatment of trauma, and some readers will find its sexual politics constraining. Its alternate Cold War context also deserves to be understood rather than replaced with a vague sense of permanent darkness. A masterpiece can reward attention and still deserve scrutiny.

## 2. Saga of the Swamp Thing: the monster learns another way to live

**The best first sustained Moore run.** Beginning with issue #20 and the decisive “Anatomy Lesson” in #21, Moore reworks the premise of a creature who believes himself to be a transformed man. Stephen Bissette, John Totleben and Rick Veitch are among the central artists. The familiar six-book sequence gathers the run and related material.[^2]

The remarkable move is to turn an apparent loss into an enlargement of possibility. What if a life does not become worthless when the explanation attached to it is wrong? The book questions the assumption that its protagonist's only worthwhile future would be restoration to a conventional human condition.

That idea makes the romance with Abby substantial. Love cannot simply confirm that everything unusual about him is secretly normal. The relationship asks both characters, and the reader, to imagine forms of contact and companionship that their society has little language for.

The art allows that imagination to become sensual and frightening. Vegetation can feel fecund, decaying, intrusive or ecstatic. Bissette and Totleben make bodies and environments difficult to separate; Veitch develops another distinctive range of physical and cosmic possibilities. Nature is not merely a reassuring place beyond civilisation. It contains processes that ignore human preference.

The horror also ranges beyond the protagonist. Violence, prejudice and exploitation appear in communities whose ordinary appearance offers no protection against them. The larger mythology expands because the local horrors have already established stakes. When the book travels into the cosmic, it carries a concern for particular lives with it.

Some passages are very much of their period, and the narration can be luxuriant. Readers seeking brisk superhero pacing may find the prose thick. But the density is often purposeful: sensation and thought spill beyond the tidy identity that once contained them. This is a reinvention that changes the emotional possibilities of the character, rather than merely updating his origin paperwork.

## 3. From Hell: the city participates

**The deepest and most demanding original work here.** Moore and Eddie Campbell use the Whitechapel murders as the basis of a historical fiction about violence, class, misogyny and the stories later built around catastrophe. It is not a reliable solution to the real murders. The annotations are part of how the book distinguishes research, speculation and invention.[^3]

The displacement of the usual mystery is crucial. The book is less interested in making a murderer into an entertaining puzzle than in asking what social arrangements allow violence to acquire its particular targets and meanings. Streets, architecture and institutions become part of the explanation.

Campbell's London is indispensable. Its roughness resists the clean separation between people and their surroundings. A face can be difficult to read; a room can feel oppressive because of its accumulated lines. The city has texture rather than the immaculate theatrical gloom of a heritage-crime postcard.

The victims' circumstances matter. Their economic insecurity and ordinary attempts to get through a day make the gulf between a grandiose account of history and the lives it consumes impossible to ignore. The book can be disturbing precisely when it slows down and restores the everyday time that the legend has erased.

Its speculative architecture is ambitious and sometimes overwhelming. The long excursions through the city can feel obsessive because the consciousness organising them is obsessive. A reader does not have to accept that consciousness's claims to understand how power recruits narratives to justify itself.

This is an adult work with graphic violence, sexual material and sustained misogynistic brutality. It is also long and dense. The black-and-white edition and Campbell's later colour *Master Edition* present different visual experiences of the same central work. Neither requires buying the other for a missing ending.

## 4. V for Vendetta: liberation is an uncomfortable inheritance

**The essential political work.** With David Lloyd, Moore imagines a fascist Britain and the masked figure working to bring its order down. The complete graphic novel provides the story; the film adaptation makes substantial changes and should not be treated as its summary.[^4]

The mask is the book's most commercially successful image, which creates an irony the book itself equips us to recognise. An image can circulate far beyond the argument that produced it. Wearing a symbol of resistance does not settle what freedom means or how a society should be rebuilt.

Lloyd's use of shadow makes concealment part of the everyday environment. Faces and spaces are withheld; authority becomes a condition under which people live, rather than a single villain awaiting a dramatic entrance. The atmosphere gives personal fear a political geography.

The most difficult material concerns V's own methods and Evey's experience of them. The book's commitment to opposing fascism does not make everything its protagonist does comforting. It asks what it means to be made free through someone else's absolute control, and the answer cannot be taken for granted merely because the person claiming control has a better slogan.

Its political alternatives are deliberately sharper than a generic battle between a bad government and a cool rebel. Readers may disagree with the argument or find elements dated, but the specificity gives that disagreement substance. The serious way to engage with *V for Vendetta* is to attend to its politics as well as its iconography.

## 5. Top 10: the superhuman city still needs people to do their jobs

**The most welcoming surprise.** Moore, Gene Ha and Zander Cannon build a police procedural in Neopolis, a city where extraordinary abilities are ordinary. The original twelve-issue series supplies the recommended starting point.[^5]

The conceit could have been exhausted by a page of visual jokes. Instead, the ordinary pressures of work give the extraordinary setting coherence. Colleagues have different competencies, prejudices and temperaments. Cases compete for attention. A public incident does not stop mattering because the next spectacular thing has arrived.

Ha and Cannon fill the environments with information, but the book's crowdedness is also social. This is a city with a population rather than a backdrop for exceptional people. A fantastic body or improbable power does not release anyone from embarrassment, loneliness or the need to negotiate shared space.

Its procedural structure lets the book change scale quickly. An absurd premise can produce a melancholy case. A joke in the background can coexist with distress in the foreground. The mixture is the argument: a world in which everyone is extraordinary would still contain ordinary human needs.

The institutional perspective deserves scrutiny, as it does in any police procedural, and some humour carries the assumptions of its period. But the book offers valuable evidence against the idea that Moore's contribution was a formula of increasing misery. There is wit, affection and curiosity here, alongside the crime.

## 6. Promethea: a comic that wants to change the reader's route

**The most sustained formal and metaphysical experiment in this selection.** Moore works with J.H. Williams III and Mick Gray to take a superhero-like premise into an exploration of imagination, symbolism and magical systems. The original series runs to thirty-two issues.[^6]

The pages increasingly ask to be navigated as environments. Borders, symmetries and compositions carry ideas that would lose force if translated into a plain paragraph. Williams makes the act of reading feel like movement through a designed space. Gray's inking contributes to the precision that keeps the elaborate structures legible.

That ambition is exhilarating when form and experience coincide. It can also become didactic. Readers who want the initial adventure premise to remain the centre of the book may feel that a story has been exchanged for a guided tour of a worldview.

The distinction matters when choosing where to begin. *Promethea* is not a neutral textbook, and its cosmology need not become your belief system for the comic to be valuable. It is a demonstration of what these creators can make sequential art do with an intensely held set of ideas. Read it when that prospect sounds inviting, rather than as a compulsory step after *Watchmen*.

## What belongs on the next shelf?

*The Ballad of Halo Jones*, with Ian Gibson, is a valuable route toward Moore's science fiction and his interest in an ordinary woman's life inside enormous historical forces. Its three existing books should be approached as the material actually produced, rather than a guarantee of every future chapter once envisaged.

*Miracleman* is central to any fuller study of his treatment of superhuman power, with major contributions from Garry Leach, Alan Davis, John Totleben and others. Editions and author credits have a complicated history, and Neil Gaiman's continuation is a different phase. Our guide keeps that distinction visible.

The Superman stories “For the Man Who Has Everything” and “Whatever Happened to the Man of Tomorrow?” provide shorter encounters with Moore's ability to expose a familiar hero's emotional stakes. They also make a useful companion to Morrison's *All-Star Superman*: different approaches to farewell and responsibility, each dependent on knowing what the hero has meant to people.[^7]

*The League of Extraordinary Gentlemen* is another major branch, with Kevin O'Neill's extraordinary designs and linework inseparable from its identity. It becomes increasingly allusive and contains material, including sexual violence, that makes a casual recommendation inadequate. Its existence expands the picture of the career; it does not have to be everyone's next purchase.

## The range matters more than the reputation

Moore's writing can be ornate, severe and overly insistent. The same appetite for structure that produces *Watchmen*'s formal precision can sometimes make an argument feel as if it has occupied every available room. Some recurring uses of sexual violence deserve criticism rather than exemption on the grounds of prestige.

Yet reducing the work to its harshest elements misses the imaginative generosity elsewhere. *Swamp Thing* makes an unfamiliar kind of life capable of love. *Top 10* finds room for human frailty inside an absurdly crowded city. Even *Watchmen*, with all its dread, depends on small acts and relationships that its grand theorists cannot fully explain away.

Choose *Watchmen* for a complete work of exceptional construction. Choose *Swamp Thing* to see an inherited premise grow new emotional possibilities. Choose *Top 10* if you would like the famous severe wizard to tell you a very good story about people at work. There is considerably more on the shelf than the imitation ever suggested.

Someone in an Ed Brubaker comic usually has a very convincing reason for making a terrible decision. They need money. They owe a friend. They have one last chance to correct an old mistake. The job is simple, the risk is manageable, and the reader should begin worrying immediately.

The appeal goes beyond competent people doing crimes. Brubaker is particularly good at the explanations people use to remain bearable to themselves. His characters can recognise danger, describe it accurately and walk straight into it because recognising the truth has not removed their need for something else.

That gives his noir a distinctive sadness. A person may understand the trap and still have no emotional life outside it. Old loyalties become a form of gravity. Places retain the consequences of things their inhabitants would rather forget.

Sean Phillips is essential to this achievement. Across a long collaboration, his faces, rooms and shadows turn private accounts into visible worlds. Val Staples, Elizabeth Breitweiser and Jacob Phillips, among the colourists who have worked on different projects, help give those worlds specific emotional temperatures. Calling these books Brubaker's best work is useful shorthand for finding them; it should never obscure whose work makes them comics.

These are the strongest starting points and major achievements, ranked editorially with endings protected. The [Ed Brubaker reading guide](../../guides/ed-brubaker/) separates independent books, shared settings and superhero runs. For a wider selection, use the [five-writer starting list](../../stories/comic-book-writers-worth-following/).

## 1. Criminal: the past already knows your address

**The defining body of work.** Brubaker and Sean Phillips build an interconnected criminal world through stories that often have their own protagonists and immediate resolutions. *Coward* is the cleanest first entry. Publication order lets recurring people, locations and family histories accumulate meaning.[^1]

The great advantage of the format is that apparent marginality can become centrality. Someone glimpsed in one person's story can later become the person through whose fears we understand the world. Knowledge moves between books unevenly, as it does between people. A reader may know what a character cannot know about their own family.

That makes the shared setting more than a branding device. The books explore how violence is transmitted: through stories about fathers, through professional habits, through the idea that a person from this neighbourhood has a limited menu of possible futures. Escaping a job is simpler than escaping the account of yourself that made the job seem inevitable.

*Coward* is an excellent introduction because Leo's caution gives criminal expertise a psychologically legible form. Rules are how he manages fear. A heist story can then test both the practical value of those rules and the needs they cannot satisfy. The book makes competence interesting without confusing it with moral safety.

*The Last of the Innocent* demonstrates a different resource: the visual language of remembered youth can become evidence against the person doing the remembering. Phillips's shifts in style expose how nostalgia edits a life. The title's apparent innocence becomes a question the book keeps making harder to answer.

*Cruel Summer* gains particular force after earlier encounters with the Lawless family. Its historical position does not make it the best first book. Knowing something of the consequences gives the past a pressure that simple chronology would remove.

The limitation is also the attraction. These stories often restrict the space available for renewal. Readers who need sustained hope may find the accumulated fatalism wearing. But within those confines, *Criminal* achieves an unusual breadth: a world where almost everyone has a private account of how things went wrong, and those accounts cannot all be reconciled.

## 2. The Fade Out: Hollywood knows how to change the story

**The strongest single complete series.** Brubaker, Sean Phillips and Elizabeth Breitweiser set a twelve-issue murder story in Hollywood at the beginning of the blacklist era. The *Complete Collection* contains the entire main story.[^2]

The murder mystery supplies forward movement. The deeper concern is an industry that manufactures narratives and can apply the same skill to inconvenient reality. A public explanation does not have to be true to become the explanation everyone must live under.

Charlie Parish is a particularly effective centre because his compromised position is built into his work and relationships. He is neither an omnipotent investigator nor an innocent observer who can remain clean while exposing everybody else. His need to keep functioning inside the system makes every discovery harder to use.

Phillips gives the period a material presence without turning every frame into a museum display. Faces can feel tired before anyone explains why. Breitweiser's colour supports the contrast between the appeal of the public image and the unease of the private space. The dream factory looks attractive enough to explain why people submit to it.

The blacklist context sharpens the institutional danger. Reputation becomes both a commodity and a weapon. A person's usefulness can determine how much truth the organisation is prepared to acknowledge about them. The question of what happened to one woman widens into a question about which lives the machine can absorb without interrupting production.

The book does not provide the uncomplicated satisfaction of a detective who can fix an institution by identifying its worst member. That may frustrate readers looking for a clean triumph. It is precisely why the ending carries weight: the investigation and the possibility of justice were never the same thing.

## 3. Pulp: an old story meets the present

**The best short introduction.** In this original graphic novel, Brubaker, Sean Phillips and Jacob Phillips follow Max Winters, a writer of Western pulp stories in 1930s New York. His relationship with the violence he sells gives the book its tension.[^3]

The contrast between the Western and the Depression-era city could have become a clever genre mash-up. Instead, it makes the question of moral action concrete. A familiar tale of an outlaw taking matters into his own hands is one thing; deciding what violence means in the world you actually inhabit is another.

Max's age matters. He has fewer opportunities to pretend that the consequences belong to a future version of himself. Physical limits, financial need and responsibility compress the space in which a choice can be made. The narrative's brevity gives those pressures little room to dissipate.

The Phillipses give the two periods distinct textures without severing their relationship. The story someone tells about the past can make that past appear cleaner and more purposeful than the present. The images let the reader feel both the attraction and the danger of that simplification.

It is a remarkably efficient book, but efficiency comes with a trade-off: supporting lives receive less space than they would in *Criminal*. Choose it for a finished evening's reading and a concentrated encounter with this collaboration. It needs no other book to deliver its effect.

## 4. Captain America: Winter Soldier: history comes back armed

**The essential superhero starting point.** Brubaker and Steve Epting, with artists including Michael Lark and Mike Perkins, give Captain America a thriller shaped by espionage and unresolved history. The *Winter Soldier Ultimate Collection* gathers *Captain America* (2005) #1–9 and #11–14.[^4]

The central reinvention works because it attacks a relationship rather than merely expanding a villain catalogue. Steve Rogers's past is part of the moral vocabulary through which he understands the present. Make that history unstable and the disturbance reaches much further than his memory.

Epting's subdued staging and physical weight are crucial. Captain America can still perform extraordinary feats, but the environments invite surveillance, uncertainty and sudden damage. The book's world feels capable of concealing things from him.

Brubaker uses that vulnerability to test a particular kind of loyalty. What does believing in someone require when their history contains acts you cannot ignore? How much of a person can an institution claim to have made or remade? The superhero setting makes those questions spectacular without depriving them of personal force.

The later Captain America run is a much larger commitment, with events, renumbering and further changes of role. The opening *Winter Soldier* story is a practical place to judge whether you want that commitment. Familiarity with the film will give away an identity, but it will not reproduce the comic's pacing, political setting or chain of consequences.

## 5. Gotham Central: the signal is someone else's solution

**The finest collaborative institutional portrait here.** Created by Brubaker, Greg Rucka and Michael Lark, *Gotham Central* follows detectives working in a city whose most famous crime-fighter answers to no ordinary chain of command. The complete series runs forty issues. Later contributors and the division of writing matter: this is not forty issues all written by Brubaker.[^5]

The setting changes scale without removing the extraordinary. A costumed villain is a recurring professional hazard for people whose equipment and bodies are not designed to survive one. The possibility of Batman's intervention is reassuring and humiliating at once. It can save lives while underlining how little control the detectives possess.

The procedural form makes that imbalance productive. Officers have partners, shifts and responsibilities. There is work to be done after the spectacular incident is over. The city has victims whose stories cannot all be organised around the hero's next revelation.

Lark's matter-of-fact rooms and faces make the premise credible. The book's ordinary spaces do not look as if everyone is pausing until a celebrity arrives. Relationships and resentment build there. Rucka's work with Renee Montoya is central to the series' emotional force and deserves explicit recognition in any recommendation.

Its institutional perspective is complicated by corruption and failures of solidarity. The detectives' vulnerability does not make every exercise of their authority benign. That tension is one reason the series holds up as something more than a clever reversal of a Batman comic.

Read it if you enjoy the question of what a superhero city looks like to people who have to work there tomorrow. Read the whole run if it catches you; stopping at an author's departure misses the completion of a shared project.

## 6. Reckless: a new case, an old bruise

**The best route for readers who like recurring protagonists and discrete books.** Ethan Reckless takes jobs in Los Angeles that bring missing people, old violence and personal compromise into his path. Brubaker, Sean Phillips and Jacob Phillips build the series as original graphic novels.[^6]

Ethan has the appeal of a person who can get things done and the liability of someone whose competence provides excuses to keep living dangerously. The format offers more immediate closure than a long monthly run. A case can end while the protagonist's larger relationship with his life remains unresolved.

Anna is vital to preventing the series from becoming nothing but a tour of one man's damage. Her presence changes its rhythms and its field of attention. *The Ghost in You*, which gives her particular prominence, demonstrates the room the format has for changing perspective.

The Los Angeles setting does more than provide attractive deterioration. Roads, properties and entertainment-industry ambitions help determine who meets whom and which pasts remain accessible. Jacob Phillips's colour makes shifts of period and atmosphere readable without turning every page into the same sepia recollection.

Start with *Reckless*, then continue in publication order. Individual books have their own cases, but familiarity with the relationships adds value. Treat the available cases as a sequence of completed stories, not as proof that the creators have closed the character's entire future.

## Further down the wrong street

*Sleeper* is the strongest next choice if you want Brubaker and Sean Phillips working in a superhuman espionage setting. An undercover agent's isolation makes identity and loyalty practical survival problems. Its two seasons tell a finished main story; *Point Blank*, drawn by Colin Wilson, is the connected prelude rather than an additional season.[^7]

The early-2000s *Catwoman* run is a particularly useful bridge from Batman into Brubaker's street-level interests. Darwyn Cooke's visual reinvention and work by artists including Cameron Stewart give Selina a city to inhabit and people to protect. Check the contents carefully: *Selina's Big Score*, sometimes bundled with the opening Brubaker material, is written and drawn by Cooke. It is a major reason to like the collection, not a Brubaker writing credit.[^8]

*Fatale* and *Kill or Be Killed* extend the Phillips collaboration into horror and unstable accounts of violence. They are substantial adult works with their own series endings. Choose *Fatale* for noir and supernatural predation; choose *Kill or Be Killed* if you want a narrator whose account demands particular scrutiny. Neither is needed before opening *Criminal*.

## A reason is not an acquittal

The danger in noir is that an explanation can become a fashionable way of removing responsibility. Everyone is damaged; everyone had a bad start; the weather was appalling. Brubaker's best work keeps the distinction alive. Understanding why a person makes a choice can intensify its tragedy without making the choice harmless.

There is a recurring limitation. Women sometimes occupy roles defined by the damage or desire of a male protagonist, and familiar noir patterns can narrow what futures a story permits them. The strongest books complicate those patterns; they do not escape every inheritance of the genre.

For a complete first book, take *Pulp*. For the deepest single series, choose *The Fade Out*. For the defining shared world, begin *Criminal* with *Coward*. Brubaker and his collaborators will make the plan sound plausible. Pay attention to what the person explaining it cannot bring themselves to say.

Brian K. Vaughan is very good at the sentence that makes someone pick up a comic. Two lovers from warring peoples try to raise a child. Four paper-delivery girls get caught in a conflict across time. Teenagers discover that their parents really are supervillains, which must make subsequent arguments about household rules unusually awkward.

The harder achievement is what happens after the premise has done its job. Vaughan's strongest books keep finding ways for characters to be less convenient than their roles. A survivor can be immature. A parent can love a child and still make a disastrous decision. A child can encounter her future without finding the reassurance an adult reader expects her to want.

His cliffhangers are famous, but suspense alone does not explain the attachment these books create. The endings of individual issues promise information; the relationships make information hurt. The reader wants to know what happens because something in these untidy lives has become difficult to put down.

The collaborators make that distinction possible. Fiona Staples creates bodies and worlds that can be extravagant without sacrificing intimacy. Pia Guerra and José Marzán Jr. give a vast journey readable human continuity. Cliff Chiang and Matt Wilson make temporal dislocation feel like an environment teenagers have to inhabit. These are shared achievements, not scripts that happened to acquire pictures.

This selection ranks major works through sustained storytelling, distinctive collaboration and accessibility. Major resolutions remain protected. The [Brian K. Vaughan reading guide](../../guides/brian-k-vaughan/) explains what is complete, where to start and how editions overlap. The [five-writer list](../../stories/comic-book-writers-worth-following/) offers other routes into comics beyond a favourite character.

## 1. Saga: the family keeps moving

**The largest and most emotionally ambitious work.** Vaughan and Fiona Staples follow Alana and Marko, lovers from opposing sides of a galactic war, as they try to keep their daughter Hazel alive. The series begins at #1 and needs no background reading. It remains an unfinished larger story at this feature's review date.[^1]

The great structural choice is to make parenthood a continuing condition. The birth is a beginning rather than the completion of a romantic promise. The family needs shelter, income and help. Ideological commitments have to survive exhaustion, fear and proximity to another person who also thinks they are trying their best.

That grounds the enormous setting. The war is not an abstract backdrop that pauses when the protagonists require a private scene. It determines employment, movement, social suspicion and the value other people assign to the child. Domestic life becomes political because the world refuses to let it remain private.

Staples's design is fundamental to how the book avoids generic space opera. Extraordinary bodies can be sexual, vulnerable, comic or exhausted. A character's unusual silhouette does not tell you everything about their emotional role. The same visual imagination that produces spectacle also makes a small gesture readable.

Hazel's narration adds a further tension. A child is telling a story about adults whose decisions she could not fully understand while living through them. Memory can preserve affection without turning it into an acquittal. The narrative voice helps the reader feel time passing even when the immediate danger monopolises the characters' attention.

The weaknesses follow from the method. Abrupt reversals and deaths can sometimes feel engineered to maximise the pain of a page turn. The openness of the project also makes it impossible to assess the whole as a finished structure. Admiring the published work does not entitle a critic to award it an ending it has not delivered.

This is emphatically adult material: explicit sex, nudity, strong language and graphic violence. For readers comfortable with that, it offers an unusually persuasive combination of scale and intimacy. Buy the first volume to find out whether the family catches you. A compendium can be an efficient way to continue; it does not contain the entire eventual series.

## 2. Y: The Last Man: survival does not equal maturity

**The strongest completed long series.** Vaughan and Pia Guerra, with José Marzán Jr. and other contributors, tell a sixty-issue journey following Yorick Brown and his monkey Ampersand after a catastrophe kills almost all mammals with a Y chromosome. The premise reflects the book's early-2000s treatment of sex and gender; it should not be mistaken for a comprehensive account of either.[^2]

The useful complication is Yorick himself. Being crucial to humanity's possible future does not instantly make him wise, generous or even particularly good at understanding the people keeping him alive. The gap between importance and maturity gives the journey much of its energy.

Agent 355 and Dr Allison Mann prevent the story from becoming a solitary male fantasy of indispensability. Their competencies, loyalties and desires make the mission possible while repeatedly exposing the limits of Yorick's perspective. A world can organise itself around his survival without becoming an extension of his needs.

Guerra's clear acting is vital over this distance. The reader can stay with a conversation, recognise an emotional shift and retain a sense of the people involved even as the geography expands. The visual continuity gives the serial surprises something stable to disturb.

The world-building is uneven. Some social scenarios feel schematic, and the gender premise can compress complicated realities into convenient oppositions. Those limitations deserve to travel with the recommendation. The series' strongest claim is the cumulative journey and the relationships built inside it, culminating in a real ending rather than an indefinitely deferred explanation.

Choose it when you want to invest in a long, complete narrative. Avoid browsing character biographies or collected-volume blurbs beyond your current point: this is a series unusually vulnerable to summaries that casually destroy its surprises.

## 3. Paper Girls: the future has opinions about you

**The most approachable complete original series.** Vaughan, Cliff Chiang and colourist Matt Wilson follow four twelve-year-old newspaper-delivery girls beginning in 1988. The main story runs thirty issues and is available as *The Complete Story*, as well as smaller collected formats.[^3]

Time travel gives the girls a version of an ordinary adolescent fear: what if the person you become disappoints you? The speculative device lets that anxiety enter the story physically. The future can be encountered, argued with and misunderstood.

The period setting matters, but the book is most interesting when it resists comfortable nostalgia. The girls do not live inside a curated collection of things an older reader enjoyed owning. They have work, family circumstances, prejudice to negotiate and incomplete information about one another. Friendship develops through shared danger and friction.

Chiang's clear silhouettes and acting keep the cast legible amid temporal confusion. Wilson's colour helps strange environments feel specific rather than simply futuristic. The spectacle can become elaborate, but a reader can still identify who is frightened, who is pushing forward and who is pretending to understand.

Its main weakness is that the mechanics occasionally outrun the emotional thread. New factions and explanations can arrive faster than a reader wants to absorb them. The strongest response is to keep the girls' changing relationships at the centre of the reading. The machinery matters because it keeps forcing them to confront themselves and each other.

It is an easier commitment than *Y* and a safer recommendation for someone who wants a finished narrative than *Saga*. It still includes violence, strong language and period slurs. The young cast and colourful pages are not evidence that it is a book for small children.

## 4. Runaways: inherited power, inherited damage

**The best Marvel entry point.** Vaughan and Adrian Alphona, with artists including Takeshi Miyazawa, introduce teenagers whose parents belong to a secret criminal organisation. The first eighteen-issue series is a strong initial stopping point; Vaughan continues into the 2005 relaunch.[^4]

The premise works because its emotional basis is so direct. Adolescence often involves discovering that adult authority is inconsistent. Here, the inconsistency has costumes, resources and a horrifying shared project. The teenagers have to decide what parts of their inheritance they can use without becoming their parents.

The powers and equipment make that problem visible. A gift can also be an obligation. A family identity can provide access and impose a role. The children need one another, but they do not arrive already equipped with the trust and judgment required to become a functioning group.

Alphona gives the characters distinctive physical presences and expressive awkwardness. They do not all stand like junior versions of established Avengers. That matters because the book's appeal depends on their feeling like young people whose lives have been disrupted, rather than replacements awaiting brand approval.

Some humour and cultural references have aged, and later Marvel history makes the full title much more complicated than this first encounter. Vaughan's own run is a coherent reading project; every later issue under the *Runaways* name is not part of his authorship.

The attraction is the formation of a chosen family under conditions that make choice difficult. The story understands that rejecting your parents' values is easier than recognising all the ways you have learned to think like them.

## 5. The Private Eye: privacy becomes a costume

**The sharpest short science-fiction choice.** Vaughan, Marcos Martín and Muntsa Vicente build a complete mystery in a future shaped by a catastrophic exposure of private information. The ten-part work began digitally through Panel Syndicate and later appeared in print through Image.[^5]

The setting turns a familiar anxiety into a visual system. People conceal their identities in public, and the masks create a street life as expressive as it is guarded. Privacy is no longer an invisible setting buried in an account menu. It has become something a person wears.

Martín's widescreen compositions and Vicente's colour make the social premise pleasurable to explore. Images carry the collision between personal concealment and public spectacle. The form is particularly comfortable on a landscape-oriented screen, which is worth considering before buying the smallest available reading device or print format.

The mystery gives the world a path rather than requiring it to explain itself in a lecture. Its satirical extremes are deliberately broad, and readers looking for a realistic forecast of technology will be asking it to perform the wrong job. The value lies in how vividly it makes information, exposure and identity into forces that organise everyday behaviour.

Choose it if you want an original, finite story without a long serial commitment. It is independent of *Saga*, *Paper Girls* and Vaughan's Marvel work.

## 6. Doctor Strange: The Oath: a doctor still has a patient

**The cleanest short superhero recommendation.** Vaughan and Marcos Martín, with colour by Javier Rodríguez, give Doctor Strange a five-issue investigation that binds magical adventure to medical responsibility. Wong's circumstances make the stakes immediate.[^6]

The title's central word matters. Strange's former profession is more than an origin-story credential. A physician's obligation to a patient can become uncomfortable when a person also has access to extraordinary means of intervention.

Martín makes the magic readable through staging and design. The reader can follow an action and enjoy its strangeness simultaneously. The combination gives the book energy without requiring an encyclopaedia of dimensions, artefacts and previous defeats of Dormammu.

Its brevity limits the supporting world, and it does not attempt the sustained formal experimentation found in some of the other authors' work in this series. It excels at a more modest task: giving a familiar hero a focused problem that reveals why his history and relationships matter.

Read *The Oath* if you want to try Vaughan in one complete superhero story. Its beginning, middle and end fit in a single modest collection, a proposition that superhero publishing occasionally treats as forbidden knowledge.

## Two more major branches

*Ex Machina*, with Tony Harris, puts a former superhero in the office of New York's mayor. Public service, ambition and political performance meet a larger mystery about his powers. The main series is complete, but the discussion of politics is strongly rooted in its post-9/11 American context. Read it for the friction between heroic identity and governing rather than as a timeless model of municipal policy.

*Pride of Baghdad*, with Niko Henrichon, is a standalone graphic novel about escaped lions in a city at war. Its animal cast should not be confused with a gentle fable. Henrichon's expressive work makes the creatures compelling, while the book's allegorical ambitions invite disagreement about how neatly they can carry the human politics around them.[^7]

Neither is required preparation for the books above. They demonstrate the range of forms through which Vaughan explores people, or creatures, caught in circumstances larger than their understanding.

## Where the hook becomes attachment

Vaughan can be too fond of the perfectly placed shock. Dialogue sometimes wears its cleverness conspicuously, and a character can appear to know exactly which observation will make the cleanest panel-ending line. His finest work gives those tendencies enough emotional continuity that the people survive the technique.

That is why the artistic partnerships matter so much. A premise is abstract. A person pausing before answering, looking at a child or recognising a friend is specific. The drawing supplies the life that makes a serial mechanism worth following.

For a complete original journey, take *Paper Girls*. For the longer finished commitment, choose *Y: The Last Man*. For the ongoing emotional epic, begin *Saga* with a clear understanding that you are joining it before the end. The hook will be easy to explain. What keeps you reading will probably be harder to put into a sentence.

Following a superhero through comics is a slightly hazardous way to develop a reading habit. You discover a Batman story you love, buy the next Batman book, and meet a man who seems to have changed his personality, his opinions about fatherhood and his preferred method of punching windows. The costume has remained dependable. Everything else has negotiated separate terms.

Following a writer offers another route. The setting can change while an appetite for certain questions survives: what power does to people, how a family works, why someone takes the job they know will ruin them. A favourite creator can lead you from a familiar hero into an original world you would never have picked up by premise alone.

These five writers make unusually rewarding starting points. They are **five recommendations, not a definitive ranking of the medium's greatest writers**. The selection gives you different kinds of reading pleasure: Hickman's ambitious structures, Morrison's humane strangeness, Moore's formal precision, Brubaker's noir and Vaughan's serial storytelling. Each entry links to a substantial best-work feature and a practical reading guide.

An essential caveat belongs at the front: comics are collaborative. Sean Phillips, Frank Quitely, Fiona Staples, Dave Gibbons, Pepe Larraz and the other artists, colourists and letterers discussed in the features determine how these books work. Following a writer is one route to finding a team you love. It is never a reason to ignore the team.

## 1. Jonathan Hickman: when the plan becomes the problem

**Start with:** *Fantastic Four by Jonathan Hickman: The Complete Collection Vol. 1*.

**Choose him for:** long payoffs, political institutions, rival geniuses and enormous science-fiction ideas with personal consequences.

Hickman's books often contain people who understand much more than they are willing to tell anyone else. Their plans are impressive. Their judgment about what those plans entitle them to do is considerably less reassuring. He can make a meeting between very intelligent people feel more dangerous than the battle it is intended to prevent.

*Fantastic Four/FF* is the most rounded introduction because its scale keeps returning to a family. Reed Richards can attempt to solve everything, but Sue, Ben and the children give the ambition a set of immediate obligations. The future must be somewhere people can live, rather than simply proof that Reed was right.

For a shorter shock, choose *House of X/Powers of X*. Its twelve interleaved issues give the X-Men a new political reality and make the exhilaration of that change inseparable from questions about how it is governed. It is an opening movement, with deliberate unanswered questions, rather than the complete Krakoan era. Our existing [HoX/PoX review](../../stories/house-of-x-powers-of-x-review/) explores why that reinvention works.[^1]

The next step depends on your appetite. *Avengers/New Avengers* leading into *Secret Wars* is the great long construction. *East of West* gives you an independent, complete forty-five-issue epic with Nick Dragotta. The latter requires less continuity knowledge and just as much willingness to meet several alarming governments.

**Be aware:** the machinery can temporarily outrun the people. If you need immediate clarity and closure in every issue, try a collected opening before committing to the whole shelf.

[Read the full Hickman feature](../../stories/best-jonathan-hickman-comics/) · [Use the Hickman reading guide](../../guides/jonathan-hickman/).

## 2. Grant Morrison: the weirdness has someone to protect

**Start with:** the complete *All-Star Superman*.

**Choose them for:** imaginative freedom, outsiders, the emotional possibilities of superheroes and pages that occasionally question their own existence.

Morrison's reputation can make the books sound like homework for people who enjoy being confused in public. The best entry point is much warmer. *All-Star Superman*, with Frank Quitely and Jamie Grant, asks how a man with extraordinary abilities uses his remaining time. It finds room for impossible adventures and the precise act of noticing someone who needs help.[^2]

That concern travels through the stranger material. *Doom Patrol* gives misfits a community. *Animal Man* asks who is responsible for the suffering a story requires. *We3* gives three weaponised animals needs that no military specification can describe. The worlds vary wildly; the question of who deserves care remains legible.

If your interest is specifically in the X-Men, *New X-Men* makes the school feel like part of a developing culture. If you want Batman, Morrison's long run makes room for the character's strange history and a particularly entertaining partnership between Dick Grayson and Damian Wayne. That project has more continuity complications, which is why it gets a separate route in the guide.

**Be aware:** difficulty varies sharply by title. *We3* is short and requires no background, but its graphic violence and animal suffering are severe. *Final Crisis* is an advanced recommendation, not the compulsory entrance exam.

[Read the full Morrison feature](../../stories/best-grant-morrison-comics/) · [Use the Morrison reading guide](../../guides/grant-morrison/).

## 3. Alan Moore: the story is hiding an argument

**Start with:** *Watchmen*, or *Saga of the Swamp Thing Book One* if horror interests you more.

**Choose him for:** formal construction, political questions, unsettling reinventions and the discovery that a comic's visual structure can carry an argument.

The easiest mistake is to credit Moore with making superheroes miserable and stop there. *Watchmen* is extraordinary because Dave Gibbons's images, John Higgins's colour and Moore's structure make revisiting a detail change its meaning. Its characters' certainty about themselves becomes something the reader can inspect. The twelve issues are the complete original work; none of its later franchise extensions is required.[^3]

*Swamp Thing* reveals a different pleasure. With Stephen Bissette, John Totleben, Rick Veitch and others, Moore questions the protagonist's understanding of his own existence, then lets that change enlarge the story's possibilities for love and identity. The new origin matters because of the life that can follow it.

For original worlds, *From Hell* is the demanding historical fiction, *V for Vendetta* the political dystopia and *Top 10* the unexpectedly welcoming superhuman police procedural. *Promethea* asks its artists to make a worldview navigable across the page. Reading several of these gives a much better account of Moore than buying only the books that look most sombre on a shelf.

**Be aware:** graphic violence, abuse and sexual violence recur in parts of the bibliography. Prestige does not make every book suitable for every reader. The guide identifies tone and commitment before purchase.

## 4. Ed Brubaker: a good excuse for a very bad idea

**Start with:** *Pulp* for one complete short book, or *Criminal: Coward* for a larger world.

**Choose him for:** noir, heists, compromised loyalties and characters who can explain the trap just before entering it.

Brubaker and Sean Phillips make understanding a person feel different from forgiving them. Someone can have a painful past, a persuasive reason and an impressively organised plan while still being responsible for what happens next. The sadness comes from watching how often insight fails to free them.

*Pulp*, with Jacob Phillips's colour, is the concentrated introduction: an ageing writer of Western stories meets danger in 1930s New York. *The Fade Out*, with Elizabeth Breitweiser, gives that moral pressure a larger Hollywood institution to work inside. Its murder investigation encounters people whose livelihood depends on controlling which version of events becomes public.[^4]

*Criminal* expands the perspective across an underworld. Families, neighbourhoods and remembered injuries connect stories whose protagonists do not possess the same information. Publication order makes those connections more rewarding than a strict in-world chronology.

There are excellent familiar-character routes too. *Captain America: Winter Soldier* turns history into an immediate threat. *Gotham Central*, co-created with Greg Rucka and Michael Lark, shows what Batman's city looks like to detectives who have to work there. The shared credit matters; the full series is not a Brubaker solo run.

**Be aware:** these books can be bleak and sexually explicit, and their noir inheritance sometimes limits the roles available to women. Try one complete story before deciding you want a long stay in this neighbourhood.

## 5. Brian K. Vaughan: the premise gets personal

**Start with:** *Paper Girls Vol. 1*, or *Doctor Strange: The Oath* for a complete five-issue sample.

**Choose him for:** strong premises, memorable companions, serial momentum and the feeling that one more chapter is a reasonable life decision.

Vaughan's best hooks are easy to repeat. What makes the books last is how inconvenient their people become. Being crucial to the future does not make Yorick Brown mature in *Y: The Last Man*. Being in love does not make raising a child simple in *Saga*. Meeting the future does not guarantee reassurance in *Paper Girls*.

*Paper Girls*, with Cliff Chiang and Matt Wilson, is the safest substantial recommendation for someone who wants a finished original story: thirty issues, four newspaper-delivery girls and a time-travel conflict that keeps returning to who they might become. *Y*, with Pia Guerra and José Marzán Jr., offers a longer completed journey, though its early-2000s framing of sex and gender has limitations worth acknowledging.[^5]

*Saga*, co-created with Fiona Staples, is the larger family epic. Its extraordinary worlds stay connected to shelter, work, exhaustion and the responsibility of loving people you cannot always keep safe. It remains an unfinished overarching story, so a large compendium should not be mistaken for the complete series.

For another short original option, *The Private Eye* combines Marcos Martín and Muntsa Vicente's visual imagination with a mystery about information and privacy. *Runaways* is the accessible Marvel branch: teenage rebellion supplied with unusually strong evidence against the parents.

**Be aware:** “page-turner” does not mean light. *Saga* is explicit adult material; other projects vary. The guides separate completion status and tone so that the hook does not make the purchase decision for you.

[Read the full Vaughan feature](../../stories/best-brian-k-vaughan-comics/) · [Use the Vaughan reading guide](../../guides/brian-k-vaughan/).

## Pick the pleasure, then the commitment

If you want a **complete single-book experience**, choose *All-Star Superman*, *Watchmen*, *Pulp* or *The Oath*. If you want a **finished longer journey**, take *East of West*, *Paper Girls*, *Y* or the full *Fade Out*. For a **familiar superhero reimagined**, try Hickman's *Fantastic Four*, Morrison's *New X-Men* or Brubaker's *Winter Soldier*.

You do not need to collect all five writers. Try one opening, notice the names of the people whose work you enjoy, and follow them somewhere less familiar. A costume can bring you into comics. A creative team can show you how much of the medium lies beyond it.
