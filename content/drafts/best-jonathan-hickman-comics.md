Jonathan Hickman has a reputation for making comics that require diagrams. This is unfair. Sometimes he supplies the diagrams himself.

There are charts, organisational hierarchies, ominous symbols, impossible histories and people who appear to have been hired specifically to say that everything is proceeding according to plan. Often, everything is proceeding according to three incompatible plans. Somewhere in the building, Reed Richards has neglected dinner.

The reputation catches the surface of the work and misses its most compelling feature. Hickman's best comics put faith in human intelligence, then ask what intelligent people will excuse once they believe everyone else's survival depends on them. Their scale is cosmic. Their recurring problem is painfully recognisable: someone has decided that being the cleverest person in the room also makes them the person entitled to lock the door.

That tension connects a remarkably varied body of work. *Fantastic Four* makes a family the necessary check on genius. *New Avengers* examines the moral collapse of an elite convinced it has no alternative. *House of X/Powers of X* gives an endangered people the machinery of a nation and invites us to admire it, fear for it and scrutinise it simultaneously. *East of West* imagines a political order that has turned apocalypse into an appointment.

These are the essential books, chosen for the force of their ideas, their visual storytelling and how well their ambitions become drama. The order below is an editorial ranking, with the interconnected Avengers saga treated as one undertaking. Major endings and the central HoX/PoX revelations stay unspoiled. For purchases and sequence, use the [Jonathan Hickman reading guide](../../guides/jonathan-hickman/); for other writers, visit [our five-author starting list](../../stories/comic-book-writers-worth-following/).

## 1. Fantastic Four and FF: the family comes first

**The strongest all-round Hickman work.** With Dale Eaglesham, Steve Epting, Nick Dragotta and a substantial artistic ensemble, Hickman turns Marvel's first family into an argument about what progress is for. The collected route covers *Dark Reign: Fantastic Four*, his main-series run and *FF*, the Future Foundation companion book.[^1]

Reed Richards wants to solve problems on the largest possible scale. This is admirable until solving everything becomes a reason to spend less time with the people who make any of it matter. The Council of Reeds gives that temptation a particularly sharp form: intelligence can multiply across realities while emotional judgment stays stubbornly scarce.

Hickman understands that Reed's defining flaw does not have to be stupidity disguised as genius. He can be extraordinarily capable and still need Sue to tell him that a useful discovery is not an adequate substitute for a husband. Family becomes a way of testing ambition against actual obligations. A universe full of theoretical beneficiaries does not release you from the child standing in front of you.

The Future Foundation broadens that argument. A school of unusual children makes the future something adults must prepare other people to inhabit, rather than a private monument to their own brilliance. Valeria's confidence is funny because she belongs to this household; it is disquieting because the household already contains a cautionary example of intelligence outrunning judgment. Franklin gives the story another scale of vulnerability and possibility. Ben supplies the emotional weight that a lesser version of this run would try to replace with another galactic war.

The artists keep its two scales in contact. Eaglesham's physical solidity makes Reed's cosmic work feel like something a body must live through. Epting's faces and staging give grief room to register. Dragotta brings a different energy to the children and the extraordinary spaces around them. The Future Foundation's visual identity makes intellectual cooperation look exciting without requiring every member to acquire matching shoulder armour.

There is a substantial price of admission. Locations, factions and future promises accumulate. Reading only the issues branded *Fantastic Four* eventually means missing half the conversation. But the apparent sprawl has an emotional centre clear enough to hold it: can a man who wants to save everyone remember how to belong to someone?

This is the best first sustained encounter with Hickman because it demonstrates both his architecture and his warmth. It also improves the experience of the later Marvel saga. Understanding Reed as a father, husband and friend gives his relationship with Doctor Doom a force that no multiverse glossary can supply.

## 2. Avengers, New Avengers and Secret Wars: intelligence meets its limit

**The great large-scale undertaking.** *Avengers* expands the public team; *New Avengers* follows a secret group confronting incursions, collisions between universes that make survival an intolerable problem. *Infinity* intersects the two series. *Time Runs Out* carries them toward *Secret Wars* in 2015. Marvel's own collections gather these linked strands, and the eventual event is nine issues by Hickman and Esad Ribić.[^2]

The public Avengers project is built on recruitment and the belief that a sufficiently capable team can meet a sufficiently large threat. The private Illuminati story asks what happens when the threat makes that belief inadequate. Between them sits the difference between a heroic institution's stated purpose and the decisions made in its closed meetings.

Hickman gives the Illuminati a problem that cannot be dismissed by announcing that superheroes should simply be nicer. He then refuses to let the difficulty of their situation cleanse their choices. The most frightening developments arrive when deliberation becomes routine. Something that would once have seemed unthinkable turns into the next item on the agenda.

Black Panther and Namor are crucial because their responsibilities cannot be reduced to an abstract equation. They bring nations, histories and incompatible obligations into the room. Captain America's presence tests the claim that moral refusal is merely an indulgence for people who have not understood the stakes. The series makes room for both the force and the limits of that objection.

Its most effective repetition is structural. A plan is made. Its inadequacy emerges. Another plan requires an additional compromise. The heroes accumulate knowledge faster than they recover moral ground. Apocalypse acquires the atmosphere of a committee that keeps extending its emergency powers.

The art is essential to that escalation. Jerome Opeña helps establish the enormous scope of the early *Avengers* material; Steve Epting makes the rooms of *New Avengers* feel heavy with unspoken decisions. Across the run, artists including Mike Deodato and Stefano Caselli give its confrontations different textures. This is a collaborative visual undertaking, with the inevitable unevenness of a long monthly schedule.

*Secret Wars* finally gives Ribić space to make the consequences monumental. His figures have the weight of damaged sculpture, while Ive Svorcina's colour helps the world feel ancient, unstable and lived in. The setting could have become an excuse to display alternate costumes. Its dramatic centre remains authority: who claims the right to preserve a world, what that preservation costs, and whether creation can be disentangled from possession.

Some of the journey is forbidding. Secondary characters can function more as components than people. Several scenes gain meaning only after later issues. Readers who want every chapter to provide immediate closure may find the run exhausting. Its strongest defence is the destination: the scale contracts at the moment that matters, until the future of everything can be understood through a few deeply personal relationships.

Read the main *Secret Wars* series if you want a shorter encounter. Read the Avengers road to it if you want its ethical pressure. Add *Fantastic Four/FF* first if you want the fullest emotional argument. You do not need every tie-in with a Battleworld logo; the multiverse has already suffered enough administration.

## 3. House of X/Powers of X: a revolution with terms and conditions

**The most electrifying reinvention.** Two six-issue series, with Pepe Larraz and R.B. Silva and colour led by Marte Gracia, establish a new political reality for mutants. They form one twelve-part work and depend on their intended interleaving; Marvel explicitly describes both as essential.[^3]

The remarkable decision is to give the X-Men's familiar argument a change of position. Mutants have spent decades asking whether they will be permitted to exist safely. Here, their leaders begin making offers, setting conditions and constructing institutions. Readers accustomed to treating the X-Men as a besieged school encounter the much stranger prospect of a people learning how to wield power.

That movement is exhilarating because the desperation behind it is legible. It is unsettling because legitimate fear does not make a government's every decision legitimate. Hickman lets the language of liberation coexist with secrecy, hierarchy and the confidence of leaders who have made extraordinary decisions on everyone else's behalf.

The data pages are part of that effect. They make a new society feel as though it possesses rules, supply chains, doctrines and a functioning information system. At the same time, an official document always raises a question: who gets to write the official account? The form makes certainty attractive before the narrative asks us to inspect its authors.

Larraz gives the new homeland a physical generosity that makes the promise feel worth defending. Silva handles temporal and technological complexity without reducing it to a procession of diagrams. Gracia's colour gives the two books a shared sensual vocabulary. The future is persuasive because it looks like somewhere people might actually want to live.

For a reader who loved HoX/PoX, the next Hickman book depends on which pleasure mattered most. Choose *Fantastic Four* for the emotional consequences of an ambitious plan. Choose *New Avengers* for the hidden cost of elite decision-making. Choose *East of West* for rival powers caught in an enormous historical machine.

The qualification matters: HoX/PoX is a brilliant foundation, not the complete history of the Krakoan era. Hickman's later *X-Men* and *Inferno* develop his contribution, but many writers shape the wider line and its eventual conclusion. Selling the whole era as a single author novel misrepresents what the books are. Our [dedicated HoX/PoX review](../../stories/house-of-x-powers-of-x-review/) examines why the opening works so well without pretending it seals every question it raises.

## 4. East of West: the apocalypse is a political project

**The strongest independent epic.** Hickman and Nick Dragotta, with colour by Frank Martin, build an alternate America of competing nations, prophecy, technology and the Horsemen of the Apocalypse. The main series is complete at forty-five issues, collected in ten standard volumes and a full-story compendium.[^4]

The early density can be intimidating. There are people to identify, territorial claims to understand and factions that appear to have spent the previous century rehearsing how best to betray one another. An initial reading can feel like arriving late to the least reassuring diplomatic summit in history.

Dragotta is the reason that density becomes a world rather than a dossier. His silhouettes make powerful figures instantly legible. A pause before violence can feel as charged as the violence itself. Martin's colour lets barren spaces, technological grandeur and sudden brutality coexist without flattening the book into a uniform apocalypse brown.

At its centre is Death, whose personal attachments interfere with the role the world expects him to perform. That gives the political scale an emotional lever. History says events must proceed in one direction; a person has reasons to want otherwise. The question is whether those reasons can withstand institutions built to make the catastrophe happen.

Its leaders also expose a recurring Hickman concern: systems can become self-justifying. A prophecy provides a reason to act, and the resulting actions provide evidence that the prophecy was right. The story's political orders do not merely anticipate disaster. They invest in it.

The book is less immediately welcoming than *Fantastic Four* and more consistently cruel. Some of its enormous cast remains more memorable as an image than as a fully developed person. But its original setting allows consequences and relationships to acquire a finality that shared superhero universes can rarely sustain. It offers a major Hickman construction with an actual ending and no request that you first familiarise yourself with forty years of somebody else's mutants.

## 5. Secret Warriors: the organisational chart has teeth

**The essential espionage book.** Hickman, working from a project launched with Brian Michael Bendis, follows Nick Fury and a younger covert team through a war between secret organisations. Stefano Caselli and Alessandro Vitti are major artistic voices. The original run ends at #28.[^5]

The series is a useful bridge between Hickman's reputation for intricate plotting and the experience of reading him. Its hierarchies, hidden connections and loyalties are recognisable as spy-fiction machinery. You may not know what every symbol means, but you understand that the man withholding information probably has a contingency plan for your funeral.

Fury's competence is part of the attraction and part of the danger. His capacity to see several moves ahead does not make being one of his pieces a healthy profession. The younger characters give that imbalance a cost. From the commander's perspective, a sacrifice may be necessary. From the recruit's perspective, necessity still arrives with a name and a body.

Caselli brings clarity and urgency to the team material; Vitti's harsher textures suit the story's attrition. The run makes the logistics of espionage feel consequential without losing the appeal of people doing dangerous things extremely well.

Its weaknesses are those of a compressed conspiracy story. Revelations can run ahead of emotional processing, and some supporting players never become as substantial as the machinery around them. Even so, it is a more manageable demonstration of the long game than the entire Avengers project, and a good choice for readers who prefer betrayal at human scale to metaphysics at universal scale.

## 6. Decorum: the visual argument

**The best reminder that Hickman's work is not merely a matter of plot.** With Mike Huddleston, *Decorum* offers an eight-issue science-fiction story involving assassins, a courier and extraordinary institutions. Its defining attraction is the breadth of Huddleston's image-making.[^6]

Painted grandeur, graphic abstraction, spare drawing and extravagant design can share the same book. The shifts in register help distinguish kinds of experience, power and space. A world can look overwhelming because the person entering it has been overwhelmed; another can feel precise and remote because its inhabitants have learned to treat living things as manageable material.

That range gives the central contrast between ordinary vulnerability and extraordinary professional capability a visual life. Expertise looks seductive. So does belonging to an organisation with beautiful clothes and terrifying standards. The book is interested in what passes from mentor to pupil besides technique.

It is not the most emotionally rounded selection here. The setting sometimes appears ready to support a much larger story than the one we receive. But as a finite encounter with Hickman's design sensibility and a collaborator capable of answering it with remarkable invention, it earns its place.

## The other books worth knowing about

*The Nightly News* matters because Hickman draws as well as writes it. Its aggressive page design and media satire make the later fascination with information systems easier to recognise. It is a sharper, angrier and more abrasive proposition than his family superhero work, rather than a necessary first step.[^7]

*S.H.I.E.L.D.*, with Dustin Weaver, transforms historical geniuses into participants in Marvel's hidden cosmic history. Choose it for elaborate visual imagination and arguments about whether history has a predetermined end. Its two principal collections, *Architects of Forever* and *The Human Machine*, belong together; the shared acronym does not make it an ordinary Nick Fury mission.[^8]

*The Black Monday Murders*, with Tomm Coker, deserves attention for making finance and occult horror feel like natural colleagues. Its material already published is compelling, but the publisher's catalogue lists the original run through #8 rather than a completed final movement. Coker discussed returning to conclude it in August 2026, but that intention is not a published ending. Readers seeking a finished story should choose *East of West* first and check for a confirmed final collection before buying on a promise of closure.[^9]

*Ultimate Spider-Man*, launched in 2024 with Marco Checchetto and contributions from David Messina, is especially appealing if you want Hickman's interest in possible futures placed inside adult domestic life. This is a different Ultimate continuity from the original Brian Michael Bendis series. Marvel bills #24 as the end of this Hickman/Checchetto run; a cover label saying “Ultimate” does not mean an indefinitely continuing Hickman project.[^10]

## Why the diagrams keep leading back to people

The fairest criticism of Hickman is that the machinery sometimes takes over. A revelation can be easier to admire than to feel. A character can enter a room as an interesting person and leave as a necessary part of a future chart. The density also rewards patience unevenly: an enormous payoff does not retroactively make every intervening conversation graceful.

His best work succeeds by making those liabilities part of its subject. Plans promise to relieve people of uncertainty. They can also turn other people into things that fit. Again and again, the decisive question is what happens when a relationship resists the scheme: a family, an old friendship, a national obligation, a child who cannot be reduced to an expected role.

Start with *Fantastic Four/FF* for the fullest expression of that idea. Start with *House of X/Powers of X* for its most concentrated shock. Take *East of West* if you want a complete original universe, or the Avengers saga if you want to watch a system exhaust every justification it has.

The charts explain how the world is supposed to work. The people explain why it might deserve another chance.
