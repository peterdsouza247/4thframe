Rebecca Welton can change the direction of AFC Richmond with a decision made in her office. Early in *Ted Lasso*, she hires an American football coach who knows little about the English game because she wants the club she inherited in a divorce to fail. The person with the power to make that choice does not have to stand on the pitch or sit among the supporters who will live with the result. The seasons that follow ask whether she can come to see the club differently.

That question has acquired a sharper edge outside television. On [29 September 2026, the Premier League published an independent commission's findings](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4727779/premier-league-statement-manchester-city-fc/) against Manchester City. The commission upheld all charges concerning serious financial-rule breaches from 2009/10 through 2017/18 and three of four allegations about failure to cooperate with the investigation. Headlines have called this a finding on 114 of 115 charges. The league says sham arrangements inflated revenue and reduced costs by more than £900 million. [City denies wrongdoing and says it will appeal](https://www.mancity.com/news/club/manchester-city-club-statement-premier-league-63926128). No sanction has been imposed at the time of writing.

Richmond is fictional. City is a real club with supporters, workers and a long history. Treating one as a model of virtue and the other as a villain would tell us little about either. Put them side by side, though, and they raise a useful question: who gets to decide what a football club is for?

## The owner can change everything

Richmond's opening premise depends on Rebecca having enormous freedom over the club. Her attempt to hurt Rupert, her former husband and its previous owner, could have cost players their places and supporters their top-flight team. Ted's approach slowly changes the atmosphere around him, but his authority comes from the same owner whose earlier decision put everyone at risk. The show's warmth does not make that power disappear.

Manchester City's ownership works on a much larger financial scale. [City Football Group says](https://www.cityfootballgroup.com/company) it is majority owned through an Abu Dhabi company wholly owned by Sheikh Mansour, with Silver Lake holding a substantial minority stake. Investment has helped City build a formidable football operation and a global reach. That is part of the story of modern English football. So is the question of what happens when a club's financial strength and influence outgrow the public's ability to understand how they are governed.

Neither case suggests that money alone decides whether someone cares about a club. Rebecca's choices become more thoughtful as she grows attached to Richmond. City supporters' attachment is not a product of an ownership structure; it existed before this era and will outlast any ruling. The owner and the club are connected, but the people who love a team are not interchangeable with the people who control it.

## A culture built by choices

At Richmond, Ted listens to players, takes the staff seriously and tries to make a dressing room in which people can improve together. That is one reason the show's version of football feels inviting. Yet kindness is not a governing rule. Rebecca could undo much of that culture, as her initial plan demonstrates. The series gives us a generous owner by the time she understands what Richmond means to others, but it also shows how much hinges on her change of heart.

City's sporting culture deserves to be described with similar care. A ruling about financial reporting cannot tell us whether a supporter loves the club sincerely, whether a youth coach has helped a child or whether a player has worked hard for a title. Those things happened in the lives of real people. At the same time, the commission's findings concern the conditions under which the club competed with other teams. Admiring football on the pitch does not settle a question about the rules off it.

This is where the comparison becomes useful. Richmond asks us to imagine the good a person with power might choose to do. City's case asks what a league must be able to establish regardless of an owner's intentions or a club's success.

## Administration is part of the competition

Supporters do not buy tickets to watch an audit. They still depend on accurate accounts and rules that apply across the league. A club's reported income and costs affect what it can spend, and spending affects the squads its rivals must face. According to the [Premier League's statement](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4727779/premier-league-statement-manchester-city-fc/), the independent commission found that City used sham commercial agreements and misstated accounts to appear compliant with spending limits. It also found failures to cooperate with the league's investigation. The club [disputes the findings](https://www.mancity.com/news/club/manchester-city-club-statement-premier-league-63926128) and says the opinion contains errors of law and fact.

The next steps matter. The commission has yet to determine a sanction, and City has a right to appeal. An article written today should not present a possible penalty as an outcome. What the published ruling already permits us to ask is whether a league can maintain trust when a case about nine seasons takes years to reach even this stage. Rival clubs and their supporters cannot replay the matches while the process works its way through hearings.

*Ted Lasso* has room to make its administration humane and visible through Higgins, Rebecca and the people who use the club every day. Real football governance is less photogenic. Its work is no less central to whether supporters believe that the contest is fair.

## Who gets a say?

Richmond's neighbourhood gives the club its character. The regulars in the pub argue, hope and suffer through results they have no direct power to produce. When Rebecca starts listening to what the team means to them, it feels like a moral advance. It also exposes the weakness in the arrangement: being heard still depends on the owner choosing to listen.

City belongs in the lives of its supporters too, whatever a corporate ownership chart says. They do not control the appeal or the eventual sanction, yet they must live with each headline and its consequences. Other clubs' fans deserve a competition whose financial rules mean something. These interests can conflict, and none can be wished away by a pleasing story about football bringing everyone together.

Richmond gives us a version of ownership capable of learning. City's case shows why English football cannot rely on that quality alone. A club can be a business, a local institution and a source of private memory at once. The people who own it can make decisions for all three. The question the fiction and the real ruling leave us with is how the game makes those owners answer to everyone else.
