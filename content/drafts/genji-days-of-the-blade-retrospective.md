Imagine a samurai rushing a formation of armoured soldiers, blades flashing in two hands. There is a satisfying rhythm to the strikes: a dash to close the distance, a sweeping cut through the group, a sudden change of weapon, then another attack before anyone can recover. You have time to enjoy the animation for perhaps half a second. Then the camera swivels behind a pillar and a soldier you cannot see hits you.

That is *Genji: Days of the Blade* in miniature. It could make fighting feel spectacular, then find a way to make you fight the camera as well.

Game Republic's PlayStation 3 launch title arrived in November 2006 with enviable art direction, four distinct warriors, instantly swappable weapons and a dramatic slow-motion technique called Kamui. Reviews were unkind. The game currently sits at [55 on Metacritic](https://www.metacritic.com/game/genji-days-of-the-blade/), and one presentation from Sony's E3 2006 conference has outlived just about everything else people remember about it.

But a reputation is not the same thing as an account of how a game feels in your hands. The criticism of *Days of the Blade* was often justified. So was the pleasure some players found in cutting through its enemies. Its swordplay deserves a second look because it offers a surprisingly clear lesson in how animation, character variety and the rhythm of combat can survive inside a flawed game.

## A launch game asked to look like the future

The story begins with *Genji: Dawn of the Samurai*, released for PlayStation 2 in 2005. It was the first game from Game Republic, the studio founded by former Capcom producer Yoshiki Okamoto. His credits included work associated with *Street Fighter II* and *Resident Evil*, which made the new company's sword-fighting adventure an object of curiosity.

The original *Genji* resembled *Onimusha*: a legendary Japanese setting, uncanny enemies, lavish environments and attacks that prized timing as well as spectacle. Its principal heroes were Minamoto no Yoshitsune, the nimble swordsman, and Musashibō Benkei, his imposing warrior-monk companion. The first game was attractive and immediately playable, although [GameSpot's 2005 review](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/genji-dawn-of-the-samurai-review/1900-6133869/) thought it ended far too quickly.

The sequel was supposed to address that problem. It would be longer, broader and more ambitious. According to [Okamoto's January 2007 interviews](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/qanda-genji-2s-yoshiki-okamoto/1100-6164197/), development began on PlayStation 2 before Sony asked the team to make a PlayStation 3 launch game instead. In [another interview](https://spong.com/feature/10109502/Interview-SPOnG-Interviews-Yoshiki-Okamoto/2), he said the project had spent roughly six months in PS2 development and was staffed by a core team of about 50.

It is easy to see why Sony wanted *Genji* on its new console. A painterly Japanese fantasy with elaborate costumes, shimmering lighting and crowds of enemy soldiers looked good in still photographs. The PlayStation 3 needed games that could demonstrate high-definition spectacle on a television showroom wall. Game Republic had a title that could do precisely that.

The price of becoming a launch title was a deadline that could not move. Okamoto said outright that features had to be cut to ship on time. That admission does not excuse every design problem, but it makes the finished product less mysterious. A sequel initially planned for familiar hardware became a showcase for unfamiliar hardware while the calendar stayed fixed.

The game was trying to be two things: an evolution of a carefully staged PS2 action adventure and an instant demonstration of everything the PS3 could render. You can feel those competing ambitions throughout the campaign.

## The first pleasure is movement

Start with Yoshitsune. His dual swords have the clearest claim on the player's affection. He can close a gap quickly, chain light and sweeping attacks, leap into the air and continue pressing against a crowd before it settles. The moves are easy to grasp, but their rhythms vary enough that choosing which one to use can feel expressive.

Chris Kohler's [2006 impressions for Wired](https://www.wired.com/2006/11/impressions-gen/) noted that the Square, Circle and Triangle buttons produced different attacks, including dashes and airborne movements, which could be combined in different orders. These were not the hundreds of highly technical inputs of a *Virtua Fighter* character. That was part of the appeal. A player who was willing to experiment could produce something convincing without spending an evening memorising a command list.

The difference between a satisfying action game and a merely busy one is often a matter of timing and animation. An attack should have a recognisable beginning, a convincing impact and a recovery that leaves room for the next decision. *Genji* gives Yoshitsune the visual grammar of a swordfighter who knows precisely where his blades are going. When several moves flow into one another, the fight briefly resembles choreography rather than frantic button pressing.

The game also makes a virtue of attacking in more than one direction. Enemies surround the player, so wide attacks and evasions are useful even when the ordinary soldiers are not especially bright. It is a looser, more accessible kind of combat than *Ninja Gaiden*, and less obsessed with grading the player than *Devil May Cry*. Its immediate reward is seeing a graceful combination take shape.

That does not mean its combat has the discipline of either series. Repeated attacks can be too effective; some enemies offer little resistance; the game rarely forces players to master its whole vocabulary. A move can be pleasurable to execute even if the enemy design does not demand that you use it.

## Four warriors, four different ideas of force

A defining addition was the ability to switch among four playable characters using the D-pad, once they had joined the party. Only one fights on-screen at a time. The transition is quick enough, however, to change the flow of a skirmish, and it becomes part of the spectacle rather than a trip to an equipment menu.

Yoshitsune is about mobility and rapid cuts. **Benkei** is his deliberate opposite: a towering warrior whose massive club makes crowd control look less like fencing than demolition. His attacks have longer commitments and far greater physical presence. Critics sometimes described him as painfully sluggish. That criticism is fair; the pleasure of using Benkei depends on enjoying the anticipation of a heavy blow as much as its arrival.

**Shizuka** brings an entirely different silhouette. Her bladed hoop and tethered attacks give her longer reach and allow her to pull enemies towards her. In a [September 2006 hands-on report](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/tgs-06-genji-days-of-the-blade-hands-on/1100-6158327/), GameSpot described using the weapon as both an attack and a grapple, with exploration routes inaccessible to other characters. She is the clearest demonstration that changing fighters was supposed to alter the player's relationship to space, not merely change the colour of an energy trail.

**Lord Buson**, the fourth warrior, fights with a spear-like double-ended weapon. His spinning attacks create a different form of reach and defence. There is a story reason for his strange presence, connected to an earlier antagonist, but it is more enjoyable to discover that in the game than have a retrospective spoil the reveal.

The most interesting possibilities arise between characters. Contemporary [hands-on coverage](https://www.gamesradar.com/genji-days-of-the-blade-not-massively-damaged/) describes launching an enemy with Benkei and swapping to Yoshitsune for an aerial follow-up. That is the kind of interaction that makes a fighting system memorable: a player can stage a tiny action scene rather than simply wait for the strongest character's animation to finish.

The game often makes that choice less significant than it should be. Yoshitsune is so flexible that sticking to him can feel sensible in almost every ordinary battle. Having a larger roster is not the same thing as requiring one. Yet even an underused mechanic can make a game more enjoyable for somebody who wants to improvise. *Genji* gives those players four quite different tools.

## Real-time weapon switching was a promise and a problem

The E3 presentation placed special emphasis on being able to change weapons instantly, and it was not an empty claim. The left shoulder controls let characters move between equipped weapons without a pause for a menu. Each weapon has its own attacks, and the player can upgrade attack strength as the campaign progresses.

On paper, the potential is enormous: switch weapons to change range, change the cadence of a combo and choose a more appropriate strike for the enemy in front of you. By contrast with the first *Genji*, the new hardware and expanded roster seemed designed to make combat more fluid.

In practice, the advantages of a second weapon are not always clear. [GameSpot's final review](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/genji-days-of-the-blade-review/1900-6161332/) found that many weapons were similar in practical damage and that players could easily favour the ones they knew or had already upgraded. When switching looks impressive but rarely solves a specific combat problem, the system risks becoming a flourish.

Still, it is a good flourish. It puts the player in charge of tempo. The great strength of *Days of the Blade* is that it understands an action game can be entertaining through the act of executing moves, even when the level design gives you modest reasons to vary them.

That enjoyment is not imaginary because a review score is low. It simply measures a different part of the experience.

## Kamui: turning a battle into a performance

The original *Dawn of the Samurai* built much of its identity around Kamui, sometimes described as the mind's eye. Activate it at the right time and the action slows. Enemies begin their attacks, and the player must time a defensive response before a powerful counterstrike. It was an unusually elegant way to give a sword fight the rhythm of anticipation and release.

*Days of the Blade* keeps the name but changes the idea. Here, landing blows fills a meter; triggering Kamui launches a stylised sequence in which the player follows attack-button prompts to slash through a succession of nearby enemies. Time seems to belong to the fighter. The screen fills with dramatic motion, the pace becomes measured, and successful inputs extend the carnage.

There is a difference between responding to an opponent's attack and performing the correct note in a series of prompts. The former is about reading the enemy; the latter resembles playing the choreography. Some critics preferred the original's defensive precision, and the sequel's system can become repetitive. Yet the replacement has an appeal of its own.

A successful Kamui turns the chaos of a crowded battle into a controlled display of competence. The normal combat may ask you to spin around searching for threats, but Kamui knows exactly where everyone is. Each button press carries the promise of another decisive strike. In an action game obsessed with elegant silhouettes, this is the mechanic that most clearly allows the player to become the director of the scene.

The timing windows can add tension even when the combat outside the special attack is forgiving. Miss an input and the sequence ends early. Maintain the rhythm and a battlefield that was dangerous a moment ago is suddenly a showcase of acrobatics.

The problem is that Kamui can become a solution to encounters that have not earned such a powerful solution. Once you learn how effective it is, saving it for an exceptional moment may seem less sensible than using it to dispose of another large group. Spectacle loses a little of its magic when it becomes housekeeping.

A sharper sequel could have kept the offensive Kamui while giving more of its enemies distinct timings and reactions. The underlying idea is strong enough to deserve an entire combat system built around it.

## The exquisite, impractical camera

Now for the objection everyone remembers who played enough of the game to become irritated: the camera. Instead of using the right analogue stick to control your view, *Days of the Blade* devotes it to dodging and evasive rolls. The camera is fixed or directed by the game as you move through environments.

This is not inherently a poor choice. Earlier action adventures used composed angles to create memorable views and guide attention. In *Genji*, the arrangement produces striking images: fires illuminate architecture; figures move against painterly backdrops; the battlefield resembles an elaborate period drama. It is also a nightmare when enemies attack from outside the frame, a corner obscures the action or a platforming challenge requires seeing what is above you.

Most damning is that the developers understood the trade-off. In a remarkable [January 2007 interview with Kikizo](https://games.kikizo.com/news/200701/064_p1.asp), Okamoto explained that the E3 demonstration had attracted complaints about the camera being too far from the action. The team moved it closer to show off the PS3's visual detail. That made it harder to see the battlefield. Okamoto acknowledged that the decision sacrificed some gameplay visibility in pursuit of the next-generation look.

Here is a rare case where a game's greatest virtue helped cause its most notorious flaw. *Genji* needed to look beautiful. Its best combat encounters needed room to breathe. The camera tried to satisfy the first demand even when it frustrated the second.

You can elect to make dodges through the Sixaxis controller's motion sensing, but contemporary reviews noted that the option was off by default and could produce unintended movement. The original Sixaxis controller also lacked rumble, removing one obvious way to reinforce the sensation of a heavy strike. It is hard to think of a more 2006 design collision: an elegant sword-fighting fantasy having to accommodate a console eager to demonstrate its new technology.

## Battles the history books missed

*Days of the Blade* draws on the Genpei War, the late-twelfth-century conflict between the Minamoto (Genji) and Taira (Heishi) clans. Yoshitsune and Benkei are drawn from Japanese history and legend. The game uses battles such as Ichinotani and Dannoura as the framework for a supernatural tale of corruption, strange crystals and armies transformed by otherworldly power.

There is an inviting contradiction here. The scenery and names take history seriously. The enemies often do not. A warrior will fight soldiers whose bodies have sprouted pink crystals and then encounter a monster that would be happier in an illustrated bestiary.

The public remembers the contradiction through Sony's E3 2006 demonstration. An executive described famous historical battles; soon afterwards the game showed an enormous enemy crab. The presenter instructed the player to exploit its weak point for extra damage. The resulting joke spread far beyond people who intended to buy *Genji*. It made the game a shorthand for inflated launch-stage presentations.

The strange part is that the battle shown was mechanically interesting. In [GameSpot's May 2006 description](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/e3-06-genji-2-first-impressions/1100-6149509/), Benkei knocked the creature over with a heavy attack, exposing an area Yoshitsune could exploit. A joke about a crab obscured a demonstration of two fighters contributing different strengths to a boss encounter.

It is entirely reasonable to laugh at the presentation. The promise of historical authenticity followed by a colossal monster practically writes the joke itself. It is less reasonable to let the joke replace the whole game. History-inspired fantasy has room for monsters; *Onimusha* and many other successful action games had already made that clear.

The crab became more famous than the swordplay. That, too, is part of the story of the early PS3.

## More enemies is not necessarily more combat

The first *Genji* was criticised for being short, so the sequel became longer. More warriors appeared on the screen, the battles grew more elaborate and the campaign stretched through a wider range of locations. The change answered an obvious consumer complaint. It also introduced a problem: the basic encounters were not always varied enough to justify their duration.

Fighting a group of enemies with Yoshitsune can be exciting. Clearing a similar group moments later is less so. Eventually the player begins to recognise the same formations and repeat the same effective attacks. The combat still looks splendid, but it stops asking fresh questions.

Bosses provide a welcome contrast. Their exaggerated bodies, exposed weak points and demands for specific movement give the four-character system more purpose. Some encourage Benkei's force; others make Yoshitsune's mobility or Shizuka's reach attractive. The best moments are miniature puzzles solved with weapons rather than keys.

Unfortunately, the game's actual puzzles often fare worse. Fixed camera angles make jumps needlessly uncertain. Navigating between battles can involve wandering through corridors that look beautiful but give poor direction. A [GameSpot review](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/genji-days-of-the-blade-review/1900-6161332/) even documented a progression-blocking problem involving a missed key. That should not be presented as every player's experience, but it illustrates how unreliable navigation could be.

There is a lesson in the contrast between the first and second games. A short action game can leave you wanting more. A longer one can make you wish its designers had been more selective. *Days of the Blade* often supplies more of what made *Genji* enjoyable without always asking whether every new room improves the experience.

## The beautiful art direction that survived the PS3 launch

For all its weaknesses, *Days of the Blade* looks unmistakably like a Game Republic production. Characters wear intricate armour and layered fabric, landscapes glow with saturated colours, and combat effects flare with almost ceremonial extravagance. Its interpretation of Japan is closer to a lavish fantasy film than a museum reconstruction.

The environmental art matters because it gives even ordinary skirmishes a setting worth occupying. There are flaming strongholds, waterlogged paths, bridges, forests and large-scale battlefields. At its best, the game seems to have been painted around the figures, giving the player the feeling of moving through one continuous historical illustration.

Many launch-window games were anxious to prove technical superiority with shiny surfaces. *Genji* used some of that power to create a more coherent artistic identity. The result could be gorgeous even when the level was forgettable.

Its music and sound presentation lend weight to that atmosphere, although the original Sixaxis's lack of vibration made physical feedback thinner than the visual drama deserved. The action keeps announcing itself through movement and colour: spinning weapons, sparks, magical attacks and enemies collapsing in wide arcs.

Those visual qualities are not substitutes for game design. The surviving critical consensus was right to say so. They are, however, an important reason the game remains in memory. Players do not remember only the balance spreadsheet. They remember what it felt like to make an extraordinary-looking thing happen.

## Why critics gave it a 55, and why someone might still love it

The [Metacritic average of 55](https://www.metacritic.com/game/genji-days-of-the-blade/) is a useful measure of its contemporary reception, not an instruction about what anybody was allowed to enjoy. *GameSpot* gave it 6.4/10, praising graphics, character variety and some boss fights while criticising shallow repetition and navigation. *GamesRadar* admired the animation and slash-heavy combat but found the camera and length exhausting. Other reviewers were harsher still.

Taken together, their complaints form a convincing account of a flawed production. It was derivative of stronger action games, uncomfortable to navigate, occasionally repetitive, and surprisingly unwilling to trust players with their own view of the battlefield.

Still, a player can love the part of an action game that is easiest to describe as 'feel'. Not all pleasures are the same as measures of balance, complexity or innovation. A spectacularly animated combo, the shift from Yoshitsune's speed to Benkei's force, or the hypnotic button rhythm of Kamui may provide exactly the kind of play someone wanted.

That is especially true of games encountered when a console is new. The first encounters with a machine have a quality that later comparisons cannot fully reproduce. A launch title's detail, scale and colours can lodge in the imagination even when later games render more and control better.

There is no need to invent a hidden masterpiece to defend that affection. If *Days of the Blade* were merely great, its weaknesses would be easier to excuse and much less interesting to examine. It is precisely the unevenness that makes the swordplay worth isolating.

## The sequel that never got to finish the argument

A third *Genji* might have been fascinating. Keep the four-person roster, give weapon changes meaningful advantages and restore a camera that players can control. Build enemy formations that respond differently to agile swords, huge clubs and ranged blade attacks. Make switching fighters necessary because the battlefield itself changes, not because the campaign temporarily locks the other characters.

Kamui could have developed into a genuine system of risk and anticipation, perhaps combining the first game's defensive counters with the sequel's offensive choreography. The art direction scarcely needed reinvention; it needed levels that made equally careful use of the space.

Game Republic did not return to *Genji* for a third instalment. The studio later worked on *Folklore*, *Majin and the Forsaken Kingdom* and other projects before reports of its closure emerged in 2011. The series remained tied to two games from a brief period when ornate Japanese action adventures, myth and bespoke combat systems were a major part of Sony's identity.

Nearly twenty years after its PS3 launch, *Days of the Blade* is mostly recalled for a giant crab. That is a memorable footnote, but a poor epitaph.

Its better legacy is more specific. The game could make the uncomplicated act of hitting something with a sword look and feel wonderful. It offered four distinct rhythms of combat, allowed players to improvise between them, then repeatedly got in their way. Some players remember the frustration. Others remember the moment before the camera moved: Yoshitsune's blades flashing, Benkei about to bring down his club, one more perfect string of Kamui attacks waiting to happen.

Both memories belong to the same game.
